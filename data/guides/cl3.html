Overview

This lecture/lab introduces the capacitor, the second energy-storing circuit element after the inductor. It covers how a capacitor is built (two conductive plates separated by a dielectric), why it stores charge when a voltage is applied, its two schematic symbols (non-polarized ceramic and polarized electrolytic), the unit farad and the practical μF/nF/pF sizes, the relationship \(C = Q/V\), how capacitors combine in parallel and in series, how voltage divides across series capacitors, and a first look at RC charging with the universal time constant chart (\(\tau = RC\)). The second half is a lab: simulating an RL circuit (10 V pulse, 10 Ω, 1 H) in LTspice to watch the inductor current rise with the \(L/R\) time constant.

Learning objectives

After this lesson you should be able to:

  • Describe the construction of a capacitor (two conductors separated by a dielectric) and explain how it stores charge and energy when a voltage is applied.
  • State the "opposition" rules: resistors oppose current, inductors oppose a change in current, capacitors oppose a change in voltage.
  • Recognise the non-polarized (ceramic) and polarized (electrolytic) capacitor symbols, and connect a polarized capacitor the right way round.
  • Explain what happens to the capacitor voltage when a switch to a DC source is closed and later opened.
  • Define the farad, convert between F, mF, μF, nF and pF, and read a small ceramic-capacitor marking given in picofarads.
  • Use \(C = Q/V\) to find charge, capacitance or voltage.
  • Combine capacitors in parallel (\(C_T = C_1 + C_2 + \dots\)) and in series (\(1/C_T = 1/C_1 + 1/C_2 + \dots\)).
  • Find the voltage across each capacitor in a series string with \(V_{Cx} = V_s\,C_T/C_x\), and check the result with KVL.
  • Compute \(\tau = RC\), and use the universal time constant chart to say how far a capacitor has charged after 1–5 time constants.
  • Build an RL circuit with a pulse source in LTspice, probe the source voltage and inductor current, and compute \(I_{max}\), \(\tau = L/R\), \(5\tau\), the stored energy and the expected current at each time constant.

Prerequisites

Charge, voltage and current
Charge \(Q\) is measured in coulombs (1 C is about \(6.24\times10^{18}\) electrons). Current is the rate at which charge moves (1 A = 1 C/s). Voltage is the energy per unit charge between two points.
Ohm's law and KVL
\(V = IR\). Kirchhoff's voltage law: the voltages around any closed loop add to zero, so in a series loop the element voltages add up to the source voltage. Elements in parallel share the same voltage.
Series/parallel resistor formulas
Series: \(R_T = R_1 + R_2 + \dots\). Parallel: \(1/R_T = 1/R_1 + 1/R_2 + \dots\), or for two, \(R_1R_2/(R_1+R_2)\). Capacitors use the same two formulas but swapped.
Inductors and the L/R time constant
An inductor stores energy in a magnetic field and opposes a change in current; after a DC step its current rises exponentially with \(\tau = L/R\) and reaches (practically) its final value after \(5\tau\). See Intro to inductors and the L/R time constant.
Universal time constant chart
The chart (introduced for inductors) gives the percentage of the final value reached after each time constant: about 63.2 % after \(1\tau\), 86.5 % after \(2\tau\), 95 % after \(3\tau\), 98 % after \(4\tau\) and 99 % (treated as 100 %) after \(5\tau\).
Metric prefixes
m = \(10^{-3}\), μ = \(10^{-6}\), n = \(10^{-9}\), p = \(10^{-12}\).

1. What a capacitor is and what it does

Context

The previous two lectures treated inductors with a DC source and a switch (so the current changed only when the switch moved). This lecture looks at capacitors from the same DC point of view. Later in the course both inductors and capacitors are revisited with AC (sine-wave) sources.

Construction

A capacitor is formed by two conductors separated by an insulator, called the dielectric. Each terminal (wire) connects to one conductive plate, so the capacitor is a two-terminal device . The plates are not electrically connected to each other.

Capacitors store charge

First statement on the page: capacitors store electrical charge when a voltage is applied. When a voltage (a battery in the sketch) is applied, current \(I\) flows : positive charge builds up on the plate connected to the battery's + terminal and negative charge on the other plate. One plate ends up with an excess of electrons (negative) and the other with a lack of electrons (positive) .

  • An inductor stores energy in a magnetic field; a capacitor stores energy as charge built up on its plates .
  • Once charged, the capacitor acts like a battery: it holds energy and that charge can be used to do work .

Physically, the separated charge creates an electric field in the dielectric, and that is where the energy lives — just as the inductor's energy lives in its magnetic field. The energy stored is \(W = \tfrac12 C V^2\) (compare \(W = \tfrac12 L I^2\) for an inductor). This formula is not derived in this video.

Capacitors oppose a change in voltage

Second statement on the page: capacitors oppose a change in voltage .

ElementOpposes…
Resistorthe flow of current
Inductora change in current
Capacitora change in voltage

The reason : when a voltage is applied, it takes time for charge to build up on the plates, so the capacitor's voltage cannot jump instantly; it builds up over time.

The instructor repeats this trio several times (again in the lab at ): resistance opposes current, inductance opposes a change in current, capacitance opposes a change in voltage.

2. Schematic symbols: ceramic vs electrolytic

There are two schematic symbols for a capacitor because there are two fundamentally different ways capacitors are constructed . Electrically, both are "a capacitor".

Non-polarized symbol (ceramic)

Two straight, parallel plates with a gap: this is the "pure"/ideal capacitor symbol. The gap reminds you that the plates are separated by the insulator and not electrically connected. Generally this symbol means a ceramic capacitor . Ceramic capacitors are small, usually flat discs (sometimes coloured) , have low capacitance values, and have no polarity: you can plug them in either way round .

Polarized symbol (electrolytic)

One plate is drawn curved and the symbol carries a polarity. This is an electrolytic capacitor. The polarity is "an artifact of construction" :

  • The flat side (+) must be at the higher voltage and the curved side (−) at the lower voltage .
  • If it is connected backwards it still works for a while, but it eventually breaks down — "probably go up in a puff of smoke" .
  • Capacitors in general don't need polarity; this one does because of the physical limitations of how it is made .

Construction : two foil strips are separated by a dielectric (think of a piece of paper), rolled up, and put into a can. Electrolytics store much more charge than ceramics; a physically larger can has larger plates, so it can hold more charge/energy .

Capacitance and size

Capacitance is how much charge a capacitor can store (written on the page). Ceramics are low in value; electrolytics can be much larger. The instructor lays real parts on the page — two small ceramic discs, a small blue electrolytic and a larger black electrolytic — and shows a fist-sized can capacitor used for filtering large voltage changes in industrial power applications (motors etc.) ; typical electronic circuits don't use parts that big.

When does polarity matter?

On paper and in circuit-simulation tools the two symbols are treated the same: an ideal capacitor . On the page, arrows from both symbols point to a single plain capacitor symbol to show this (visible at , next to the note "Capacitance — how much charge it can store"). Polarity only matters when you physically build a circuit and choose an electrolytic part for the value you need; then you must connect it with the correct orientation . There are other trade-offs between the two types, but they are not needed at this point.

On a real electrolytic capacitor the negative lead is normally marked with a stripe of "−" signs on the can, and the negative lead is the shorter one when the part is new. Match that stripe to the curved plate in the schematic.

3. Charging a capacitor through a switch — a temporary battery

The instructor draws a simple circuit: a 10 V source, a switch and a capacitor .

  1. Switch open : no charge has built up on the plates, so \(v_C = 0\).
  2. Switch closed : current flows and charge starts to develop on the plates. At first \(v_C\) is less than 10 V.
  3. After some time : the capacitor reaches 10 V. At that point it holds as much charge as it can for this voltage — the plates are only so large, and the electrons can't be "squished" any further . The capacitor is fully charged with \(v_C = 10\,\text{V}\). (How long this takes depends on component values — that is the time constant, section 8.)
  4. Switch opened again : the charge stays on the plates because it has nowhere to go, so \(v_C\) is still 10 V . In reality it slowly dissipates over time, but we normally ignore that and treat the capacitor like a small battery that can give us 10 V .

So a capacitor is a temporary storage of charge — like a battery that can charge and discharge quickly . This makes it useful (a) when you want things to take some amount of time, because the charge does not build up instantly, and (b) as an energy-storage device that acts as a temporary battery.

Two facts used constantly in later lessons follow from this:

  • The capacitor voltage cannot change instantly (it takes time for charge to build up), so the voltage just after a switch moves equals the voltage just before it.
  • At DC steady state a capacitor acts as an open circuit: once \(v_C\) equals the source voltage, no more charge flows, so the current is zero. Compare the inductor, which acts as a short (a plain wire) at DC steady state — exactly what the lab in this video shows at .

4. The farad and practical capacitor values

To quantify how much charge a capacitor stores — quite literally, how many electrons pile up on a plate — we use the unit of capacitance, the farad (F), named after Michael Faraday .

\[ 1\,\text{F} = \frac{1\,\text{C}}{1\,\text{V}} \]
F
farad, unit of capacitance
C
coulomb, unit of charge
V
volt, unit of potential difference

Written on the page : 1 F is the amount of capacitance where a charge of 1 coulomb develops a potential difference of 1 V across the cap. ("Cap" is simply short for capacitor .)

Example 1 — The definition as a circuit

1. A 1 V source is connected across a capacitor; the capacitor charges to 1 V.

2. Suppose the charge that builds up is \(Q_C = 1\) coulomb (remember, a coulomb is a certain number of electrons) .

3. Then, by the definition, \(C = \dfrac{1\,\text{C}}{1\,\text{V}} = 1\,\text{F}\).

\(C = 1\,\text{F}\)

A farad is a lot

A picofarad is a very small capacitance — but look how physically small the 10 pF ceramic is compared with a big electrolytic . The farad is the base unit, but you rarely meet a whole farad of capacitance in ordinary circuits . (The prefixes are spoken, not written on the page.)

At the instructor first says "pico is 10 to the minus 9", then immediately corrects himself: pico is \(10^{-12}\); \(10^{-9}\) is nano.

Voltage rating

Two capacitors can have the same capacitance but different voltage ratings: the two electrolytics shown are both 100 μF, but the black one can safely handle 350 V while the small blue one can only handle 25 V. The higher-voltage part is physically larger.

Exceeding the voltage rating can break down the dielectric (the insulator conducts), which permanently damages the capacitor — electrolytics can vent or burst. A common design rule is to choose a rating comfortably above the highest voltage the capacitor will see. (The video only states the ratings; this paragraph is general background.)

5. Capacitance, charge and voltage: \(C = Q/V\)

The relationship between capacitance, charge and voltage :

\[ C = \frac{Q}{V} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad Q = CV \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad V = \frac{Q}{C} \]
\(C\)
capacitance, farads (F)
\(Q\)
charge stored, coulombs (C)
\(V\)
voltage across the capacitor, volts (V)

This holds for an ideal capacitor at any instant; in particular, once a capacitor is fully charged to the source voltage, \(V\) is that source voltage.

Example 2 — Charge on a 10 F capacitor at 12 V

Given: \(V = 12\,\text{V}\) applied to a (rather large) \(C = 10\,\text{F}\) capacitor. Find: the stored charge \(Q\).

1. Start from \(C = Q/V\) .

2. Substitute: \(10\,\text{F} = \dfrac{Q}{12\,\text{V}}\) .

3. Solve: \(Q = (10\,\text{F})(12\,\text{V}) = 120\,\text{C}\) (F·V = (C/V)·V = C).

\(Q = 120\,\text{C}\) — "which is a lot of charge" .

6. Capacitors in parallel and in series

The capacitor combination rules are the opposite of the resistor rules .

Parallel: capacitances add

\[ C_T = C_1 + C_2 + \dots + C_n \qquad \text{(parallel)} \]
\(C_T\)
total (equivalent) capacitance, F
\(C_1 \ldots C_n\)
individual capacitances in parallel, F

Why: parallel capacitors all have the same voltage across them, so together they behave like one capacitor with physically larger plates — more plate area, more charge for the same voltage.

Example 3 — 5 μF in parallel with 10 μF

1. Capacitors in parallel add: \(C_T = C_1 + C_2\).

2. \(C_T = 5\,\mu\text{F} + 10\,\mu\text{F}\) .

\(C_T = 15\,\mu\text{F}\)

Series: like resistors in parallel

\[ \frac{1}{C_T} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{C_n} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C_T = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{C_n}} \qquad \text{(series)} \]

For exactly two capacitors in series :

\[ C_T = \frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1 + C_2} \]

Two capacitors in series give less capacitance than either one alone.

Why series capacitance drops: in series, the same charge \(Q\) sits on every capacitor (the inner plates are only connected to each other, so whatever charge leaves one arrives on the next). The voltages add: \(V = Q/C_1 + Q/C_2\), so \(V/Q = 1/C_1 + 1/C_2 = 1/C_T\). Physically it is like one capacitor with a thicker dielectric (plates further apart).

Example 4 — Two 2 μF capacitors in series

1. Two in series: \(C_T = \dfrac{C_1C_2}{C_1+C_2}\).

2. \(C_T = \dfrac{(2\,\mu\text{F})(2\,\mu\text{F})}{2\,\mu\text{F} + 2\,\mu\text{F}} = \dfrac{4\,\mu\text{F}^2}{4\,\mu\text{F}}\) .

\(C_T = 1\,\mu\text{F}\)

Shortcut: \(n\) equal capacitors \(C\) in series give \(C/n\) — here \(2\,\mu\text{F}/2 = 1\,\mu\text{F}\).

7. Voltage distribution across capacitors

Parallel capacitors share the same voltage

The rules from earlier in the semester still hold: things in parallel have the same voltage across them. With a 10 V source across the parallel pair, each capacitor has 10 V across it once fully charged , because both capacitors are in parallel with the source.

Series capacitors: voltage is inversely proportional to capacitance

In series, the voltage across any one capacitor is inversely proportional to its capacitance — the smaller capacitor gets the larger share of the voltage. For capacitor \(x\) (any of \(C_1, C_2, C_3, \dots\)) :

\[ V_{Cx} = V_s\,\frac{C_T}{C_x} \]
\(V_{Cx}\)
voltage across capacitor \(x\), V
\(V_s\)
total voltage applied to the series string, V
\(C_T\)
total series capacitance, F
\(C_x\)
capacitance of the capacitor of interest, F

Applies to capacitors in series (starting uncharged) once they have charged from the applied voltage \(V_s\).

Where it comes from: all series capacitors carry the same charge \(Q = C_T V_s\). Then \(V_{Cx} = Q/C_x = V_s C_T / C_x\). This also explains the inverse proportionality — same \(Q\), so a smaller \(C\) needs a bigger \(V\). Compare the resistor voltage divider \(V_{Rx} = V_s R_x/R_T\), where the voltage is directly proportional to resistance.

Example 5 — 90 V across 3 μF and 6 μF in series

Given: \(V_s = 90\,\text{V}\), \(C_1 = 3\,\mu\text{F}\), \(C_2 = 6\,\mu\text{F}\) in series . Find: \(V_{C1}\) and \(V_{C2}\).

1. Total capacitance first : \(C_T = \dfrac{(3\,\mu\text{F})(6\,\mu\text{F})}{3\,\mu\text{F}+6\,\mu\text{F}} = \dfrac{18}{9}\,\mu\text{F} = 2\,\mu\text{F}\) .

2. Voltage across \(C_1\) : \(V_{C1} = 90\,\text{V}\cdot\dfrac{2\,\mu\text{F}}{3\,\mu\text{F}} = 60\,\text{V}\) .

3. KVL predicts the rest: \(90\,\text{V} - 60\,\text{V} = 30\,\text{V}\) .

4. Check with the formula: \(V_{C2} = 90\,\text{V}\cdot\dfrac{2\,\mu\text{F}}{6\,\mu\text{F}} = 30\,\text{V}\) — it agrees.

\(V_{C1} = 60\,\text{V}\) (across the smaller 3 μF), \(V_{C2} = 30\,\text{V}\) (across the larger 6 μF); \(60 + 30 = 90\,\text{V}\).

Charge check: \(Q = C_T V_s = (2\,\mu\text{F})(90\,\text{V}) = 180\,\mu\text{C}\) on each capacitor; \(180\,\mu\text{C}/3\,\mu\text{F} = 60\,\text{V}\) and \(180\,\mu\text{C}/6\,\mu\text{F} = 30\,\text{V}\).

Where do the \(C_x\) values come from? Reading markings

In the example they were simply given. On real parts, the capacitance is printed on the capacitor: an electrolytic prints its value in μF; a small ceramic is printed with a number that is in picofarads by default, because there is no room to print units . The ceramic shown is marked "10", meaning 10 pF.

Larger ceramic values use a three-digit code: two significant digits followed by a multiplier (number of zeros), still in pF. For example "104" = \(10\times10^4\,\text{pF} = 100\,000\,\text{pF} = 100\,\text{nF} = 0.1\,\mu\text{F}\), and "103" = 10 nF. This code is not covered in the video.

Summary : series capacitors need the extra step of finding \(C_T\) and then multiplying the applied voltage by the ratio \(C_T/C_x\); parallel capacitors are easy — they all have the same voltage.

8. Charging through a resistor: \(\tau = RC\) and the universal time constant chart

( The instructor briefly checks the clock before deciding to start this topic.)

Putting a resistor in series with the capacitor limits the current that can flow to it , so the charge — and therefore the capacitor voltage — does not develop instantaneously; it takes time. The same universal time constant chart used for inductors describes how long it takes for the capacitor voltage to reach the applied voltage .

The chart

  • It takes five time constants to reach the maximum (100 %) value , just like the inductor.
  • After one time constant the capacitor voltage is at 63.2 % of its maximum ; after two, 86.5 % ; then 3, 4 and 5 time constants follow the exponential curve.
  • Here the "max value" is the voltage across the capacitor, which ends at the source voltage .
\[ \tau = RC \]
\(\tau\)
time constant, seconds (Ω·F = s)
\(R\)
series resistance, Ω
\(C\)
capacitance, F

Applies to a capacitor charging (or discharging) through a resistance \(R\) from a DC source . The larger the capacitance — the more charge it can store — the longer it takes to reach the maximum, but it always takes \(5\tau = 5RC\) .

The instructor says "62 percent" once at ; the value written on the chart and stated a moment earlier is 63.2 %.

Example 6 — 10 V, 100 Ω, 1 F

Given: \(V_s = 10\,\text{V}\), \(R = 100\,\Omega\), \(C = 1\,\text{F}\) ; capacitor uncharged before the switch closes. Find: the time constant and how long the capacitor takes to reach 10 V.

1. Before the switch is thrown there is no charge, so \(v_C = 0\,\text{V}\) (0 % of max) . After closing, \(v_C\) rises toward the 10 V maximum.

2. \(\tau = RC = (100\,\Omega)(1\,\text{F}) = 100\,\text{s}\) — the answer is always in seconds.

3. Time constants fall at 100 s, 200 s, 300 s, 400 s and 500 s .

4. Full charge after \(5\tau = 500\,\text{s}\) .

\(\tau = 100\,\text{s}\); the capacitor goes from 0 V at \(t = 0\) to 10 V after 500 s.

The time-constant chart and RC charging are treated in more detail (with a live oscilloscope demonstration) next class : see RC time constant.

The chart's curve is \(v_C(t) = V_s\left(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\right)\) for an initially uncharged capacitor. At he shows an old-school variable parallel-plate capacitor: literally the basic construction of parallel plates, but with many plates, and adjustable . It was used to tune the frequency of radios: turning the shaft changes how the plates sit relative to each other and therefore changes the capacitance . Radios of the 1960s–70s worked this way; later, diodes were used to tune the frequency electronically instead of mechanically changing the capacitance .

This illustrates the factors that set capacitance: \(C = \varepsilon A/d\) for parallel plates — more overlapping plate area \(A\) or a smaller gap \(d\) gives more capacitance, and the dielectric material sets \(\varepsilon\). In a tuning capacitor, rotating the shaft changes the overlapping area. The diodes he mentions are varactor ("varicap") diodes, whose capacitance changes with the reverse voltage applied. Why set the series resistance to 0?

A real inductor is just a coil of wire : the instructor shows a plain coil, a toroid (the doughnut shape constrains the magnetic flux lines), and a spool-like inductor . 11. Lab 7: setting up the pulse source and simulation

Why a pulse?

If the source is just a constant 10 V, the inductor behaves like a wire: with a 1 s stop time the simulation shows a steady 1 A through it "all day long" (\(10\,\text{V}/10\,\Omega\)), because nothing is changing. To see the inductor oppose a change in current, the current must change — so the source is made to act like a switch: 0 V at first, then 10 V after one second .

Inductor current

For a plain resistor the current would jump with the voltage. With the inductor, self-inductance opposes the change: as the current increases, the magnetic field increases and induces a voltage (back emf) that opposes the current . Clicking on the inductor (probe \(I(L1)\)) shows the current not changing instantly; it follows the universal time constant chart and takes five \(L/R\) time constants to reach its maximum . Hover the cursor over the trace to read values as the current increases .

Induced voltage (back-EMF)

As current begins to flow, it creates the magnetic field, which induces the voltage — the back-EMF — that opposes the current. The lab sheet asks you to sketch this induced voltage on a printout of the waveform.

Why it looks like this: KVL around the loop gives \(v_L = V_{source} - iR\). At the instant the source jumps to 10 V the current is still 0 (it cannot change instantly), so all 10 V appears across the inductor; as \(i \to 1\,\text{A}\), \(iR \to 10\,\text{V}\) and \(v_L \to 0\). At 2 s the source drops to 0 V while the current is still 1 A, so \(v_L = 0 - (1\,\text{A})(10\,\Omega) = -10\,\text{V}\), which then decays as the current dies out.

Assumptions & limitations

  • Ideal capacitor. The plates are perfectly insulated from each other, so a charged, disconnected capacitor holds its voltage. Real capacitors slowly leak (self-discharge) — the instructor notes the charge "will slowly dissipate over time" but we ignore it .
  • Polarity is ignored in analysis and simulation; it only matters for real electrolytic parts .
  • \(C = Q/V\) assumes a linear capacitor (capacitance doesn't depend on voltage) — true for the ideal element used in this course.
  • Combination rules apply only to capacitors that are truly in series (one path, nothing branching off between them) or truly in parallel (same two nodes).
  • \(V_{Cx} = V_s C_T/C_x\) assumes the series capacitors started uncharged and have finished charging (all carry the same charge). It does not apply if a capacitor had an initial charge.
  • "Fully charged after \(5\tau\)" is an engineering approximation (99.3 %). The time-constant chart applies to a single capacitor (or a single equivalent \(C\)) charging through a single equivalent resistance from a DC step.
  • Lab: the inductor's series resistance is set to 0 to make it ideal; with a real coil's resistance \(R_{coil}\), the current would settle at \(V/(R + R_{coil})\) and \(\tau = L/(R + R_{coil})\). The 1 ns edge is treated as an instantaneous step.
  • All of this is DC/switching behaviour; AC (sine-wave) behaviour of capacitors and inductors comes later in the course.

Common mistakes

  • Using the resistor rules for capacitors. Capacitors in parallel add; in series use the reciprocal (product-over-sum) rule. Sanity check: series \(C_T\) must be smaller than the smallest capacitor; parallel \(C_T\) larger than the largest.
  • Giving the bigger capacitor the bigger voltage. In series, voltage is inversely proportional to capacitance: in Example 5 the 3 μF gets 60 V and the 6 μF gets 30 V. Always check that the voltages add to the source voltage (KVL).
  • Forgetting to find \(C_T\) first in the series voltage formula.
  • Prefix slips: μ = \(10^{-6}\), n = \(10^{-9}\), p = \(10^{-12}\) (even the instructor briefly said pico = \(10^{-9}\) before correcting it). Convert to farads before computing \(\tau = RC\) or \(Q = CV\).
  • Reading a ceramic marking as μF. A bare number on a small ceramic ("10") is in pF.
  • Reversing an electrolytic. The flat (+) side goes to the higher voltage, the curved (−) side to the lower voltage; reversed, it fails (possibly violently).
  • Ignoring the voltage rating. Same μF value does not mean interchangeable: a 25 V part cannot replace a 350 V part.
  • Thinking a disconnected capacitor is "empty". After the switch opens, a charged capacitor keeps its voltage (10 V in the video) — it can still deliver energy, and in real high-voltage equipment, a shock.
  • Mixing up the two time constants: RC circuit \(\tau = RC\); RL circuit \(\tau = L/R\) (not \(LR\)).
  • Lab: measuring time constants from \(t = 0\). The pulse starts at 1 s, so the time constants fall at 1.1 s, 1.2 s, … 1.5 s.
  • Lab: a flat-line source trace means the source was not changed to PULSE (Advanced tab). Also remember the ground symbol, and stop time = 3 s.
  • Lab: leaving the default series resistance — tiny here (1 mΩ vs 10 Ω), but set it to 0 as instructed.

Recap & formula sheet

A capacitor is two plates separated by a dielectric. Applying a voltage moves charge onto the plates (+ on one, − on the other); the capacitor then holds that charge like a temporary battery, and it opposes changes in its voltage. Capacitance (farads; practically μF, nF, pF) measures charge stored per volt. Ceramic capacitors are small and non-polarized; electrolytics are larger and polarized. Parallel capacitances add; series capacitances combine like parallel resistors, and the voltage across each series capacitor is inversely proportional to its capacitance. Through a resistor, a capacitor charges with \(\tau = RC\), taking about \(5\tau\) to fully charge. The lab showed the same exponential behaviour for current in an RL circuit with \(\tau = L/R\).
