Overview

This lecture (recorded 24 Sep 2020) introduces Thévenin's theorem: any linear two-terminal network of resistances and sources can be replaced, as seen from two terminals A and B, by one voltage source \(V_{TH}\) in series with one resistance \(R_{TH}\). The instructor defines \(V_{TH}\) as the open-terminal voltage across A–B and \(R_{TH}\) as the resistance seen looking back into A–B with the voltage sources shorted. He practises each on small circuits, then "Thévenizes" a 100 V ladder circuit (\(V_{TH} = 50\,\text{V}\), \(R_{TH} = 25\,\Omega\)). A student question then clears up a very common confusion: when does a short make a resistor "disappear"?

Learning objectives

After this lesson you should be able to:

  1. State Thévenin's theorem and explain what "equivalent" means: anything connected between A and B gets the same voltage and current from the equivalent as from the original circuit.
  2. Pick out the two load terminals A and B, and explain why moving them gives a different Thévenin equivalent.
  3. Find \(V_{TH}\) as the open-circuit voltage \(V_{AB}\) using total resistance, Ohm's law, KVL and node voltages, and state its polarity (A is the "+" terminal).
  4. Find \(R_{TH}\) by redrawing the circuit with every independent voltage source shorted and reducing the resistance seen from A–B.
  5. Tell whether a short eliminates a resistor (short in parallel with it) or only joins resistors in series, by tracing where current divides and sums.
  6. Draw the finished Thévenin equivalent with values and polarity.
  7. (Extra) Use the equivalent to get load current and voltage for any \(R_L\); open current sources when finding \(R_{TH}\); use \(V_{OC}/I_{SC}\) or a test source when the circuit has dependent sources.

Prerequisites

Ohm's law
\(V = IR\). Used to get the total current and each resistor's voltage drop.
Series and parallel resistance
Series: same current, \(R_{eq} = R_1 + R_2 + \dots\). Parallel: same two nodes, \(R_{eq} = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\) for two resistors. The instructor's test: imagine current flowing from A to B. Where it divides (dividing node) and where it sums back together (summing node) mark the two ends of a parallel group. If the current doesn't divide between two resistors, they are in series.
Voltage divider
Two series resistors across a source share its voltage in proportion to resistance: \(V_2 = V_s\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\).
KVL and node voltages
The voltage drops around a loop add to zero. In practice: start at a node with a known voltage and subtract each drop to get the next node's voltage. The voltage across an element is the difference of its two node voltages.
Turning off a voltage source
As in superposition, an independent voltage source is "turned off" by replacing it with a short (a wire, 0 V). The instructor refers back to this at .
Resistance between two points
The single resistance an ohmmeter would read between two terminals. You measured this in an earlier lab (mentioned at ).

1. Who was Thévenin?

The instructor opens with some history. Léon Charles Thévenin was a French telegraph worker. In his day, the most advanced use of electricity was tapping two wires together to send "dits and dashes" over long distances. He was interested in Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's laws. The page at lists the dates:

PersonDates (as written on the page)Known for
Léon Charles Thévenin1857–1926Thévenin's theorem (this lecture)
George (Georg) Ohm1789–1854Ohm's law
Gustav Kirchhoff1824–1887KVL and KCL

His point: all the circuit laws used in this course are fairly recent. From telegraph wires to smartphones took only about 130 years .

At the instructor says "1856 to 1926", but the page says 1857–1926. Thévenin was born in 1857, so the page is right. This is history only and won't affect any calculation.

2. Thévenin's theorem

The statement as written on the page ():

\[ \text{Any linear two-terminal network of resistances and sources} \;\Longrightarrow\; V_{TH} \text{ in series with } R_{TH} \]
Any linear two-terminal network of resistances & sources can be simplified by an equivalent circuit consisting of a single voltage source (\(V_{TH}\)) & a single series resistance (\(R_{TH}\)).
  • Two-terminal : there are two connection points. Every circuit you have solved so far can be turned into one: put a terminal "A" on any point and a terminal "B" on any other point .
  • Linear: everything in this course so far (resistors and sources) is linear .
  • Equivalent : as seen from A and B, the equivalent behaves exactly like the original. If you plug a resistor between A and B on the original circuit, it gets some voltage and current. Plug the same resistor into the Thévenin equivalent and it gets the same voltage and current .
  • Big reduction : the box could hold "a hundred voltage sources and two resistors" or "a zillion resistors and five voltage sources". It still reduces to one source and one resistor.

Vocabulary. The result of the process is the Thévenin equivalent circuit (written in red). The process itself is called Thévenizing the circuit (written in blue at ). The result is always one voltage source and one resistor, brought out to the two terminals.

Possible verbal slip. At the instructor says the result is "one voltage source with one resistor in parallel". The theorem he wrote, and every equivalent he draws, has the resistor in series with the source. Series is correct for Thévenin. (A source with a resistor in parallel is the Norton form, which comes next in Thévenin cont. & Norton.)
The equivalence holds only "from the perspective of these two terminal points" . The two circuits match only for what you connect between A and B.
Why bother? Suppose you want to try many different loads between A and B: ten resistor values, a lamp, a meter. Without Thévenin you would re-solve the whole network for every load. With the equivalent you solve the network once for \(V_{TH}\) and \(R_{TH}\). After that every load is a one-line series-circuit calculation, \(I_L = V_{TH}/(R_{TH}+R_L)\). See Example 7 for a worked case. This also models real hardware: a battery, a function generator or a sensor output behaves like an ideal source with a series "internal" resistance.

3. Finding \(V_{TH}\): the open-terminal voltage

Look at the equivalent circuit: it contains only two values. So Thévenizing a circuit comes down to finding two numbers, \(V_{TH}\) and \(R_{TH}\), and plugging them into the standard picture . There is a separate procedure for each.

Definition, as written at :

\(V_{TH}\): is the open-terminal voltage "felt" across the load terminals (A & B).
\[ V_{TH} = V_{AB}\Big|_{\text{nothing connected to A–B}} = V_A - V_B \]
\(V_{TH}\)
Thévenin voltage, V
\(V_A, V_B\)
voltages of terminals A and B with respect to a common reference, V

This applies to any linear circuit. Leave A–B open (no load), solve the circuit with your usual tools, and report the voltage between A and B. "That's all it is" .

You must have two terminals first. A circuit with no A and B marked cannot be Thévenized: you have to decide which two points the equivalent is "seen" from. They are called load terminals because a load will be attached there eventually . While you derive the equivalent, you do not connect anything to them.

Procedure for \(V_{TH}\):

  1. Mark A and B and remove any load (leave the terminals open).
  2. Solve the circuit normally (total resistance, total current, Ohm's law, KVL/node voltages, divider).
  3. \(V_{TH}\) = the voltage between A and B, with A taken as "+".

Example 1: Source directly across the terminals

Given: 10 V source; 10 Ω connected straight across it; A on the top node, B on the bottom node. Goal: \(V_{TH}\).

1. A and B sit on the same two nodes as the source, so \(V_{AB}\) equals the source voltage.

\(V_{TH} = 10\,\text{V}\) (A positive).

Example 2: Voltage divider, terminals across the 100 Ω

Given: 5 V source, 100 Ω and 400 Ω in series; A at the top of the 100 Ω, B at the junction between the two resistors. Goal: \(V_{TH}\).

1. Total resistance (series): \(R_T = 100 + 400 = 500\,\Omega\).

2. Total current (Ohm's law) : \(I = \dfrac{5\,\text{V}}{500\,\Omega} = 0.010\,\text{A} = 10\,\text{mA}\).

3. Drop across each resistor (Ohm's law) : \(V_{100} = 0.010 \times 100 = 1\,\text{V}\) and \(V_{400} = 0.010 \times 400 = 4\,\text{V}\). Check: \(1 + 4 = 5\,\text{V}\) ✓ (KVL).

4. A and B sit across the 100 Ω, so \(V_{AB} = V_{100}\). The top node is the higher one, so A is "+".

\(V_{TH} = 1\,\text{V}\)

Example 3: Same circuit, terminals moved across the 400 Ω

1. Nothing in the circuit changed, so the current is still 10 mA and the drops are still 1 V and 4 V.

2. Now A–B spans the 400 Ω, so \(V_{AB} = V_{400}\), with the junction (A) above the bottom node (B).

\(V_{TH} = 4\,\text{V}\)

Same circuit, different answer . The Thévenin equivalent "very much depends on where A and B is". Always check which two points you are Thévenizing from.

A third circuit (a source feeding a series resistor, a shunt resistor and another series resistor, with no values) was drawn on the page but skipped. The instructor said a similar problem would come once \(R_{TH}\) was covered. That is the ladder problem in section 5.

Polarity. \(V_{TH}\) is defined as \(V_A - V_B\). If you swap the labels A and B, \(V_{TH}\) changes sign. In the drawn equivalent, that means the source is flipped (+ toward the new A). In every example in this video, A is the higher-voltage terminal, so the "+" of \(V_{TH}\) faces A.

4. Finding \(R_{TH}\): look back with the sources shorted

Definition, as written at :

\(R_{TH}\): the resistance "seen" when looking back into the circuit from the two terminals with voltage sources shorted.
\[ R_{TH} = R_{AB}\Big|_{\text{all independent voltage sources replaced by shorts}} \]
\(R_{TH}\)
Thévenin resistance, Ω
\(R_{AB}\)
equivalent resistance between terminals A and B (what an ohmmeter would read), Ω

This applies to circuits whose sources are independent voltage sources, which is every circuit in this video. The extras below cover current sources and dependent sources. Shorting a voltage source is the same "disable" step you used in superposition : connect one side of the source straight to the other.

Procedure for \(R_{TH}\):

  1. Redraw the circuit as a new problem. Don't reuse the \(V_{TH}\) drawing or any of its voltages and currents .
  2. Replace each voltage source by a short (wire).
  3. Find the resistance between A and B with series/parallel reduction. If in doubt, imagine current entering at A and leaving at B .

Example 4: \(R_{TH}\) of the Example 2 circuit (A–B across the 100 Ω)

1. Redraw with the 5 V source replaced by a wire . The 100 Ω and 400 Ω stay where they were. A is at the top, B at the junction.

2. Series or parallel? Imagine current entering at A . At the top node it divides. Part goes down through the 100 Ω straight to B. The rest goes around through the short and up through the 400 Ω to B. It sums back together at B. Dividing node = A, summing node = B, so the 100 Ω and 400 Ω are in parallel.

3. Product over sum : \(R_{TH} = \dfrac{400 \times 100}{400 + 100} = \dfrac{40\,000}{500} = 80\,\Omega\).

\(R_{TH} = 80\,\Omega\)

Example 5: \(R_{TH}\) of the Example 1 circuit (10 V across 10 Ω)

1. Short the 10 V source. The wire now connects the top and bottom nodes, the same two nodes as the 10 Ω.

2. The short is in parallel with the 10 Ω, and \(0 \parallel 10 = 0\). Current takes the wire and the resistor effectively disappears. A and B are joined by a wire.

\(R_{TH} = 0\,\Omega\)

That is why he did this one second : shorting the source also shorts out the resistor, which is less intuitive as a first example.
Putting the small examples together. These equivalents were not drawn in the video, but they follow directly from its numbers:
  • Example 1 → \(V_{TH} = 10\,\text{V}\), \(R_{TH} = 0\,\Omega\): the equivalent is just the ideal 10 V source. Any load gets exactly 10 V.
  • Example 2 → \(V_{TH} = 1\,\text{V}\) in series with \(R_{TH} = 80\,\Omega\).
  • Example 3 → \(V_{TH} = 4\,\text{V}\). Its \(R_{TH}\) was not computed in the video. Shorting the source again leaves the 100 Ω and 400 Ω both directly between A and B, so \(R_{TH} = 80\,\Omega\) here too. Only \(V_{TH}\) changed. In general both numbers can change when you move the terminals.
Current sources: open them (not in this video). The instructor's definition mentions only voltage sources because every circuit in this lecture has only voltage sources. The general rule is to turn off every independent source. A voltage source turned off is 0 V, which is a short. A current source turned off is 0 A, which is an open circuit (remove it and leave a gap). Shorting a current source is a classic mistake. Thévenin cont. & Norton continues from here.
An alternative: \(R_{TH} = V_{OC}/I_{SC}\) (not mentioned in this video). Put a wire from A to B and find the short-circuit current \(I_{SC}\) flowing from A to B through it. Then \(R_{TH} = V_{OC}/I_{SC} = V_{TH}/I_{SC}\). This works because shorting the equivalent circuit gives \(I_{SC} = V_{TH}/R_{TH}\). Check on Example 2: a wire across the 100 Ω leaves only the 400 Ω, so \(I_{SC} = 5/400 = 12.5\,\text{mA}\) and \(R_{TH} = 1\,\text{V}/12.5\,\text{mA} = 80\,\Omega\) ✓. It fails for Example 1: a wire across an ideal source draws unlimited current (that's the \(R_{TH}=0\) case).

5. Full example: Thévenizing a 100 V ladder circuit

Example 6: Find the Thévenin equivalent at A–B

Given: 100 V source (+ on top); 25 Ω in the top line (\(R_1\)) and 25 Ω in the bottom line (\(R_2\)); two 100 Ω resistors (\(R_3\), \(R_4\)) from the top rail to the bottom rail; A at the top rail and B at the bottom rail (across \(R_4\)). Goal : Thévenize, i.e. find \(V_{TH}\) and \(R_{TH}\) and draw the equivalent. The result must behave identically at A–B .

Part 1: Find \(V_{TH}\)

The instructor redraws the circuit under the heading "1) Finding \(V_{TH}\)" to keep the original page clean. Nothing is attached to A–B.

1. Total resistance . With A–B open, \(R_3\) and \(R_4\) share the same two nodes: \(100 \parallel 100 = 50\,\Omega\). That 50 Ω is in series with both 25 Ω resistors: \(R_T = 25 + 50 + 25 = 100\,\Omega\).

2. Total current (Ohm's law) : \(I_T = \dfrac{100\,\text{V}}{100\,\Omega} = 1\,\text{A}\).

3. Back-fill . The 1 A leaving the source flows through the top 25 Ω and must come back through the bottom 25 Ω. Drop across each: \(1\,\text{A} \times 25\,\Omega = 25\,\text{V}\) .

4. Node voltages via KVL, taking the source's "−" side as 0 V . Top: start at 100 V, lose 25 V across \(R_1\), so the whole top rail is at \(100 - 25 = 75\,\text{V}\). Bottom: the rail ends at 0 V after dropping 25 V across \(R_2\), so the bottom rail is at \(0 + 25 = 25\,\text{V}\).

5. Voltage across the 100 Ω resistors : \(75 - 25 = 50\,\text{V}\) across \(R_3\). \(R_4\) is on the same two rails (in parallel), so it also has 50 V .

6. Read off \(V_{AB}\) . A is wired to the 75 V rail and B to the 25 V rail: \(V_{TH} = V_A - V_B = 75 - 25 = 50\,\text{V}\), A positive. This makes sense: A–B is in parallel with both 100 Ω resistors, which each have 50 V.

\(V_{TH} = 50\,\text{V}\) (written into the equivalent at )

Why is \(R_4\) included in the total resistance even though A and B are "the output"? With the terminals open, \(R_4\) is just another resistor in the circuit. It carries current and sets the voltage. What has to stay open is the load position, not the resistor that happens to sit across A–B.

Part 2: Find \(R_{TH}\)

This is where the instructor sees the most mistakes . Students try to find \(V_{TH}\) and \(R_{TH}\) on the same drawing and get mixed up. Shorting the source makes a fundamentally different circuit, so the 75 V, 25 V and 1 A values no longer apply. "Redraw the circuit, please. I beg everyone to redraw this."

1. Redraw and short the source . Under "2) Finding \(R_{TH}\)", the 100 V source is replaced by a wire joining the left ends of the two 25 Ω resistors.

2. Find what is in series . Imagine current entering at A. It divides at the top rail: some goes down \(R_4\), some down \(R_3\), and some goes left through the top 25 Ω. The part in the top 25 Ω does not divide again. It goes through the short and then through the bottom 25 Ω. Same current, so the two 25 Ω are in series: \(25 + 25 = 50\,\Omega\) .

3. Everything else is in parallel . The 50 Ω branch, \(R_3\) and \(R_4\) all connect between the top rail (A) and the bottom rail (B): \(R_{TH} = 50 \parallel 100 \parallel 100\).

4. Reduce: \(100 \parallel 100 = 50\,\Omega\), then \(50 \parallel 50 = 25\,\Omega\) . Check with reciprocals: \(\frac{1}{50} + \frac{1}{100} + \frac{1}{100} = \frac{4}{100}\), so \(R_{TH} = 25\,\Omega\) ✓.

\(R_{TH} = 25\,\Omega\)

At the auto-captions read "put the 225s in series we get 150". The page clearly shows 50 Ω, and \(25 + 25 = 50\,\Omega\) is correct. The caption misheard "the two 25s … a 50".

Part 3: The Thévenin equivalent

Thévenin equivalent at A–B: a 50 V source in series with 25 Ω (A = +). From the perspective of A and B, the two circuits now behave identically .

Cross-check with \(V_{OC}/I_{SC}\) (not in the video). Put a wire from A to B. It shorts out both 100 Ω resistors, leaving 100 V across \(25 + 25 = 50\,\Omega\). So \(I_{SC} = 100/50 = 2\,\text{A}\) and \(R_{TH} = 50\,\text{V}/2\,\text{A} = 25\,\Omega\) ✓.

Example 7 (added, not from the video): Why the equivalent is useful when the load changes

Attach a load resistor \(R_L\) between A and B of the ladder circuit and find the load current and voltage for several values. With the equivalent, every case is one series circuit:

\[ I_L = \frac{V_{TH}}{R_{TH} + R_L} = \frac{50}{25 + R_L}, \qquad V_L = I_L R_L = 50\,\frac{R_L}{25 + R_L} \]
\(R_L\)\(I_L = 50/(25+R_L)\)\(V_L = I_L R_L\)
10 Ω1.429 A14.29 V
25 Ω1.000 A25.00 V
50 Ω0.667 A33.33 V
75 Ω0.500 A37.50 V
100 Ω0.400 A40.00 V

Check: each row was also computed by nodal analysis of the original ladder with \(R_L\) attached, and the results agree exactly. That agreement is what "equivalent" means. Without Thévenin you would redo that full analysis five times.

For example, with \(R_L = 75\,\Omega\): \(I_L = 50/100 = 0.5\,\text{A}\) and \(V_L = 37.5\,\text{V}\).

6. Q&A: why didn't the parallel resistors become zero?

A student asked why, after shorting the 100 V source, the parallel resistors didn't all become 0 Ω (as the 10 Ω did in Example 5). The answer: a short eliminates a resistor only when the short is in parallel with that resistor, meaning it connects the resistor's two ends together. Shorting the source in the ladder did not put a short across any resistor .

How to tell: follow the current . "Current's your friend." Imagine current flowing from A to B:

  • Short in parallel (the instructor's red sketch). Current divides at the top node: it can go through the resistor or through the wire. It sums again at the bottom node. The wire and the resistor share both nodes, so they are in parallel. All the current takes the 0 Ω wire, the resistor "effectively disappears", and the result is a short (0 Ω) in its place . This is what happened to the 10 Ω in Example 5.
  • Short in series (the ladder) . Run current through it and mark where it divides and where it rejoins. The parallel group runs between the top rail and the bottom rail. The short (the old source position) does not span those two points. It only connects the left end of the top 25 Ω to the left end of the bottom 25 Ω. All it does is put the two 25 Ω in series . If the short were in parallel with something, you would see current divide at one end of it, and that doesn't happen here.
"Do not skip steps" . Run the current through it and draw it by hand to convince yourself . Just looking at the circuit is not enough. If you still struggle to recognise shorts, parallel and series, set up office hours with the instructor . Don't let it carry into the next course (311).

Assumptions & limitations

  • Linear circuits only. The theorem as stated applies to linear networks of resistances and sources . (Extra:) it does not hold for non-linear parts such as diodes or lamps whose resistance changes with current. Those can be the load, but not part of the network being Thévenized.
  • Equivalent only at A–B. The two circuits match only for what you connect between A and B . (Extra:) they are not equivalent inside. With A–B open, the original ladder still draws 1 A from its 100 V source and dissipates 100 W internally, while the open equivalent dissipates 0 W.
  • Depends on the terminals. Moving A or B gives a different equivalent .
  • Load removed. \(V_{TH}\) is the open-terminal voltage. If a load were left in place you would get the loaded voltage, which is not \(V_{TH}\).
  • Source deactivation as taught covers independent voltage sources (short them). (Extra:) independent current sources are opened. Ideal sources are assumed. A real source's internal resistance would stay in the circuit after deactivation.
  • \(R_{TH} = 0\) case. If an ideal voltage source sits directly across A–B (Example 5), the equivalent is just that source. The \(V_{OC}/I_{SC}\) shortcut can't be used there because \(I_{SC}\) is unbounded.
Circuits with dependent sources: finding \(R_{TH}\) (not from the video). The EE 211 course videos never cover dependent (controlled) sources. This box is for textbook problems and later courses.

A dependent source (diamond symbol) has a value set by some other voltage or current in the circuit, e.g. \(2I_x\) volts. It is not an independent excitation, so it cannot be zeroed. If you short it like an independent source, you get the wrong \(R_{TH}\). Leave every dependent source in place and use one of these:

  1. Test source: zero only the independent sources. Apply a test voltage \(V_{test}\) (or test current \(I_{test}\)) at A–B, solve for the other quantity, and \(R_{TH} = V_{test}/I_{test}\).
  2. \(V_{OC}/I_{SC}\): keep all sources on. Find \(V_{OC}\) (open A–B) and \(I_{SC}\) (short A–B, current from A to B). Then \(R_{TH} = V_{OC}/I_{SC}\).

Example 8 (added): Thévenin equivalent with a current-controlled voltage source

Given: 12 V, \(R_1 = 4\,\Omega\), CCVS \(2I_x\) (V, with \(I_x\) in A), \(R_2 = 2\,\Omega\). Find: \(V_{TH}\), \(R_{TH}\).

1. \(V_{OC}\). With A–B open, \(I_x\) flows around the single loop. KVL clockwise: \(+12 - 4I_x + 2I_x - 2I_x = 0 \Rightarrow I_x = 3\,\text{A}\). Then \(V_{OC} = I_x R_2 = 3 \times 2 = 6\,\text{V}\). Check: \(V_m = 12 - 4(3) = 0\,\text{V}\) and \(V_a = 0 + 2(3) = 6\,\text{V}\) ✓.

2. \(I_{SC}\). Short A–B, so \(V_a = 0\). \(R_2\) carries no current and all of \(I_x\) goes through the short. KVL: \(12 - 4I_x + 2I_x = 0 \Rightarrow I_x = 6\,\text{A} = I_{SC}\). So \(R_{TH} = V_{OC}/I_{SC} = 6/6 = 1\,\Omega\).

3. Test-source check. Short the 12 V (independent) but keep the \(2I_x\) source. Apply \(V_{test} = 1\,\text{V}\) at A–B. Current down \(R_2\): \(1/2 = 0.5\,\text{A}\). In the left branch, \(V_m = 0 - 4I_x\) and \(V_a = V_m + 2I_x = -2I_x = 1\), so \(I_x = -0.5\,\text{A}\). That means 0.5 A actually flows right-to-left, out of node a. Current supplied by the test source: \(I_{test} = 0.5 - I_x = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1\,\text{A}\). So \(R_{TH} = 1\,\text{V}/1\,\text{A} = 1\,\Omega\) ✓.

4. The trap. If you had also shorted the dependent source, you'd get \(R_1 \parallel R_2 = 4 \parallel 2 = 1.33\,\Omega\). That's wrong, because the dependent source changes how current responds to voltage at A–B.

\(V_{TH} = 6\,\text{V}\), \(R_{TH} = 1\,\Omega\). Check with a 2 Ω load: the equivalent gives \(6 \cdot \frac{2}{1+2} = 4\,\text{V}\), and solving the full circuit also gives \(V_a = 4\,\text{V}\) ✓.

Common mistakes

Finding \(V_{TH}\) and \(R_{TH}\) on one drawing. The node voltages and currents from the \(V_{TH}\) step mean nothing once the source is shorted. Fix: redraw a clean copy for \(R_{TH}\), as the instructor stresses at .
"Shorting the source makes everything zero." A short removes a resistor only if it is in parallel with it, i.e. it joins the resistor's two ends. Spot it: trace current from A to B and check whether the short spans a dividing/summing node pair. Fix: in the ladder, the short just puts the two 25 Ω in series ().
Using the wrong terminals. The same divider gave \(V_{TH} = 1\,\text{V}\) or \(4\,\text{V}\) depending on where A and B were. Fix: circle A and B before you start and read \(V_{AB}\) across exactly those points.
Leaving out the resistor that sits across A–B when computing \(V_{TH}\) (e.g. ignoring the right-hand 100 Ω). With nothing connected, it is part of the circuit and carries current. What stays open is the load position.
Drawing \(R_{TH}\) in parallel with \(V_{TH}\). The Thévenin form is always source in series with resistance (the "in parallel" at was a slip of the tongue). A resistor in parallel with an ideal voltage source would have no effect at the terminals.
Polarity flipped. \(V_{TH} = V_A - V_B\). Put the source's "+" toward A when A is the higher terminal. If you compute a negative \(V_{AB}\), flip the source.
Leaving a load connected while finding \(V_{TH}\) (extra). That gives the loaded voltage, not the open-circuit voltage. Remove the load first.
Shorting current sources or zeroing dependent sources (extra). Independent current sources are opened. Dependent sources stay in and need a test source or \(V_{OC}/I_{SC}\).
Arithmetic slips in parallel reductions. Quick checks: \(n\) equal resistors \(R\) in parallel give \(R/n\) (\(100 \parallel 100 = 50\)). Parallel resistance is always smaller than the smallest branch (80 Ω < 100 Ω; 25 Ω < 50 Ω).

Recap & formula sheet

Any linear two-terminal network can be replaced, as seen from terminals A and B, by \(V_{TH}\) in series with \(R_{TH}\). To Thévenize: (1) mark A and B and remove the load; (2) \(V_{TH}\) = open-circuit voltage from A to B, solved with your usual tools; (3) redraw, short the voltage sources, and \(R_{TH}\) = resistance seen from A–B; (4) draw the equivalent with "+" toward A. In this video: divider examples gave \(V_{TH} = 10\), 1 and 4 V, with \(R_{TH} = 0\) and 80 Ω. The 100 V ladder Thévenized to 50 V in series with 25 Ω. Trace current to decide series/parallel: a short only removes resistors it is in parallel with.
