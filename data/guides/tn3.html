Overview

This lecture/lab session continues Norton equivalent circuits. The instructor works two complete examples by hand: a 30 V circuit, where the short-circuit current is found with a voltage-drop method and then with a current-divider shortcut (\(I_N = 1.16\,\text{A}\), \(R_N = 16.39\,\Omega\)), and a 50 V circuit, where shorting the terminals removes a 51 Ω resistor from the circuit (\(I_N = 2.5\,\text{A}\), \(R_N = 14.37\,\Omega\)). The session ends with a preview of where Thévenin/Norton are used (DACs, Millman's theorem, transistor biasing) and an overview of Lab 5 (Thévenin–Norton equivalence, checked by hand and in LTspice).

Learning objectives

After this lesson you should be able to:

  • State what a Norton equivalent circuit is (a current source \(I_N\) with a shunt resistance \(R_N\)) and what "equivalent at terminals A–B" means.
  • Find \(I_N\) by shorting terminals A and B and solving for the current in the short, using series/parallel reduction, Ohm's law, KCL and the current divider.
  • Spot when the added short removes a resistor (a resistor directly across A–B) and redraw the circuit correctly.
  • Find \(R_N\) by shorting the independent voltage source and reducing the network seen from A–B, treating it as a new problem.
  • Use an imagined ("fake") test current from A to B to find where branches divide and recombine, and so which resistors are in series or parallel.
  • Explain why a 0 Ω short in parallel with any resistor gives 0 Ω, and why no current flows in open terminals even though a voltage exists across them.
  • Describe how Lab 5 checks equivalence: the same load resistor \(R_L = 1\,\text{k}\Omega\) must see the same \(V_{RL}\) and \(I_{RL}\) in the original, Thévenin and Norton circuits, by hand and in LTspice.
  • (Added) Convert the Norton results to Thévenin form with \(V_{TH} = I_N R_N\) and get the polarity right.

Prerequisites

Series and parallel resistors
Series resistors add: \(R_{eq} = R_1 + R_2\). Two parallel resistors combine as product over sum: \(R_1 \parallel R_2 = \frac{R_1R_2}{R_1+R_2}\). Two equal resistors in parallel give half the value.
Ohm's law
\(V = IR\). It gives the voltage drop across a resistor from its current, or the current from its voltage.
Kirchhoff's current law (KCL)
Current into a node equals current out of it (conservation of charge). The instructor uses it to say that 2.32 A entering a node must also leave it.
Current divider
Current entering two parallel branches splits in inverse proportion to their resistances: \(I_1 = I_T\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}\). For equal resistances each branch gets half.
Thévenin equivalent (tn1)
Any linear two-terminal network can be replaced by a voltage source \(V_{TH}\) in series with \(R_{TH}\). \(R_{TH}\) is found by turning off the independent sources (a voltage source becomes a short) and finding the resistance seen from the terminals.
Norton introduction (tn2)
The Norton form uses a current source \(I_N\) in parallel with \(R_N\). Last lecture introduced it with a simple example; this lecture adds two harder ones.
Short circuit and open circuit
A short is a 0 Ω wire: it has no voltage across it, but it can carry any current. An open circuit has no current, but it can have a voltage across it.

1. Norton recap: goal and procedure

Last time Norton circuits were introduced with a simple example. The goal is the same as for Thévenin: build an equivalent circuit that behaves every which way like the original as seen from the terminals. In Norton form the whole circuit reduces to a single current source and a shunt resistance:

To build it you need two values:

  1. \(I_N\): short terminals A and B together and find the current flowing through that short.
  2. \(R_N\): found exactly the same way as the Thévenin resistance. Short out the voltage source and find the resistance seen from terminals A and B.

The instructor finds \(I_N\) first in both examples. The two calculations are separate problems.

2. Example 1: Norton current by shorting A–B

Example 1 — 30 V source, 30 Ω \(\parallel\) 9 Ω, two 12 Ω

Given (values written on the paper at and ): a 30 V source. Its + terminal feeds two resistors in parallel, 30 Ω (top branch) and 9 Ω (middle branch), which meet at a junction. From that junction a 12 Ω resistor goes down to the bottom rail, and another 12 Ω goes right to terminal A. Terminal B is the bottom rail (the source's − side).

Goal: the Norton equivalent (\(I_N\) and \(R_N\)) seen from A–B. This section finds \(I_N\).

Step 1: short A and B. Redraw the circuit with a wire across A–B. The current in that wire is \(I_N\). Follow the current: it leaves the source, splits between the 30 Ω and 9 Ω, recombines at junction x, and then splits again between \(R_3\) (down) and \(R_4\) (toward the short). The current through the right-hand 12 Ω (\(R_4\)) is the Norton current, because \(R_4\) is in series with the short.

Step 2: combine the parallel pairs. With the short in place, \(R_4\) runs from x straight to the bottom rail, so it is in parallel with \(R_3\). The 30 Ω and 9 Ω are also in parallel.

\[ R_1 \parallel R_2 = \frac{30 \cdot 9}{30 + 9} = \frac{270}{39} = 6.923\,\Omega \approx 6.92\,\Omega \qquad R_3 \parallel R_4 = \frac{12\cdot 12}{12+12} = 6\,\Omega \]

6.92 Ω and 6 Ω.

Step 3: total resistance and total current. The two equivalents are in series: \(6.92 + 6 = 12.92\,\Omega\).

\[ I_T = \frac{30\,\text{V}}{12.92\,\Omega} = 2.32\,\text{A} \]

Step 4: voltage-drop method (the instructor's "most straightforward" approach, ). Backfill: 2.32 A leaves the source, splits between the 30 Ω and 9 Ω, recombines, and splits again between the two 12 Ω. The 30 Ω and 9 Ω are in parallel, so they share one voltage drop. Ohm's law on their 6.92 Ω equivalent ():

\[ V_{30\parallel 9} = I_T\,(R_1\parallel R_2) = 2.32 \times 6.92 = 16.05\,\text{V} \]

Starting from 30 V at the source's + terminal and dropping 16.05 V, junction x is at

\[ V_x = 30 - 16.05 = 13.95\,\text{V} \]

The bottom rail, and therefore the shorted terminal A–B, is at 0 V. So the right-hand 12 Ω has 13.95 V across it, and by Ohm's law ():

\[ I_N = \frac{V_x}{R_4} = \frac{13.95\,\text{V}}{12\,\Omega} = 1.16\,\text{A} \]

Step 5: shortcut, the current divider . The 2.32 A leaving junction x splits between two equal 12 Ω resistors, so it divides evenly ():

\[ I_N = I_T\,\frac{R_3}{R_3 + R_4} = 2.32 \times \frac{12}{24} = \frac{2.32}{2} = 1.16\,\text{A} \]

\(I_N = 1.16\,\text{A}\), flowing from A to B through the short (, written into the Norton circuit at ). "Either way we end up getting the correct answer" ().

Rounding. The instructor rounds at each step (6.92, 12.92, 2.32). Without rounding, \(I_T = 30/12.923 = 2.3214\,\text{A}\), \(V_x = 13.93\,\text{V}\) and \(I_N = 1.1607\,\text{A}\). Both versions round to 1.16 A, so this is rounding, not an error. The 13.95 V on the board comes from the rounded 2.32 A and 6.92 Ω (checked in Python).

Legibility. In the redrawn circuits () the top resistor's "30" looks like "70". The audio says 30 and the math uses 30 \(\parallel\) 9 = 6.92, so the value is 30 Ω.

3. Example 1: Norton resistance

Finding \(R_N\) is "a new problem": go back to the original circuit (not the shorted one), short out the voltage source, and find the resistance seen from A and B.

Step 1: replace the source with a short and redraw (–).

Step 2: use a fake current to see the groupings. If the series/parallel relationships are hard to see, imagine current flowing from A to B. "Where current divides and where current sums defines my start and end position of my parallelism." From A the current goes left through \(R_4\) and reaches junction x. There it divides: part goes down through \(R_3\), and part goes through the 30 Ω and 9 Ω (which divide again) and returns down the wire that replaced the source. Everything recombines on the bottom rail and leaves at B. So:

  • 30 Ω \(\parallel\) 9 Ω, and that combination is also in parallel with the 12 Ω \(R_3\), because both connect junction x to the bottom rail once the source is a wire ().
  • The result is in series with \(R_4\) on the way to A.

Without a source there is no current, because voltage is what causes current to flow. The instructor's analogy: the earthquake causes the tsunami, and the voltage causes the current. The fake current is only something you imagine to help you see the circuit.

Step 3: reduce one pair at a time (, to avoid "punching in that rather large inverse equation" into the calculator). From before, \(30\parallel 9 = 6.92\,\Omega\). Redraw it as an interim step (), then :

\[ 6.92 \parallel 12 = \frac{6.92 \times 12}{6.92 + 12} = \frac{83.04}{18.92} = 4.39\,\Omega \]

Step 4: add the series resistor. 4.39 Ω is in series with the 12 Ω leading to A ():

\[ R_N = 4.39 + 12 = 16.39\,\Omega \]

Norton equivalent of Example 1: \(I_N = 1.16\,\text{A}\) in parallel with \(R_N = 16.39\,\Omega\) ().

This circuit behaves every which way like the original from terminals A and B. Today's lab, a simulation lab, demonstrates this kind of equivalence: you derive both a Norton and a Thévenin equivalent for a new circuit ().

Converting to Thévenin form, and getting the polarity right. \(R_{TH} = R_N\), and the open-circuit voltage is the Norton current flowing through \(R_N\):

\[ V_{TH} = I_N R_N = 1.16 \times 16.39 = 19.0\,\text{V} \]

Check directly: with A–B open, no current flows in \(R_4\), so \(V_{AB}\) equals the junction voltage. That is a divider of 30 V between 6.923 Ω and 12 Ω: \(30 \times \frac{12}{18.923} = 19.02\,\text{V}\). The exact \(I_N R_N = 1.1607 \times 16.390 = 19.02\,\text{V}\) agrees (Python).

Direction ↔ polarity. The short-circuit current flows out of terminal A, through the short, and into B, so the Norton source arrow points toward A. The current source pushes current out at A, so A is the + terminal of \(V_{TH}\). If the short-circuit current flowed from B to A instead, the arrow would point toward B and B would be the + terminal of the Thévenin source. (Source transformations like this are covered in st1.)

4. Q&A: the current divider and the fake-current direction

Question 1 : "Can you go over how you got the current again?" This refers to treating the two 12 Ω as a current divider ().

  • 2.32 A enters junction x. Some goes one way and some the other, but by conservation of charge (KCL) the same 2.32 A must leave the node.
  • The two branches are equal (12 Ω each), so the current divides in half: 1.16 A down through \(R_3\) and 1.16 A through \(R_4\). The instructor wrote "1.16" on both arrows. Check: \(1.16 + 1.16 = 2.32\,\text{A}\) ().
  • The short is in series with \(R_4\), so the short also carries 1.16 A, which is \(I_N\).

Question 2 : "When you solved for the Norton resistance, why was the current reversed?"

  • No current actually flows, and nothing is going "backwards". The arrows from A to B are pretend current, a tool for seeing what is in parallel and what is in series ().
  • Imagine a voltage source attached to A and B (the instructor drew a small battery beside the terminals) pushing fake current through the network.
  • The polarity does not matter: you could flip that imagined source (+ at B) and run the current the other way. It still shows the same series/parallel structure.

Many students say they have trouble seeing parallel and series. "Pretend to run current" is the instructor's standard way to see it. Put the dividing node and the summing node on the picture and the parallel groups appear.

5. Example 2: when the short removes a resistor

Example 2 — 50 V, 10 Ω, 20 Ω \(\parallel\) 20 Ω, 51 Ω across A–B

Given (): a 50 V source, + on top. 10 Ω is in series with two parallel 20 Ω resistors. Below them, a 51 Ω resistor goes to the bottom rail. Terminal A is the junction below the 20 Ω pair, and terminal B is the bottom rail. The 51 Ω therefore sits directly across A–B.

Goal: Norton equivalent seen from A–B (). The instructor jokes that \(R_N\) also stands for "registered nurse". This section finds \(I_N\).

Step 1: short A–B, and watch what that does to the circuit. Adding the short risks changing the original circuit. Depending on where A and B are, a component can get shorted out. Here the 51 Ω is in parallel with the new short, so it is bypassed: "this 51 ohm resistor goes out the door as soon as we draw this short". Redraw without it (). The short takes its place.

Step 2: the Norton current is now just the total current. "This problem gets easier when you try to solve for the Norton current."

\[ 20 \parallel 20 = 10\,\Omega, \qquad R_T = 10 + 10 = 20\,\Omega, \qquad I_T = \frac{50\,\text{V}}{20\,\Omega} = 2.5\,\text{A} \]

–. However the current divides between the two 20 Ω branches, it recombines at the summing node and returns to the source through the short ().

\(I_N = I_T = 2.5\,\text{A}\) (A→B through the short), written into the Norton circuit at .

6. Why a short "deletes" a resistor in parallel with it

Student question: "Why did the short get rid of the 51?" The quick answer is that current takes the path of least resistance (). The more formal explanation uses the parallel-resistance formula.

Mini-example — 10 Ω in parallel with a short

A source feeds a 10 Ω series resistor, then \(R_1 = 10\,\Omega\) across A–B. A short (\(R_2 = 0\,\Omega\)) is placed between A and B (). It is an extremely low-resistance path directly in parallel with \(R_1\) ().

Treat it as two resistors in parallel:

\[ R_T = R_1 \parallel R_2 = \frac{10 \cdot 0}{10 + 0} = \frac{0}{10} = 0\,\Omega \]

The numerator is zero, so the result is 0 Ω whatever \(R_1\) is. Redrawn, the circuit is 10 Ω in series with 0 Ω, and \(R_1\) no longer matters.

Any resistor in parallel with a short combines to 0 Ω. More current takes the path of lesser resistance, and with a 0 Ω path all the current goes through the short ().

The current divider gives the same result. The current in \(R_1\) is \(I_T\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2} = I_T\frac{0}{10} = 0\). Also, the voltage across a short is 0 V, so by Ohm's law the parallel resistor carries \(0/R_1 = 0\) current. In Example 2 the 51 Ω is in exactly this position across A–B, so it carries no current while \(I_N\) is being found.

7. Example 2: Norton resistance

Short out the voltage source and find the resistance between A and B. "This is a new problem so don't rely on your previous solution". Do not reuse the shorted-terminal circuit from the \(I_N\) step. In particular the 51 Ω is back.

Step 1: 20 Ω \(\parallel\) 20 Ω. A fake current shows it right away: it divides at the top of the pair and comes back together at the bottom. \(20\parallel 20 = 10\,\Omega\) ().

Step 2: the two 10 Ω are in series. After redrawing (10 Ω top, 10 Ω equivalent, then A, with 51 Ω between A and B), imagine a source across A–B (). Current goes from the highest voltage (A) to the lowest (B), and the only route other than the 51 Ω passes through both 10 Ω resistors. The same current flows through each, so they are in series (): \(10 + 10 = 20\,\Omega\).

Step 3: 20 Ω \(\parallel\) 51 Ω. Now it is clear that the 20 Ω path and the 51 Ω are in parallel across A–B:

\[ R_N = 20 \parallel 51 = \frac{20 \times 51}{20 + 51} = \frac{1020}{71} = 14.37\,\Omega \]

Norton equivalent of Example 2: \(I_N = 2.5\,\text{A}\) in parallel with \(R_N = 14.37\,\Omega\) (–).

Directly: with A–B open, 50 V divides across 20 Ω and 51 Ω, so \(V_{AB} = 50\cdot\frac{51}{71} = 35.92\,\text{V}\). The two agree. 8. Q&A: is there current in the open terminals?

– (time check: about nine minutes of class remain, and the instructor offers to answer questions.)

Student: "In the original circuit, the current between A and B is pointing down, right?" The instructor draws the real current paths in the original Example 2 circuit (): from the source through the 10 Ω, splitting down through both 20 Ω resistors, recombining, down through the 51 Ω, and back along the bottom wire to the source.

  • There is no current through A or B or along the lead wires to them. With nothing connected, there is no path.
  • There is a voltage between A and B, because there is the potential to make current flow if a path were added.
  • Physically, electrons move through the source, the 10 Ω, the 20 Ω branches, the 51 Ω and the wires of the completed loop. Electron flow is opposite to the conventional current direction (). In the wire stub to the right of the junction (toward A) no charge moves: "there's no electrons wiggling" ().

This is why the Thévenin voltage is called the open-circuit voltage. Because the leads carry no current, the voltage at A equals the voltage at the junction it is wired to (no IR drop in the lead). In Example 1 the same reasoning lets you ignore \(R_4\) when computing \(V_{AB}\) with A–B open, since no current flows in it.

Digital-to-analog converters

Two pre-drawn circuits () are "incredibly useful", "highly crucial" circuits in embedded and computing systems. You probably have a few in your computer right now (). Both are digital-to-analog converters: inputs \(V_{B0}\)…\(V_{B3}\) are each either 0 V or 5 V (a binary number), and the output is an analog voltage ().

  • You can analyse these with the fundamental rules, but it gets messy quickly, with overly large equations that are hard to reduce, especially across many input combinations ().
  • Applying Thévenin/Norton equivalents lets you derive the equations much more simply.
  • Example use: a sound card takes digital values stored in memory and outputs them as an analog waveform.
  • The two circuits do the same job in different ways. To understand the trade-offs you have to analyse and compare them, and Norton/Thévenin and ultimately Millman's theorem are the key tools (). This is covered in 311.

The binary-weighted page also shows a partially written expression "\(V_{out} = V_{B0}\) …" and a stray "\(V_i\)". Neither was completed or discussed in this video, so no formula from the board is given here.

Preview of the results (not derived in the video; checked by nodal analysis in Python for all 16 input codes). For the binary-weighted DAC with no load, Millman's theorem gives a conductance-weighted average of the inputs:

\[ V_{out} = \frac{\frac{V_{B0}}{R} + \frac{V_{B1}}{2R} + \frac{V_{B2}}{4R} + \frac{V_{B3}}{8R}}{\frac{1}{R} + \frac{1}{2R} + \frac{1}{4R} + \frac{1}{8R}} = \frac{8V_{B0} + 4V_{B1} + 2V_{B2} + V_{B3}}{15} \]

For the R–2R ladder, repeated Thévenin conversions from the grounded end show that each node "looks like" a source with resistance R. The unloaded output is

\[ V_{out} = \frac{V_{B3}}{2} + \frac{V_{B2}}{4} + \frac{V_{B1}}{8} + \frac{V_{B0}}{16} \]

Note that the two drawings weight the bits oppositely. In the ladder, \(B_0\) is farthest from the output and has the smallest weight. In the binary-weighted drawing, \(V_{B0}\) has the smallest resistor and so the largest weight. Which input is the "most significant bit" is a labelling choice. With all inputs at 5 V: ladder output 4.6875 V, binary-weighted output 5 V.

No employer will hand you a circuit on day one, ask for its Norton equivalent, and send you home with a full day's pay. The value of these tools shows up when you design and analyse real circuits such as DACs.

Transistor biasing

In 311 you work with more powerful components such as transistors, which computer engineers should understand. One of the simplest transistor circuits has a two-resistor divider from \(V_{CC}\) to ground feeding the transistor's input (base), plus a resistor above and below the transistor. There are approximation techniques, but a better, more accurate one is to take a Thévenin equivalent of the front half. Redraw \(V_{CC}\) as a voltage source, mark A at the divider's midpoint (the transistor input) and B at ground, and exclude the transistor. The divider becomes a single constant voltage source with a series resistance (), "a much easier problem" than the original.

For the divider above, with the source as drawn and A–B open: \(V_{TH} = V_{CC}\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\) (voltage divider), and \(R_{TH} = R_1 \parallel R_2\) (short \(V_{CC}\), and both resistors connect A to B). This is the standard "voltage-divider bias" simplification you will meet in 311.

Without more background these conversions can look academic, like busy work. The instructor says he is never trying to give busy work: you crawl before you run, the way arithmetic leads to algebra and algebra to calculus (–).

  1. Thévenin voltage (A–B open): from the top node, \(R_2 = 2\,\text{k}\Omega\) is in parallel with the path \(R_4 + R_3 = 2\,\text{k}\Omega\), giving 1 kΩ. The top node is at \(30\cdot\frac{1}{2+1} = 10\,\text{V}\). \(R_4\) and \(R_3\) split that 10 V equally, so \(V_{TH} = V_{AB} = 5\,\text{V}\) (A positive).
  2. Thévenin/Norton resistance (\(V_1\) shorted): from A to B, \(R_4\) is in parallel with \((R_1\parallel R_2) + R_3 = 1 + 1 = 2\,\text{k}\Omega\). So \(R_{TH} = R_N = 1\,\text{k}\parallel 2\,\text{k} = 666.7\,\Omega\).
  3. Norton current: \(I_N = V_{TH}/R_{TH} = 5/666.7 = 7.5\,\text{mA}\). Directly, with A–B shorted, \(R_4\) is bypassed, the top node is at \(30\cdot\frac{0.667}{2.667} = 7.5\,\text{V}\), and \(7.5\,\text{V}/R_3 = 7.5\,\text{mA}\).
  4. Load \(R_L = 1\,\text{k}\Omega\): \(V_{RL} = 5\cdot\frac{1000}{1666.7} = 3.0\,\text{V}\) and \(I_{RL} = 3.0\,\text{mA}\). The Norton version gives the same: \(7.5\,\text{mA}\times(666.7\parallel 1000) = 7.5\,\text{mA}\times 400\,\Omega = 3.0\,\text{V}\).

Assumptions & limitations

  • Linear circuits. Norton (and Thévenin) equivalents apply to circuits of resistors and sources. Everything in this lecture is DC and resistive.
  • Equivalent only at the terminals. The Norton circuit reproduces the voltage and current at A–B for any load. It does not tell you the currents or power inside the original circuit. In Example 1, for instance, the 30 Ω and 9 Ω currents are not visible in the equivalent.
  • "Short the voltage source" applies to independent voltage sources when finding \(R_N\). (Added: an independent current source would be replaced by an open circuit, and dependent sources need a different method. Neither appears in this video.)
  • Adding the short can change the circuit. Any resistor directly across A–B is bypassed while you find \(I_N\), but it is back when you find \(R_N\).
  • Ideal parts. Wires and the added short are 0 Ω, and the source is ideal.
  • Rounding. The board values are rounded at intermediate steps (6.92, 2.32, 13.95). Final answers agree with exact values to 3 significant figures.
  • (Added) The Norton form needs a finite, non-zero \(R_N\) for conversion to Thévenin (\(V_{TH} = I_NR_N\)). If \(R_N = 0\) (an ideal voltage source across A–B), no Norton equivalent exists, and if \(R_N\) is infinite (an ideal current source), no Thévenin equivalent exists.

Common mistakes

Reusing the \(I_N\) circuit to find \(R_N\). The shorted-terminal circuit is a different problem. Start again from the original, short the source (not A–B), and keep every resistor, including any that the A–B short had removed (the 51 Ω in Example 2). The instructor stressed this at .

Keeping a resistor that the short bypasses. When finding \(I_N\), a resistor connected directly across A–B carries no current (\(R\parallel 0 = 0\)). Leaving the 51 Ω in Example 2 would give the wrong \(I_N\). Check: which components have both ends on A and B?

Missing new parallel combinations created by the short. In Example 1, shorting A–B puts the series 12 Ω (\(R_4\)) in parallel with the shunt 12 Ω (\(R_3\)). If you treat \(R_4\) as dangling, you get the wrong total current.

Using the wrong voltage for Ohm's law. To get \(I_N\) in Example 1 you need the voltage across \(R_4\): \(13.95 - 0 = 13.95\,\text{V}\), not 30 V and not 16.05 V. Always ask which two node voltages bracket the resistor.

Using the current divider with unequal branches as "half". The "split in half" shortcut works only because both branches are 12 Ω. Otherwise use \(I_1 = I_T R_2/(R_1+R_2)\).

Treating the fake current as real, or worrying about its direction. It is only a visual aid for finding dividing and summing nodes. Either direction works (). It is not \(I_N\).

Thinking current flows in the open terminals. With nothing connected, the A and B leads carry 0 A, but a voltage still exists between them ().

Drawing \(R_N\) in series, or the source arrow backwards. Norton means current source in parallel with \(R_N\). The arrow points toward the terminal that the short-circuit current flows out of (A here). That terminal is also the + side of \(V_{TH}\).

Lab 5: answering \(V_{R4}\), \(I_{R4}\) instead of \(V_{RL}\), \(I_{RL}\). The table wants the load resistor's voltage and current (). All six entries in each row should match.
