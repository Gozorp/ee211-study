Overview

This lecture/lab explains how a capacitor charges through a resistor. At the instant voltage is applied, an uncharged capacitor acts like a short (maximum current \(V_S/R\)). As it charges, KVL forces the resistor voltage and the current to decay. After five time constants (\(\tau = RC\)) it holds the full supply voltage and acts like an open circuit. The same universal time constant chart used for inductors gives the percentages, and the formulas \(v_C = V_S(1-\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau})\) and \(v_R = V_S\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\) give exact values. A worked example (50 V, 100 kΩ, 0.1 μF) is followed by the lab: an LTspice simulation of a 10 V pulse into 470 Ω and 10 μF, and the same circuit on a breadboard measured live with an oscilloscope (cursor measurement of τ, capacitor tolerance, LCR meter).

Learning objectives

After this lesson you should be able to…

  • describe what an uncharged capacitor does at the instant a voltage is applied (acts like a short) and after it is fully charged (acts like an open), and compute the maximum current \(V_S/R\);
  • explain with KVL and Ohm's law why the current and the resistor voltage decay while the capacitor voltage builds up;
  • compute \(\tau = RC\) with correct unit prefixes and explain why Ω·F = s;
  • read the universal time constant chart: 63.2 %, 86.5 %, 95 %, 98 %, 99.3 % (build-up) and 36.8 %, 13.5 %, 5 %, 2 %, 0.7 % (decay), and apply the "5τ = fully charged" rule;
  • evaluate \(v_C(t) = V_S(1-\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau})\), \(v_R(t) = V_S\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\) and \(i(t) = v_R/R\) at any time, and check the results against each other with KVL;
  • set up an LTspice PULSE source and transient simulation for an RC circuit and read the 1τ and 5τ values with a cursor, remembering to add the pulse delay;
  • measure τ on an oscilloscope with cursors (Δt = τ, read ΔV ≈ 63.2 % of the step) and explain why measured values differ from calculated ones (capacitor tolerance, source loading, noise).

Prerequisites

  • Capacitor basics (Capacitors: Introduction): a capacitor is two conducting plates separated by an insulator. It stores charge \(Q\), and the stored charge produces a voltage \(v_C = Q/C\). Capacitance is measured in farads (F); practical values are μF, nF and pF. Capacitors "oppose a change in voltage".
  • Ohm's law: \(I = V/R\). Used here to turn the resistor voltage into the circuit current at any instant.
  • Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL): the voltages around a closed loop add to zero. In a series source–R–C loop this means \(V_S = v_R + v_C\) at every instant.
  • Series circuits: the same current flows through every element of a series loop, so \(i_R = i_C\).
  • Universal time constant chart and \(\tau = L/R\) (Introduction to Inductors, L/R time constant): the same exponential "build-up" and "decay" curves were used for inductor current. This lecture reuses the chart for capacitors.
  • Scientific notation and metric prefixes: k = 103, m = 10−3, μ = 10−6, n = 10−9. LTspice uses m for milli, u for micro and n for nano.
  • LTspice transient simulation with a PULSE source: set up the same way as in the inductor lab (previous lecture).
  • Oscilloscope basics (volts/div, time/div, probes, cursors) are developed further in the first oscilloscope lesson and measuring period and volts/division.

1. Charging a capacitor through a resistor: the "storybook"

The lecture opens with a recap : a capacitor stores charge; storing charge develops a voltage across it; pulling charge off (to power something else) lowers that voltage over time. The rate of charging follows the same universal time constant chart used for the inductor (L/R time constant) .

The simplest capacitor circuit

A DC source \(V_S\), a switch, a resistor \(R\) and a capacitor \(C\) in one series loop. The switch closes at \(t = 0\). Before that, the capacitor has no charge, so it has 0 V across it and no current flows .

Stage 1 — at \(t = 0\) the uncharged capacitor acts like a short

With zero charge on its plates, the capacitor has 0 V across it, so for that brief moment it behaves like a plain wire. The only thing limiting the current is the resistor, so the current is the maximum it will ever be :

\[ I_{max} = \frac{V_S}{R} \qquad (\text{at } t = 0) \]
\(V_S\)
applied (supply) voltage, V
\(R\)
series resistance, Ω
\(I_{max}\)
current at the instant the switch closes, A

Applies to an uncharged capacitor in series with R at the instant the voltage is applied.

Quick example — 100 V and 100 Ω

Given: \(V_S = 100\,\text{V}\), \(R = 100\,\Omega\), capacitor uncharged. Find: the current when the switch closes.

1. At \(t = 0\) the capacitor is a short, so the whole 100 V is across R.

2. Ohm's law: \(I = 100\,\text{V} / 100\,\Omega\).

\(I_{max} = 1\,\text{A}\) — 100 % on the current (blue) curve of the chart at \(t = 0\) .

The instructor stresses: at time zero the capacitor behaves like a short , and he writes "@T=0 cap acts like a short" under the second circuit sketch.

Why you sometimes see a spark when you plug in a charger

Every power supply (linear or switching — the difference doesn't matter here) has a few capacitors connected almost directly across the wires that go to the wall outlet . When the device has been unplugged, those capacitors have no charge . At the instant you plug it in they act like a short circuit and draw a very large current for a brief moment — that is the little spark . They then charge up very quickly to the wall voltage (120 V RMS, 170 V peak; RMS is covered in a later lecture) .

Stage 2 — while charging, KVL makes the current decay

As charge collects, a voltage builds on the capacitor. By KVL the source voltage is shared: \(V_S = v_R + v_C\). For example, with 10 V applied and 1 V already on the capacitor, the resistor has 9 V . So:

  • \(v_C\) goes up (the red "build-up" curve) ;
  • \(v_R = V_S - v_C\) goes down, and therefore the current \(i = v_R/R\) goes down (the blue "decay" curve) .

"I drops/decays as C charges; VC increasing" — written under the third circuit sketch . He labels the falling curve "Decay" and the rising one "Buildup" .

The time constant

\[ \tau = R\,C \]
\(\tau\)
time constant, s
\(R\)
resistance in series with the capacitor, Ω
\(C\)
capacitance, F

Multiplying the resistance (in ohms) by the capacitance (in farads) gives the time of the first time constant on the chart . It takes five time constants for the capacitor to be (practically) fully charged.

Why Ω·F is seconds: \(1\,\Omega = 1\,\text{V/A}\) and \(1\,\text{F} = 1\,\text{C/V}\) (coulomb per volt), so \(\Omega\cdot\text{F} = \frac{\text{V}}{\text{A}}\cdot\frac{\text{C}}{\text{V}} = \frac{\text{C}}{\text{A}} = \frac{\text{A}\cdot\text{s}}{\text{A}} = \text{s}\).

Which R? Here there is only one resistor. In a larger network, the R in \(\tau = RC\) is the Thévenin resistance seen by the capacitor (remove C, turn off the independent sources, and find the resistance between the capacitor's terminals). If the capacitor charges through one path and discharges through a different path, each path has its own R and therefore its own τ.

Stage 3 — after 5τ the capacitor is fully charged and acts like an open circuit

Eventually \(v_C = V_S\): the capacitor holds as much charge as the applied voltage can push onto it . With, say, 10 V on the source and 10 V on the capacitor, the resistor has 0 V, so no current flows and the capacitor behaves like an open circuit . On the page: "after 5T, VS = VC, I = 0" .

\[ t \ge 5\tau:\quad v_C \approx V_S,\qquad v_R \approx 0,\qquad i \approx 0 \]

This is the DC steady state of a capacitor: once charged, it blocks DC current.

The whole "storybook" in one table

The instructor tells the charging process as a story , drawn as four circuit sketches under the chart:

Moment\(v_C\)\(v_R\)\(i\)Capacitor behaves like…
Before the switch closes000— (no charge, nothing happening)
\(t = 0\) (switch just closed)0\(V_S\)\(V_S/R\) (max, 100 %)short circuit

The current tells the "inverse story" of the voltage: it starts as large as Ohm's law allows and ends at zero amps once the capacitor is fully charged .

3. The charging equations

The chart curves are described by two formulas . With them you can find "everything you want to know over time" as a capacitor charges .

\[ v_C(t) = V_S\left(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\right), \qquad \tau = RC \]
\(v_C(t)\)
capacitor voltage at time \(t\), V
\(V_S\)
supply voltage (final capacitor voltage), V
\(t\)
time since the switch closed (since the step), s
\(\tau\)
time constant \(RC\), s
\(\mathrm{e}\)
2.71828…

Written at . Applies to an initially uncharged capacitor charging from a constant voltage \(V_S\) through \(R\).

\[ v_R(t) = V_S\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau} \]
\(v_R(t)\)
resistor voltage at time \(t\) during charging, V

Written at . Note that \(v_R + v_C = V_S\) at every instant (KVL).

\[ i(t) = \frac{v_R(t)}{R} = \frac{V_S}{R}\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau} \]
\(i(t)\)
loop current (through R and C), A

To get the current, "solve for your voltage across the resistor and apply Ohm's law" .

The instructor calls the constant 2.71828 "epsilon" and writes ε . Its usual name is e (Euler's number). On a calculator use the \(e^x\) key, usually near the π key .

Discharging, the general form, and solving for time. The lecture writes only the charging formulas (discharging is shown on the LTspice plot in section 6). These standard results extend them:

  • Discharging a capacitor that starts at \(V_0\) through a resistance \(R\): \(v_C(t) = V_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\). The current has the same shape but flows the opposite way, which is why the LTspice current goes negative after 100 ms.
  • General form for any starting voltage \(V_0\) and final voltage \(V_f\): \(v_C(t) = V_f + (V_0 - V_f)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\). With \(V_0 = 0,\ V_f = V_S\) it is the charging formula; with \(V_f = 0\) it is discharging. On the scope (section 8) the capacitor swings from about −5 V to +5 V, so \(V_0 = -5\,\text{V}\), \(V_f = +5\,\text{V}\) — the 63.2 % rule still applies to the 10 V change.
  • Continuity: the voltage across a capacitor cannot jump instantly (that would need infinite current), so \(v_C\) just after switching equals \(v_C\) just before. This is the "capacitor opposes a change in voltage" idea used at .
  • Solving for time: from \(v_C = V_S(1-\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau})\), \(t = -\tau\ln\!\left(1 - \frac{v_C}{V_S}\right)\). Example with the lab values (\(\tau = 4.7\,\text{ms}\), 10 V): the time to reach 5 V is \(t = -4.7\,\text{ms}\times\ln(0.5) = 3.26\,\text{ms}\).

4. Worked example: 50 V, 100 kΩ, 0.1 μF at t = 10 ms

Example 1 — find \(v_C\), \(v_R\) and \(i\) after 10 ms

Given : \(V_S = 50\,\text{V}\), a switch that closes at \(t = 0\), \(R = 100\,\text{k}\Omega\), \(C = 0.1\,\mu\text{F}\) , capacitor initially uncharged.

Goal : \(v_R\) and \(v_C\) after \(t = 10\,\text{ms}\). (He picked 10 ms "somewhere along this graph" without telling the class where — it turns out to be exactly 1τ.)

1. Write the capacitor formula : \(v_C = V_S\left(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t/RC}\right)\).

2. Substitute , writing 0.1 μF in scientific notation as \(0.1\times10^{-6}\,\text{F}\) : \[ v_C = 50\left(1 - 2.718^{-\frac{10\times10^{-3}}{(100\times10^{3})(0.1\times10^{-6})}}\right) \]

3. Evaluate the exponent : \(RC = 100\,000 \times 0.1\times10^{-6} = 0.01\,\text{s}\), so \(t/RC = 0.01/0.01 = 1\) and \(\mathrm{e}^{-1} = 0.3679\).

4. \(v_C = 50\,(1 - 0.3679) = 50 \times 0.6321\).

\(v_C(10\,\text{ms}) = 31.61\,\text{V}\)

5. Where are we on the chart? Divide by the maximum, 50 V : \(31.61/50 = 0.632 = 63.2\,\%\) . That is the first time constant, so 10 ms must equal τ.

6. Check τ : \(\tau = RC = 100\,\text{k}\Omega\times(0.1\times10^{-6}\,\text{F}) = 0.01\,\text{s} = 10\,\text{ms}\) ✓ . So the chart's time axis for this circuit reads 10, 20, 30, 40 ms, and the capacitor is "fully charged" at 5τ = 50 ms (written in pencil under the chart).

7. Resistor voltage by KVL — "our basic rules do not go away": \(v_R = 50 - 31.61 = 18.39\,\text{V}\) .

8. Current by Ohm's law (the same through R and C because it is a series circuit ): \(I_R = 18.39\,\text{V} / 100\,\text{k}\Omega = 0.184\,\text{mA}\) .

\(v_R(10\,\text{ms}) = 18.39\,\text{V}\), \(i(10\,\text{ms}) = 0.184\,\text{mA}\)

9. Compare with the starting current . At \(t = 0\) the capacitor is a short: \(I_{max} = 50\,\text{V}/100\,\text{k}\Omega = 0.5\,\text{mA}\) . Ratio: \(0.184/0.5 = 0.368 = 36.8\,\%\) — exactly the decay-curve value at 1τ.

10. Check \(v_R\) with its own formula : \(v_R = V_S\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau} = 50 \times 2.718^{-\frac{10\,\text{ms}}{(100\,\text{k})(0.1\times10^{-6})}}\) \(= 50\times \mathrm{e}^{-1} \approx 18.39\,\text{V}\) ("18.395" on the page), the same as the KVL result .

The instructor marks these results on the chart in pencil: 0.5 mA at the top of the current curve, 31.61 V at the 63.2 % point, 0.184 mA at the 36.8 % point, and 50 V at 5τ .

5. Real parts are analog: tolerances and parasitics

Before the lab, the instructor explains why lab measurements never come out "perfect" :

  • Digital circuits feel exact (ones and zeros), but the analog world has component tolerances: resistors are not exactly their marked value, and wires are not exactly 0 Ω .
  • Every real part has all three properties — resistance, inductance and capacitance. A capacitor is called a capacitor because it has much more capacitance than R or L, but it still has some of each . A good wire is mostly a resistance very close to 0 Ω, with a little inductance and capacitance .
  • The unwanted R and L are called parasitic resistance and parasitic inductance .
  • "Digital circuits are made from analog parts" — to get digital you must have analog . Courses that interface with the outside world (embedded systems) run into far more analog issues than gate-level logic courses .

"If you want to be a decent engineer you kind of have to pay attention to everything" — the analog behaviour is always there, even when it can be abstracted away.

6.5 How long until the capacitor reaches 10 V?

Before 50 ms nothing is applied, so the capacitor does not charge. At 50 ms the source jumps to 10 V within 1 ns, but the capacitor "is going to oppose a change in voltage" and needs time to follow . Full charge (100 % on the chart) takes five time constants .

Example 2 — time constant of the lab circuit

Given: \(R = 470\,\Omega\), \(C = 10\,\mu\text{F} = 10\times10^{-6}\,\text{F}\), \(V_S = 10\,\text{V}\) (pulse height).

1. One time constant : \(\tau = RC = 470 \times 10\times10^{-6} = 0.0047\,\text{s}\) \(= 4.7\,\text{ms}\) .

2. Five time constants: \(5\tau = 5RC = 5(470)(10\text{E}{-6}) = 23.5\,\text{ms}\) .

3. Voltage at 1τ: \(63.2\,\% \times 10\,\text{V} = 6.32\,\text{V}\) .

4. The pulse starts at 50 ms, so on the LTspice time axis 1τ is at \(50 + 4.7 = 54.7\,\text{ms}\) and 5τ is at \(50 + 23.5 = 73.5\,\text{ms}\) .

5. Maximum current (capacitor = short at the step) : \(I_{max} = 10\,\text{V}/470\,\Omega = 0.02128\,\text{A} \approx 21.3\,\text{mA}\) .

\(\tau = 4.7\,\text{ms}\); \(v_C(1\tau) = 6.32\,\text{V}\) at 54.7 ms; about 10 V after \(5\tau = 23.5\,\text{ms}\) (73.5 ms on the plot); \(I_{max} = 21.3\,\text{mA}\).

The page writes 10 μF as 10E-6. The instructor warns that this "e" is just scientific notation (× 10−6), "not to be confused with epsilon" (Euler's e) .

Q&A : once charged, the capacitor sits at 10 V. It cannot charge higher than the applied 10 V (not 11 V or 12 V), and it won't stay lower (8 V) because the source keeps pushing charge onto the plates. When it is fully charged, the resistor voltage is zero .

6.7 The current

  • The current plot looks different from the chart only because of its scale .
  • Maximum current happens at the 50 ms edge, when the capacitor has 0 V on it: \(10\,\text{V}/470\,\Omega = 21.3\,\text{mA}\) . The plot peaks between 20 and 24 mA .
  • Right-click the current axis to set its limits: top 21.3 mA (then bottom −21.8 mA, and finally bottom 0) so the current curve lines up with the voltage curve like the chart .
  • The current goes negative during discharge because it now flows the other way .
  • Physical picture: the current falls as the capacitor's voltage builds, "two voltage sources fighting each other" — the source and the charging capacitor . When the pulse goes back to 0 V, the capacitor discharges through the resistor .

In this circuit the discharge path is the same as the charge path (the source at 0 V acts like a wire), so the discharge also has \(\tau = 4.7\,\text{ms}\) and the falling curve after 100 ms is the mirror image of the rise.

Current through C vs through R : hover over the capacitor and click to plot I(C1). I(R1) appeared as the negative of I(C1) because LTspice's current arrow for the resistor pointed the other way (to the left) . After rotating the resistor (Ctrl+R twice) and re-running, the two currents are identical : "the current is the same through all elements of a series circuit" .

8. Live demo: measuring the time constant on the oscilloscope

The instructor recaps the numbers to look for : \(\tau = 4.7\,\text{ms}\); at 4.7 ms the capacitor should be at 63.2 % of the maximum, 6.32 V; the maximum, 10 V, takes five time constants, 23.5 ms . He then starts the oscilloscope's PC software (a GW Instek scope) and switches it to continuous capture . "That sure looks like a cap charging and discharging" .

8.1 The breadboard circuit

  • A 470 Ω resistor in series with a 10 μF electrolytic capacitor on a breadboard .
  • A function generator (red and black alligator clips) applies a square wave to the RC series pair — it plays the role of the LTspice pulse, switching repeatedly between low and high.
  • The scope probe measures the voltage across the capacitor ; a second channel is then connected to the applied (generator) voltage so both can be seen together .

8.3 Why the "square wave" sags at each rising edge

The yellow trace (the generator output) is not a clean square wave: right after each rising edge it starts lower and then rounds up toward the top. With the capacitor unplugged, the generator output is a clean square wave again . Reason: at each edge the uncharged capacitor acts like a short, so the generator must suddenly supply a large current — it is acting like a power supply and "having a little bit of a hard time keeping up with that current demand" . The capacitor is loading the generator. For practical purposes the measurement still gives the answers we want .

Why the generator sags: a function generator has an internal output resistance (commonly 50 Ω). At the edge, the current \(V/(R_{out}+R)\) flows through that internal resistance, so the voltage at its terminals drops by \(i R_{out}\) and recovers as the current decays. That internal resistance is effectively in series with the 470 Ω, so in principle it lengthens the time constant a little, and the sag means the step applied to the RC pair is slightly smaller than the generator's unloaded amplitude.

8.4 Measuring τ with cursors — the 63.2 % point

  1. Show only the capacitor trace (CH2) and trigger on it .
  2. Place the first vertical (time) cursor exactly where the voltage starts to rise .
  3. Move the second cursor until the readout Δt = 4.7 ms, one time constant . (The Δt readout is the time between the two cursors .)
  4. Read ΔV, the voltage change between the two cursor points, and compare it with 63.2 % of the 10 V step (6.32 V) .

What the screen showed. With Δt = 4.70 ms the readout gave ΔV = 5.84 V and 5.92 V ("5.926… we're bouncing around a little bit") . Nudging the cursors ("monkey around with the values") gave readings of 6.00 V, 6.08 V and 6.16 V — with Δt between 4.70 and 4.90 ms on the screen — and he calls it "close enough for government work" . He summarizes: about 6.1–6.2 V, with an occasional bounce up to 6.3 V . Switching the acquire mode to Average 32 makes the trace cleaner (the readout then showed ΔV = 6.00 V at Δt = 4.90 ms ).

Is averaging cheating? "Only when you don't know what you're doing with it" .

Reading τ without cursors (divisions × time/div). Find the level that is 63.2 % of the way from the bottom to the top of the step (here about 3.2 divisions above the bottom at 2 V/div), see how many horizontal divisions after the start of the rise the trace crosses it, and multiply by the time/div. For τ = 4.7 ms at 10 ms/div that is about 0.47 division — so for a more precise reading use a faster time base (e.g. 2 ms/div, where τ is about 2.35 div) or the cursors, as the instructor did.

Unmentioned in the video: at the scope's automatic "RiseTime" readout for the capacitor trace showed 10.32 ms. For an exponential, the 10 %→90 % rise time is \(\tau\ln 9 \approx 2.2\tau\), so \(\tau \approx 10.32/2.197 = 4.70\,\text{ms}\) — another way to measure τ.

8.5 Why it is not perfect: capacitor tolerance

"Why is it not perfect, and why am I okay with it not being perfect?" The missing information is the capacitor's tolerance :

  • Aluminum electrolytic capacitors are typically ±20 %; some are +20 %/−40 % .
  • For a 10 μF part, 20 % is 2 μF, so the capacitor could be anywhere from 8 μF to 12 μF and still be "in spec" .
  • On Digi-Key (aluminum electrolytics, about 2,250 results for his search), filtering to ±10 % leaves only 17 parts, about 40 cents each — the best tolerance available for general-purpose electrolytics . The ±20 % filter covers about 2,000 parts, from about 3 cents each .
  • With perhaps 20 capacitors in a consumer design, paying 40 cents each for a still-poor ±10 % is not affordable, so ±20 % parts are normal . The scope measurement "is really at the mercy of the tolerance of my cap" .

How much tolerance moves the numbers (with R = 470 Ω): \(C = 8\,\mu\text{F} \Rightarrow \tau = 3.76\,\text{ms},\ 5\tau = 18.8\,\text{ms}\); \(C = 12\,\mu\text{F} \Rightarrow \tau = 5.64\,\text{ms},\ 5\tau = 28.2\,\text{ms}\). So a ±20 % capacitor alone can shift τ by ±20 %.

8.6 Checking 5τ on the scope

Moving the second cursor to Δt = 23.5 ms (5τ) shows the capacitor "just about done charging", about its 99 % charge state . The readout gave ΔV = 9.76 V (it fluctuated between 9.67 V and 9.76 V on screen), again "a bit at the mercy of our cap" .

8.7 Measuring the real capacitance with an LCR meter

To find the true capacitance, use an LCR meter rather than a multimeter: many multimeters can measure capacitance, but an LCR meter is better . He switches a Proster BM4070 LCR tester to capacitance (20 μF range), checks the zero , inserts the capacitor and reads 9.70 μF — not 10 μF .

His conclusion: the time-constant measurements on the scope will be a little off; with 9.7 μF the capacitor should charge a little more quickly . Electrolytic capacitors at ±20 % are "still considered acceptable margins", so "don't expect accuracy where you aren't going to get accuracy" — something learned by experience .

Possible inconsistency in the explanation. A 9.7 μF capacitor gives \(\tau = 470\times9.7\,\mu\text{F} = 4.56\,\text{ms}\), so it should charge faster and read more than expected: 6.43 V at 4.7 ms and 9.94 V at 23.5 ms. The scope readings were lower (≈5.8–6.2 V at 1τ and 9.67–9.76 V at 5τ), which points to slower charging, so the low capacitance does not explain them. The instructor says he saw "9.3, 9.6 volts somewhere around there" at 5τ ; the frames show 9.67–9.76 V. More likely contributors (our analysis, not discussed in the video): the generator output sags under load, so the real step is a little under 10 V — the trace started at about −4.96 V and was at about +4.8 V at 5τ, a step of roughly 9.8 V, and 63.2 % of 9.8 V is 6.2 V, very close to the readings; noise on the trace (readings jumped by ±0.1 V); and exactly where cursor 1 sits on the start of the rise. The scope's automatic rise-time readout (10.32 ms, see the extra note in 8.4) corresponds to τ ≈ 4.70 ms, which suggests the time constant itself was close to the calculated value. The generator's internal resistance and the resistor's own tolerance could also shift τ slightly.

Assumptions & limitations

  • One resistor in series with one capacitor. The lecture treats only a single series R and C. For a capacitor inside a bigger network, the R in \(\tau = RC\) is the Thévenin resistance seen from the capacitor's terminals, and different charge/discharge paths can give different time constants.
  • The capacitor starts uncharged (0 V) when the switch closes or the pulse rises. \(v_C = V_S(1-\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau})\) and \(v_R = V_S\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\) assume this. If the capacitor already holds a voltage, use the general form \(v_C = V_f + (V_0 - V_f)\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\) (extra note in section 3).
  • The source is an ideal step. A real function generator has internal resistance and cannot supply unlimited current, which is why its square wave sagged at .
  • \(t\) is measured from the moment the voltage changes. In LTspice the pulse rises at 50 ms, so 1τ is at 54.7 ms on the plot, not 4.7 ms.
  • "Fully charged after 5τ" is a rule of thumb. The exponential never mathematically reaches 100 %; at 5τ it is 99.3 %. Engineers treat that as done.
  • Ideal parts. Calculations use marked values. Real electrolytic capacitors are often ±20 % (sometimes +20/−40 %), resistors have their own tolerance, and every part has parasitic R, L and C. Measured τ will differ from the calculated value by several percent.
  • At \(t = 0\) the ideal model allows a very large current (unlimited if R = 0). In real circuits wiring and source resistance limit it; that inrush is the spark when plugging in a charger.
  • The percentages apply to the size of the step. If the source swings from −5 V to +5 V (as on the scope), 63.2 % means 63.2 % of the 10 V change measured from the starting level, not 6.32 V above 0 V.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting unit prefixes. \(100\,\text{k}\Omega \times 0.1\,\mu\text{F}\) must be entered as \(100\,000 \times 0.1\times10^{-6}\). Entering 100 × 0.1 gives "10", which is not 10 ms. Since Ω·F = s, convert the answer to ms only at the end.
  • Confusing e with the "E" (exponent) notation. 10E-6 means 10 × 10−6; it has nothing to do with Euler's e . Use the \(e^x\) key for \(\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}\).
  • Measuring time from 0 instead of from the step. In the simulation the pulse starts at 50 ms: 1τ is at 54.7 ms and 5τ at 73.5 ms on the time axis.
  • Treating the capacitor as an open circuit at \(t = 0\). An uncharged capacitor is a short at the instant of switching (max current \(V_S/R\)). It is an open only after it is fully charged (≈5τ).
  • Thinking the capacitor can charge above the source. It approaches \(V_S\) (10 V in the lab) and stops there .
  • Using the wrong curve. While charging, \(v_C\) follows the rising (build-up) curve; the current and \(v_R\) follow the falling (decay) curve. While discharging, \(v_C\) follows the decay curve.
  • Quoting 99.8 % for 5τ. The correct value is \(1-\mathrm{e}^{-5} = 99.3\,\%\) (4τ is 98.2 %).
  • Being confused by a negative current in LTspice. LTspice measures current in the direction set by the part's orientation. If the resistor was placed "backwards", its current plots negative. Rotate it (Ctrl+R twice) or flip the sign mentally .
  • Starting the scope measurement from the wrong point. Put the first time cursor exactly where the capacitor voltage starts to rise, then put the second one Δt = τ later. Measure ΔV from the bottom of the waveform (the starting level), not from the screen centre or 0 V.
  • Forgetting the ×10 probe setting. If the probe is ×10 but the channel is set to ×1, all voltages read 10 times too small. Match the channel setting to the probe .
  • Expecting the measurement to match the calculation exactly. A ±20 % capacitor marked 10 μF can be anywhere from 8 μF to 12 μF. Differences of a few percent between calculated, simulated and measured values are normal.
