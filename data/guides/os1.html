Overview

This lecture/lab is where the course leaves constant (DC) voltages behind and starts working with alternating current (AC). The instructor contrasts a DC source with an AC (sine-wave) source, names the positive and negative alternations, and gives the three ways an AC magnitude is quantified — peak, peak-to-peak and RMS — using the US wall outlet (120 V RMS = 170 V peak = 340 V peak-to-peak) as the running example. He explains why RMS is the "useful average" (it produces the same heating as an equal DC voltage). Then he fires up an oscilloscope and function generator: probe and ground-clip connections, zooming the time base, the scope's automatic frequency readout, AC passing through a transformer while a 9 V battery (DC) does not, and finally cursor measurements of a wall transformer's output (80 V p-p) and input (340 V p-p).

Scope note: the middle and later parts of the video teach transformer theory (turns ratio, step-up/step-down). That is outside this topic and is only outlined in the "Other content" section, except where a transformer calculation is needed to interpret a scope reading.

Learning objectives

After this lesson you should be able to…

  • Distinguish a DC waveform (constant line on a voltage-vs-time graph) from an AC waveform (sine wave centred on 0 V), and draw the DC (battery) and AC (circle with a sine) source symbols.
  • Identify the positive and negative alternations of a sine wave and state which way current flows through a resistor during each.
  • Define peak voltage \(V_{pk}\), peak-to-peak voltage \(V_{pp}\) and RMS voltage \(V_{RMS}\), and convert any one into the other two for a sine wave (e.g. 170 V peak = 340 V p-p ≈ 120 V RMS; 80 V p-p = 40 V peak = 28.28 V RMS).
  • Explain why the plain average of a sine wave (0 V) is useless and how "square → mean → square root" fixes that.
  • State the meaning of RMS (same heating/power as an equal DC value) and use \(V_{RMS} = I_{RMS}R\) and \(P_{avg} = I_{RMS}V_{RMS}\).
  • Define frequency (cycles per second, Hz) and period (time of one cycle), and convert with \(T = 1/f\).
  • Connect a scope probe tip and ground clip across a source, identify the volts/division, time/division, coupling and frequency readouts on the screen, and read amplitude and period from the number of divisions.
  • Choose a time base that shows a few cycles, and explain why the frequency readout fails ("<2 Hz") when less than one cycle is on screen.
  • Use the scope's voltage cursors (Δ readout) to measure a peak-to-peak voltage and convert it to peak and RMS.
  • Explain why a transformer's output on the scope is a sine wave for AC input but a flat 0 V line for a DC input, and compute a turns ratio from input and output scope readings.
  • Check a measurement against the scope's voltage rating before connecting to mains.

Prerequisites

Ohm's law and power
\(V = IR\); a resistor dissipates \(P = VI = I^2R = V^2/R\). This lecture reuses exactly these equations with RMS values.
Voltage is measured between two points
A voltmeter (and a scope) always reads the difference between two nodes, so you need two connections: the "+" lead and the reference ("−"/ground) lead.
Inductors store energy in a magnetic field
A current through a coil builds a magnetic field; only a changing field induces a voltage in a nearby coil. This is why a transformer passes AC but not DC (shown on the scope in this video).
Graphs of voltage versus time
Time on the horizontal axis (increasing to the right), voltage on the vertical axis with 0 V on the centre line. An oscilloscope screen is exactly this graph.
Sine function basics
\(\sin\) swings between +1 and −1, repeats every full cycle, and spends equal time above and below zero.
Metric prefixes
k = 10³ (10 kHz = 10 000 Hz); m = 10⁻³ (5 ms = 0.005 s); μ = 10⁻⁶ (50 μs = 0.000 05 s).

1. DC versus AC

1.1 Direct current

Everything in the course so far has been DC: a source that always gives one fixed value (10 V, 12 V, 5 V…) for the whole time the circuit runs. Connect a DC source \(V_S\) to a resistor and a constant current \(I = V_S/R\) flows, by Ohm's law .

Once voltages change, you have to look at a graph of voltage versus time . For DC the graph is "boring": a horizontal line at \(V_S\) (e.g. 10 V) while time increases to the right .

1.2 Alternating current

What comes into our houses is alternating current. (The instructor mentions the Tesla–Edison "battle" over how to power the country ; AC won for practical reasons that turn out to be transformers, later in the video.) The AC source is drawn as a circle with a small sine wave inside . Its graph, centred at 0 V, goes up to \(V_S\), back down through zero to \(-V_S\), back up, and continues forever . On the paper the example source is labelled \(V_S = 10\,V_{pk}\), with the graph marked \(V_S = 10\,\text{V}\) at the top and \(-V_S = -10\,\text{V}\) at the bottom .

1.3 Positive and negative alternations

  • Positive alternation : whenever the applied voltage is above the time axis, the source's "+" is on top and "−" on the bottom, so current flows through the resistor in one direction.
  • Negative alternation : whenever the voltage is below the axis, the polarity has flipped ("+" on the bottom) and the current flows the opposite way .

Half of each cycle is positive and half is negative. Naming them is useful because many circuits behave differently when the voltage is positive versus negative .

2. Three ways to quantify an AC magnitude: peak, peak-to-peak, RMS

How big is an AC voltage? Saying "it goes up to \(V_S\) and down to \(-V_S\)" is one answer, but there are three ways to quantify the magnitude, each useful in different circumstances . The page at lists them:

  1. Peak Voltage — \(V_{pk}\)
  2. Peak to Peak Voltage — \(V_{pk\text{-}pk}\) or \(V_{pp}\) or \(V_{pkpk}\)
  3. RMS — Root Mean Square — \(V_{RMS}\)

2.1 Peak voltage \(V_{pk}\)

The highest voltage the AC actually reaches. If \(V_S\) is 10 V, the waveform goes up to 10 V, so it is "10 V peak".

2.2 Peak-to-peak voltage \(V_{pp}\)

The difference between the highest and the lowest voltage. It may be written \(V_{pk\text{-}pk}\), \(V_{pp}\), \(V_{pkpk}\) or "V peak to peak" — all mean the same thing .

Example 1 — 10 V peak expressed as peak-to-peak

Given: the AC source from section 1, \(V_S = 10\,V_{pk}\): the wave goes up to +10 V and down to −10 V. Find: its peak and peak-to-peak values.

1. Peak = highest value reached = 10 V → "10 V peak" .

2. Peak-to-peak = highest − lowest = \(10\,\text{V} - (-10\,\text{V}) = 20\,\text{V}\) .

10 V peak is identical to 20 V peak-to-peak — two names for the same waveform.

2.3 RMS voltage \(V_{RMS}\) — motivated by the wall outlet

The third way, root mean square, is written \(V_{RMS}\) . The instructor motivates it with a question: what is the voltage of a US electrical outlet? Students answer 120 V . But the outlet waveform he draws goes up to 170 V and down to −170 V . So where is 120 V on this graph ?

Example 2 — the US outlet in all three forms

Given: the outlet sine wave reaches +170 V and −170 V.

1. Peak: highest value = 170 V peak.

2. Peak-to-peak: \(170\,\text{V} - (-170\,\text{V}) = 340\,\text{V}\) p-p .

3. RMS: what the outlet is quoted as — 120 V RMS (the conversion is worked in Example 3).

170 V peak = 340 V peak-to-peak = 120 V RMS (US outlet)

Why not just use peak or peak-to-peak? You can't calculate much from them: the waveform is at its peak for only one moment; the rest of the time it is somewhere else. It would be nicer to describe the AC voltage by an average .

But the plain average of a sine wave is zero ("AVG V = 0 V" on the paper) — it dips negative exactly as much as it goes positive. That is useless: a sine wave applied to a resistor clearly drives current and dissipates power, so calling it "0 V" makes no sense .

2.4 Where "root mean square" comes from

Each word of the name is an operation : Root = square root; Mean = average (written "½" on the paper — for a squared sine wave the average comes out to one half of its top value); Square = raise to the power 2. They are applied in reverse order of the name :

  1. Start with the sine wave of peak \(V_{pk}\) .
  2. Square it: every negative value becomes positive, so the squared wave is a train of positive humps reaching \(V_{pk}^2\) .
  3. Average (mean) the squared wave: the result is \(\tfrac12 V_{pk}^2\) . This is a genuine average, but of the squared voltage.
  4. Square root "to undo the square" .
\[ V_{RMS} = \sqrt{\tfrac12 V_{pk}^{2}} \;\Rightarrow\; V_{RMS} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\,V_{pk} \quad\text{or}\quad V_{RMS} = 0.707\,V_{pk} \]
\(V_{RMS}\)
root-mean-square voltage, V (RMS)
\(V_{pk}\)
peak voltage, V

This is the boxed result on the paper ; the instructor says to memorize it. It holds for a sine wave centred on 0 V (the ½ comes from the shape of a squared sine). The same factor converts currents: \(I_{RMS} = 0.707\,I_{pk}\) .

Why the mean of a squared sine is ½ of its peak. With \(v(t) = V_{pk}\sin\omega t\), the identity \(\sin^2\theta = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2\theta)\) gives \(v^2 = \tfrac12 V_{pk}^2 - \tfrac12 V_{pk}^2\cos 2\omega t\). Over a whole cycle the cosine term averages to zero, leaving \(\overline{v^2} = \tfrac12 V_{pk}^2\), so \(V_{RMS} = \sqrt{\overline{v^2}} = V_{pk}/\sqrt2\). In integral form, \(V_{RMS} = \sqrt{\frac1T\int_0^T v(t)^2\,dt}\). A numerical average of \((170\sin\theta)^2\) over one cycle gives 14 450 V², whose square root is 120.2 V. Other shapes have other factors (a ±A square wave has \(V_{RMS} = A\); a triangle wave \(A/\sqrt3\)).

Example 3 — where the outlet's 120 V comes from

Given: \(V_{pk} = 170\,\text{V}\). Find: \(V_{RMS}\).

1. Use \(V_{RMS} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}V_{pk}\): \(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(170\,\text{V}) = 120\,V_{RMS}\) .

2. With the rounded factor on the calculator: \(0.707 \times 170 = 120.19\) (exact \(1/\sqrt2\) gives 120.21).

\(V_{RMS} \approx 120\,\text{V}\) — the "120 V" of a US outlet is its RMS value.

3. What an RMS value means: equal heating, Ohm's law and average power

3.1 Definition

From the paper : RMS voltage is the value of AC voltage or current which produces the same heating effect (i.e. power) as an equal value of DC voltage. "In short: we get back Ohm's law & power" .

The two loads are identical; one is driven by 10 V DC, the other by 10 V RMS AC . The power dissipated by \(R_{L1}\) equals the power dissipated by \(R_{L2}\) : as far as power is concerned, 10 V RMS is functionally identical to 10 V DC .

3.2 Ohm's law and power with RMS values

\[ V_{RMS} = I_{RMS}\,R \qquad\qquad P_{avg} = I_{RMS}\,V_{RMS} \]
\(V_{RMS}\)
RMS voltage across the resistor, V
\(I_{RMS}\)
RMS current through it, A
\(R\)
resistance, Ω
\(P_{avg}\)
average power dissipated, W

These are Ohm's law and the power equation, "written a little differently" for AC (boxed on the paper at ). They apply to resistors, with both quantities in RMS.

Example 4 — 10 V RMS across a 10 Ω load

Given: \(V_{RMS} = 10\,\text{V}\), \(R_{L2} = 10\,\Omega\). Find: \(I_{RMS}\) and \(P_{avg}\).

1. Ohm's law with RMS values: \(I_{RMS} = \dfrac{V_{RMS}}{R} = \dfrac{10\,V_{RMS}}{10\,\Omega} = 1\,\text{A}\) RMS .

2. Average power: \(P_{avg} = I_{RMS}V_{RMS} = (1\,A_{RMS})(10\,V_{RMS}) = 10\,\text{W}\) .

3. Check against the DC circuit: 10 V DC across 10 Ω gives 1 A and 10 W — the same, which is the whole point of RMS.

\(I_{RMS} = 1\,\text{A}\), \(P_{avg} = 10\,\text{W}\) (average power, not "RMS watts")

Power is never stated in terms of RMS — there is no "root-mean-square power"; it is average power .

Why equal heating and "root of the mean of the square" are the same idea. The instantaneous power in a resistor is \(p(t) = v(t)^2/R\), so the average power is \(\overline{v^2}/R\). A DC voltage \(V_{DC}\) gives \(V_{DC}^2/R\). Setting them equal gives \(V_{DC} = \sqrt{\overline{v^2}}\) — the RMS value. A numerical check: a 10 V RMS sine (14.14 V peak) on 10 Ω averages 10.0 W. Equivalent forms: \(P_{avg} = I_{RMS}^2R = V_{RMS}^2/R\).

4. Lab demo: the oscilloscope and the function generator

To see AC, the instructor turns on an oscilloscope — an instrument that lets you view a changing voltage with respect to time — and a function generator, which behaves like an AC voltage source . The scope in the video is a GW Instek digital oscilloscope, displayed on the computer through its PC software; the function generator itself is not shown on screen.

4.1 Connecting the probe and ground clip

The probe goes into the measurement instrument, and the leads come out of the function generator . Voltage is always measured between two points, so as well as the "+" side of the AC source you must also connect the "−" side : the probe tip clips to the generator's red lead and the probe's ground clip to its black lead (visible at ). Nothing else is connected yet — the scope is simply looking at what comes out of the generator .

Proof that the trace really is the generator: when he disconnects the probe (lower left of the video) the waveform disappears, and when he reconnects it the waveform comes back .

4.3 Frequency, cycles and period

Frequency is how many cycles per second. The scope's readout in the lower right says 10 kHz, which means 10 000 cycles per second . Each cycle is measured from where it starts to where it repeats; one cycle starts at one point along the x-axis and ends where the pattern begins again — and you get 10 000 of those every second.

\[ f = \frac{1}{T} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad T = \frac{1}{f} \]
\(f\)
frequency, Hz = cycles per second
\(T\)
period = time for one cycle, s

(The video defines frequency as cycles per second; \(T = 1/f\) follows directly — if 10 000 cycles fit in one second, each takes 1/10 000 s.)

Example 5 — 10 kHz on the screen, before and after zooming the time base

Given (screen): frequency readout 10.0001 kHz; time base first 500 μs/div , then 50 μs/div . Find: the period and how many cycles fit on the 10-division screen.

1. Period: \(T = 1/f = 1/(10\,000\,\text{Hz}) = 0.0001\,\text{s} = 100\,\mu\text{s}\).

2. At 500 μs/div: one cycle spans \(100\,\mu\text{s} \div 500\,\mu\text{s/div} = 0.2\) div, so the 10-div screen (5 ms) holds \(5\,\text{ms}/100\,\mu\text{s} = 50\) cycles — the crowded "zoomed out" picture.

3. At 50 μs/div: one cycle spans \(100 \div 50 = 2\) divisions; the screen (500 μs) holds 5 cycles — individual cycles are now easy to see.

4. Reading it back from the grid: crest-to-crest = 2 div × 50 μs/div = 100 μs → \(f = 1/100\,\mu\text{s} = 10\,\text{kHz}\), agreeing with the readout.

\(T = 100\,\mu\text{s}\); 50 cycles on screen at 500 μs/div, 5 cycles (2 div each) at 50 μs/div.

4.4 Turning the generator down to 60 Hz

What comes out of our outlets is not 10 000 cycles per second but 60 cycles per second, 60 Hz, so the instructor cranks the generator down to 60 Hz to match the wall . At first the scope is still set for the fast signal and is "quite zoomed out" in the wrong sense : at 50 μs/div the screen shows only a sliver of a 60 Hz cycle, and the frequency readout shows "<2Hz" because it cannot find a complete cycle. After he changes the time scale the sine wave comes back .

Example 6 — three cycles of 60 Hz on 5 ms/div

Given (screen at ): 1 V/div, 5 ms/div, frequency readout 59.9924 Hz. Find: the period and the number of cycles on screen; check against the instructor's count.

1. Period: \(T = 1/f = 1/60\,\text{Hz} = 16.67\,\text{ms}\) (from the readout, \(1/59.9924 = 16.669\,\text{ms}\)).

2. Divisions per cycle: \(16.67\,\text{ms} \div 5\,\text{ms/div} = 3.33\) div.

3. Screen width: \(10\,\text{div} \times 5\,\text{ms/div} = 50\,\text{ms}\); cycles shown \(= 50\,\text{ms} / 16.67\,\text{ms} = 3\).

\(T \approx 16.7\,\text{ms}\) ≈ 3.33 div per cycle → about 3 cycles on screen — matching the instructor's count "one, two, about three cycles" .

Example 7 — back to 1 kHz: period and amplitude from the grid

Given (screen at ): the instructor goes back up to 1 kHz "for the sake of this example"; settings 1 V/div, 500 μs/div, DC coupling; readout 999.994 Hz. Find: divisions per cycle, and the amplitude read from the grid.

1. Period: \(T = 1/1000\,\text{Hz} = 1\,\text{ms}\) → \(1\,\text{ms} \div 0.5\,\text{ms/div} = 2\) div per cycle (5 cycles on screen) — consistent with the trace, whose crests are 2 divisions apart.

2. Amplitude: the crests reach about 2½ divisions above the centre line → \(V_{pk} \approx 2.5\,\text{div} \times 1\,\text{V/div} \approx 2.5\,\text{V}\).

3. Converting (sine): \(V_{pp} \approx 5\,\text{V}\), \(V_{RMS} \approx 0.707 \times 2.5 \approx 1.8\,\text{V}\).

\(T = 1\,\text{ms}\) (2 div); amplitude ≈ 2.5 V peak ≈ 5 V p-p (estimate read from the frame).

The instructor never states the generator's amplitude; the ≈2.5 V peak figure is this guide's reading of the grid (crests about 2.4–2.6 divisions above centre at 1 V/div), so treat it as approximate.

5. AC versus DC through a transformer, seen on the scope

5.1 AC goes through

With the generator at 1 kHz, the instructor connects the generator's leads to the transformer's input (primary) and moves the probe to the output (secondary) . The output is a sine wave: "it's exactly what I said, I'm getting AC out of this." He considers adding a second probe to show input and output together but decides it would take too long , so input and output are viewed one at a time.

The scope at shows the secondary at 1 V/div and 500 μs/div with a readout of 999.971 Hz: still 2 divisions per cycle (same frequency as the input), and crests about 2½–2.6 divisions high — essentially the same size as the generator signal in Example 7.

Example 8 — why the output looked the same as the input (1 : 1 transformer)

Later in the video the instructor explains the demo: "this is a one-to-one transformer — that's why you did not see any magnitude change when I probed the input versus the output."

1. Transformer relation (taught at ): \(\dfrac{N_P}{N_S} = \dfrac{V_P}{V_S}\).

2. For 1 : 1, \(N_P/N_S = 1\), so \(V_S = V_P\) — the same type of voltage (peak, p-p or RMS) on both sides. His board example: 10 Vpk in → 10 Vpk out .

3. Applied to the scope: input ≈ 2.5 div at 1 V/div → output ≈ 2.5 div at 1 V/div, same 1 kHz.

A 1 : 1 transformer passes the AC unchanged in size and frequency, which is what the scope showed.

5.2 DC does not go through: the 9 V battery

Why not? With AC, the alternating current in the primary acts like a generator, inducing alternating current in the secondary. With a constant voltage on the primary, the magnetic field grows as large as it can and then just stays there; it doesn't get bigger or smaller, so nothing more is induced . The electrons in the secondary get one initial push and then nothing .

Demonstration : a 9 V battery on the primary — black mini-grabber clip on the battery's "−" terminal, yellow clip on "+9 V" — and the scope on the secondary . Result: "nothing… we have no voltage coming out of this" . When the battery was first attached there was a brief change in the field, but once the field stopped changing the output was zero . The paper summarises: AC in → AC out; DC in → ⊘ → 0 V, "DC cannot pass" .

5.3 Seeing the alternating field: the compass demo

Inside a transformer driven by AC the magnetic field keeps alternating. To show it, the instructor places a plain coil of wire (an inductor) next to a compass and drives it from the function generator :

  • At 1 Hz the compass needle wobbles back and forth once per second — one cycle per second .
  • At 2 Hz it wobbles twice as fast .
  • Increasing the amplitude gives a stronger magnetic field, so the needle swings further ("more weeble to my wobble") .
  • With straight DC the needle just gets stuck in one direction — a one-time push and that's it .

The compass makes frequency tangible: at 1 Hz the period is \(T = 1/1\,\text{Hz} = 1\,\text{s}\) per swing cycle, at 2 Hz it is 0.5 s. At 60 Hz (16.7 ms) or 1 kHz (1 ms) the changes are far too fast for a compass or your eye to follow — which is exactly why the oscilloscope is needed.

6. Measuring a wall transformer with the scope's cursors

6.1 Set-up and safety

Now the input is the wall outlet: 120 V RMS into the primary of a larger transformer . (He remarks that once you start looking at changing voltages you really need something to view them, and the oscilloscope is your number one tool for that .) He probes the output first, between the outer secondary terminals ("don't worry about that center tap for now") .

6.2 Output: adjusting the vertical scale

With the scope still at 1 V/div the trace is far too big — "it's a bit high" — so he changes the vertical scale and backs it off . The screen at shows 20 V/div, 5 ms/div, DC coupling, frequency 60.0106 Hz.

  • Peaks: about 2 divisions above and below centre → \(2 \times 20\,\text{V/div} = 40\,\text{V}\) peak.
  • Period: about 3⅓ divisions per cycle → \(3.33 \times 5\,\text{ms} = 16.7\,\text{ms}\) → 60 Hz (three cycles on the screen), agreeing with the readout.

Example 9 — output voltage with the cursors

Given (screen at ): 20 V/div, 5 ms/div; one horizontal cursor placed at the bottom of the waveform and one at the top . Cursor readout: cursor 1 = 40.0 V, cursor 2 = −39.2 V, Δ = 79.2 V. Find: \(V_{pp}\), \(V_{pk}\), \(V_{RMS}\).

1. The Δ readout is the difference between the high and the low voltage — that is the peak-to-peak value: \(40.0 - (-39.2) = 79.2\,\text{V}\), which the instructor rounds to 80 V peak-to-peak: the top cursor is at 40 V and the bottom cursor at about −40 V .

2. Peak = half of peak-to-peak: \(80/2 = 40\,V_{pk}\) .

3. RMS (sine): \(V_{RMS} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(40\,\text{V}) = 0.707 \times 40 = 28.28\,V_{RMS}\) .

Output: 80 Vpkpk = 40 Vpk = 28.28 VRMS (written on the paper at )

Example 10 — input (wall) voltage with the cursors

Given (screen at ): same 5 ms/div time base, readout 60.02 Hz; cursor readout: cursor 1 = 172 V, cursor 2 = −176 V, Δ = 348 V. Find: \(V_{pk}\), \(V_{pp}\), \(V_{RMS}\) and compare with the outlet values from section 2.

1. Peak: the top cursor reads 172 V; the instructor reads it as "about 170 volts" peak — "exactly what I expect to get out of my electrical outlets."

2. Peak-to-peak: Δ = \(172 - (-176) = 348\,\text{V}\); the instructor says "look at the delta, 340 volts peak to peak", adding that he should have "backed this off a little bit" .

3. RMS: \(0.707 \times 170 = 120\,V_{RMS}\).

Rounding, not an error. The cursor readouts were 79.2 V and 348 V peak-to-peak; the instructor rounds them to 80 V and 340 V (and the peaks to 40 V and 170 V). Neither the probe attenuation (1×/10×) nor the input's volts/division is visible or mentioned.

6.4 Turns ratio from the two scope readings

This is a transformer calculation, but it is how the instructor interprets the two measurements, so it is worked here.

Example 11 — step-down and 4.25 : 1

Given: input 170 Vpk, output 40 Vpk (both peak — same type of voltage). Find: step-up or step-down, and the turns ratio.

1. Less voltage comes out than goes in (40 V < 170 V), so it is a step-down transformer .

2. Turns ratio = voltage ratio: \(\dfrac{N_P}{N_S} = \dfrac{V_P}{V_S} = \dfrac{170\,V_{pk}}{40\,V_{pk}} = 4.25\) .

3. The primary has more turns than the secondary, because the side with fewer turns has the lower voltage.

4. Check with the other voltage types: p-p \(340/80 = 4.25\); RMS \(120/28.28 = 4.24\). (With the unrounded cursor values, \(348/79.2 = 4.39\).)

Other content in this video (outside this topic)

Transformer theory is taught in the rest of the video. Only an outline is given here; the parts needed to interpret scope readings are worked in sections 5 and 6.

  • AC is "already changing", which a transformer can make use of; transformers are why Tesla's AC won — they make a voltage higher or lower .
  • Transformers only work with AC. Schematic symbol: two coupled coils; input side = primary, output side = secondary .
  • The coils are not electrically connected; they share a magnetic field. Physical picture: a ferrous core with primary and secondary windings ; an air core also works if the coils are close .
  • Small-signal and larger transformers shown; primaries and secondaries are electrically isolated ; AC in the primary makes the core's field alternate, inducing AC in the secondary .
  • Summary: a transformer works for AC, not DC; it is just coils of wire, and that simplicity is why AC won for power distribution .
  • Turns ratio \(N_P : N_S\) (e.g. 10 turns : 10 turns = 1 : 1) ; only the ratio matters, not the absolute number of turns .
  • \(N_P/N_S = V_P/V_S\). 1 : 1 example: 10 Vpk in → 10 Vpk out ; keep the voltage type (peak, p-p or RMS) the same on both sides .
  • Step-up (increases \(V_S\): \(V_P \lt V_S\)) and step-down (decreases \(V_S\): \(V_P \gt V_S\)) transformers.
  • Step-up example: 1 : 4 with 120 VRMS in → \(120 \times 4 = 480\,V_{RMS}\) out ; the instructor's shortcut: multiply when the bigger number is on the secondary, divide when it is on the primary . A step-down transformer can be flipped and used as a step-up .
  • Step-down example: 2 : 1 with 60 Vpkpk in → \(60/2 = 30\,V_{pkpk}\) out .
  • Q&A: distribution lines run at thousands of volts because transmitting at higher voltage is more efficient; a practical limit is core saturation . At-home oscilloscopes for 211/311 mentioned .

Assumptions & limitations

  • Sine-wave assumption. \(V_{RMS} = V_{pk}/\sqrt2 \approx 0.707\,V_{pk}\) and \(V_{pp} = 2V_{pk}\) hold for a pure sine wave centred on 0 V (no DC offset). Other shapes have different RMS factors, and a waveform with a DC offset does not have \(V_{pp} = 2V_{pk}\) measured from 0 V. Everything in this video is a sine wave.
  • 0.707 is rounded. \(1/\sqrt2 = 0.70711\ldots\); 0.707 × 170 gives 120.19 V instead of 120.21 V — fine for lab work.
  • RMS Ohm's law and power (\(V_{RMS} = I_{RMS}R\), \(P_{avg} = I_{RMS}V_{RMS}\)) as used here apply to resistive loads. With capacitors and inductors, voltage and current are out of phase and later lessons add more to the power calculation.
  • Same voltage type on both sides of any ratio or conversion: peak with peak, p-p with p-p, RMS with RMS.
  • Reading the scope screen. Divisions × volts/div gives volts only if the scope's probe setting matches the probe (1× or 10×); the cursor and automatic readouts depend on it too. Grid readings by eye are approximate (±0.1–0.2 div); cursors and automatic measurements are more precise.
  • Frequency readout needs at least about one full, triggered cycle on screen; with too fast a time base it reports "<2Hz" (seen at ).
  • Mains measurements are only safe within the scope's and probe's ratings (here "rated to safely do 300 V RMS"); 120 V RMS (170 V peak) is inside that rating.
  • Transformers pass only changing (AC) signals; a steady DC input gives at most a brief transient, then 0 V.
  • The turns ratio from measured voltages assumes an ideal transformer; real, unloaded transformers give a somewhat higher output than their rating.

Common mistakes

  • Dropping the qualifier. "30 V" is not an answer — say 30 V peak, 30 V p-p or 30 V RMS. The instructor insists on this every time (, , ).
  • Mixing peak and RMS, e.g. dividing 170 V peak by 28.28 V RMS (gives 6.0, not 4.25). Convert both readings to the same type first.
  • Using \(V_{pp}\) in place of \(V_{pk}\) in \(V_{RMS} = 0.707\,V_{pk}\): \(0.707 \times 80 = 56.6\,\text{V}\) is wrong for the 80 V p-p reading; halve it first (40 V peak → 28.28 V RMS).
  • Treating the average of a sine as its size. It is 0 V; that's why RMS exists.
  • Calling power "RMS power". It's average power, \(P_{avg} = I_{RMS}V_{RMS}\).
  • Counting divisions from the wrong place. Peak is from the centre (0 V) line to the top; peak-to-peak from the bottom to the top. Multiply by the V/div setting shown on the screen — it changes when you rescale (2 V → 1 V → 20 V per division in this video).
  • Wrong time base. Too slow (500 μs/div for 10 kHz) crams 50 cycles together; too fast (50 μs/div for 60 Hz) shows a nearly flat line and the frequency readout gives up. Aim for a few cycles on screen.
  • Measuring with only one lead. Voltage is between two points: the ground clip must be on the source's "−"/reference. With the probe disconnected the trace disappears (shown at ).
  • Expecting a transformer to pass DC. A 9 V battery on the primary gives a flat 0 V line on the secondary.
  • Connecting a scope to mains without checking ratings or with exposed conductors. Check the rating (here 300 V RMS) and never touch exposed mains connections.

Recap & formula sheet

  • DC is a constant line on a v–t graph; AC is a sine wave centred on 0 V with a positive and a negative alternation each cycle (current reverses).
  • Three magnitudes for the same sine: peak (0 V to top), peak-to-peak (bottom to top = 2 × peak), RMS (= 0.707 × peak). US outlet: 170 V peak = 340 V p-p = 120 V RMS.
  • RMS = square → mean → root; it is the AC value giving the same heating as that much DC, so Ohm's law and \(P_{avg} = I_{RMS}V_{RMS}\) work with RMS values.
  • Frequency = cycles per second (Hz); period \(T = 1/f\). Mains is 60 Hz (16.7 ms).
  • Scope: probe tip on the signal, ground clip on the reference; the grid is a voltage-vs-time graph; value = divisions × (V/div or time/div). Use the frequency readout and Δ cursors for quick measurements, and pick a time base that shows a few cycles.
  • Transformers pass AC, not DC (9 V battery → 0 V out). Wall transformer: 340 V p-p (170 V peak) in, 80 V p-p (40 V peak, 28.28 V RMS) out → 4.25 : 1 step-down.

Amplitude conversions (sine wave, no DC offset)

\[ V_{pp} = 2V_{pk} \qquad V_{RMS} = \frac{V_{pk}}{\sqrt2} \approx 0.707\,V_{pk} \qquad V_{pk} = \sqrt2\,V_{RMS} \approx 1.414\,V_{RMS} \]
\(V_{pk}\)
peak voltage (0 V to maximum), V
\(V_{pp}\)
peak-to-peak voltage (minimum to maximum), V
\(V_{RMS}\)
root-mean-square voltage, V

RMS from its definition (square → mean → root)

\[ V_{RMS} = \sqrt{\overline{v^2}} = \sqrt{\tfrac12 V_{pk}^2}\ \ (\text{sine}) \qquad I_{RMS} = 0.707\,I_{pk} \]

Ohm's law and power with RMS values (resistive load)

\[ V_{RMS} = I_{RMS}R \qquad P_{avg} = I_{RMS}V_{RMS} \;\big(= I_{RMS}^2R = V_{RMS}^2/R\big) \]
\(I_{RMS}\)
RMS current, A
\(R\)
resistance, Ω
\(P_{avg}\)
average power, W

Period and frequency

\[ f = \frac{1}{T} \qquad T = \frac{1}{f} \]
\(f\)
frequency, Hz = cycles/s
\(T\)
period (time of one cycle), s

Reading the oscilloscope grid

\[ V = (\text{vertical divisions}) \times \frac{\text{V}}{\text{div}} \qquad T = (\text{horizontal divisions per cycle}) \times \frac{\text{time}}{\text{div}} \qquad \text{cycles on screen} = \frac{10\,\text{div} \times (\text{time/div})}{T} \]

Transformer (used to interpret the scope readings)

\[ \frac{N_P}{N_S} = \frac{V_P}{V_S} \]
\(N_P, N_S\)
primary / secondary turns
\(V_P, V_S\)
primary / secondary voltages, in the same type (peak, p-p or RMS)

Key numbers: US outlet 120 V RMS ≈ 170 V peak ≈ 340 V p-p, 60 Hz (\(T \approx 16.7\,\text{ms}\)); 10 kHz → 100 μs; 1 kHz → 1 ms; \(1/\sqrt2 = 0.7071\); scope rating in the video: 300 V RMS (≈ 424 V peak).
