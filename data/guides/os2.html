Overview

This lecture/lab (LectLab 26, 24 Nov 2020) is mostly about diodes driven by AC (the series diode clipper / half-wave rectifier). Along the way it contains a lot of practical measurement skill, which is what this guide covers in full: turning a frequency into a period (\(T = 1/f\)) to set an LTspice run of exactly five 60 Hz cycles; reading voltage differences with cursors (diode drop, ripple peak-to-peak); and a real oscilloscope demo — connecting the probes and ground clip, changing volts/division from 500 mV/div to 2 V/div so the signal fits on the 8-division screen, counting divisions to read a 5 V peak, measuring the period with cursors (16.7 ms → 59.9 Hz ≈ 60 Hz) and comparing with the scope's own frequency readout, and finally measuring the diode's forward voltage as the difference between the input and output peaks (~600 mV on the scope vs 650 mV in LTspice).

Learning objectives

After this lesson you should be able to…

  • convert a frequency to a period (\(T = 1/f\)) and compute the time needed to display or simulate \(N\) cycles (\(N\cdot T\));
  • connect a scope probe and its ground clip correctly to a circuit driven by a function generator;
  • choose a volts/division setting so the whole waveform fits within the screen's vertical divisions, and explain why 500 mV/div is too small for a 5 V-peak signal;
  • read a peak voltage by counting divisions and multiplying by the V/div setting;
  • measure a period with two time cursors (Δt) and compute \(f = 1/\Delta t\), using the unit trick (1/ms → kHz, 1/s → Hz, 1/μs → MHz);
  • explain why the instructor double-checks the scope's automatic frequency reading;
  • measure a voltage difference with two voltage (horizontal) cursors — e.g. a ripple peak-to-peak, or a diode's forward voltage as \(V_{in,pk} - V_{out,pk}\) — and compare it with simulation.

Prerequisites

Peak, peak-to-peak, RMS
For a sine centred on 0 V, \(V_{pp} = 2V_p\) and \(V_{rms} = V_p/\sqrt{2}\). See AC vs DC, Vp/Vpp/Vrms.
Period and frequency
\(T\) is the time for one cycle; \(f = 1/T\) is cycles per second (Hz).
Oscilloscope screen
A grid of divisions: horizontally time (s/div), vertically voltage (V/div). Reading = number of divisions × setting. First demo in os1; RC measurement in cl4.
Voltage is always relative to a reference
Every measured voltage is "across" two points; on a scope both channels are measured relative to the ground clip / common ground .
Diode forward voltage \(V_F\)
A conducting silicon diode drops roughly 0.7 V, so the voltage after it is about 0.7 V lower than before it. (The theory is outside this topic; it is the thing being measured in §2 and §7.)

1. From frequency to period: simulating exactly five 60 Hz cycles

What is being simulated. The LTspice circuit is the series diode clipper from the first half of the lecture: a sine source V1, a 1N4001 diode D1 and a 1 kΩ load R1, with net labels vin (source side) and vout (across the resistor). In the source's "advanced" settings the instructor chose a SINE function with DC offset 0 (centred on 0 V), amplitude 5 V and frequency 60 Hz . In LTspice the "amplitude" of a sine is its peak value, so this is 5 Vpk = 10 Vpp; the schematic shows it as SINE(0 5 60).

The question : how long should the transient simulation run so that the plot shows five cycles ? To answer it you need the length of one cycle — the period.

\[ T = \frac{1}{f} \qquad\qquad t_{\text{sim}} = N\cdot T = \frac{N}{f} \]
\(T\)
period — time for one cycle (s)
\(f\)
frequency (Hz = cycles per second)
\(N\)
number of cycles wanted on screen (here 5)
\(t_{\text{sim}}\)
simulation stop time (s)

\(T = 1/f\) holds for any periodic signal; multiplying by \(N\) gives the time occupied by \(N\) whole cycles — useful both for a simulator's stop time and for choosing a scope time base.

Example 1 — Stop time for five cycles of 60 Hz

Given: \(f = 60\) Hz (source setting). Find: \(T\) and the stop time for \(N = 5\) cycles.

1. Period from frequency : \(T = \dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{60\,\text{Hz}}\). The instructor typed this out as "T = 1/60Hz", "5 Cycles = 5 * 1/60Hz", "Sim Time = 5 Cycles" .

2. Units : "my base unit is hertz, my answer is going to be in seconds" — since \(1\,\text{Hz} = 1/\text{s}\), \(1/\text{Hz} = \text{s}\). On the calculator \(1/60 = 0.01666\ldots\,\text{s}\) (≈ 16.7 ms).

3. Five cycles : \(t_{\text{sim}} = 5 \times 0.016667\,\text{s} = 0.083333\ldots\,\text{s}\) (the calculator showed 0.0833333…).

4. Enter it : Stop time = 0.0833, giving the directive .tran 0.0833; he remarks that the exact number of digits you type does not matter.

5. Check the result : count the cycles of V(vin) on the plot — one, two, three, four, five — centred on 0 V, going up to +5 V and down to −5 V .

\(T = 1/60\,\text{s} \approx 0.0167\,\text{s} = 16.7\,\text{ms}\); five cycles take \(5T \approx 0.0833\,\text{s} = 83.3\,\text{ms}\).

Why the rounding doesn't matter. 0.0833 s is 33 μs shorter than \(5T = 0.083333\) s, i.e. 0.04 % of the run — invisible on the plot. If you round down a lot (say 0.08 s), the last cycle is cut off (0.08 s = 4.8 cycles); if you round up, you see a little of a sixth cycle. Either way \(N = t_{\text{sim}}\cdot f\) tells you how many cycles you will get.

The same arithmetic sets an oscilloscope's time base: a 10-division screen showing \(N\) cycles needs \(\text{s/div} \approx N T / 10\). For 3 cycles of 60 Hz: \(3 \times 16.7\,\text{ms}/10 = 5\) ms/div — exactly the setting used later in the lab (§5).

2. Measuring voltage differences with cursors in LTspice

With the simulation run, V(vout) is pulsating DC: during each positive alternation the resistor gets a hump of voltage, and during the negative alternation the output flat-lines at 0 V because the diode is not conducting . The question the instructor then asks is quantitative: how big is the diode's drop in the real (simulated) diode compared with the 0.7 V "second approximation"? He answers it with cursors.

How the LTspice cursors were used : zoom in on one cycle; left-click the trace name V(vin) to attach cursor 1 and drag it to the peak; click V(vin) again to get cursor 2, then click V(vout) to move cursor 2 onto the output trace . Place the two cursors at the same instant ("right on top of each other") and read the box labelled Diff (Cursor2 − Cursor1) .

Example 2 — Diode drop as the difference of two peaks (LTspice)

Given (cursor window, ):

CursorTraceHorz (time)Vert (voltage)
1V(vout)37.519978 ms4.3455332 V
2V(vin)37.519978 ms4.995573 V
Diff (2 − 1)0 s650.03977 mV

1. Both cursors are at the same time (Horz diff = 0 s), at the top of a cycle, so the difference compares the two peaks at the same instant.

2. KVL around the loop: \(v_{in} = v_D + v_{out}\), so the diode voltage is \(v_D = v_{in} - v_{out}\).

3. Substitute: \(v_D = 4.995573\,\text{V} - 4.3455332\,\text{V} = 0.6500398\,\text{V} \approx 650\,\text{mV}\).

\(V_F \approx 0.65\) V at the peak — "pretty close to 0.7 volts" .

Checking the cursor time. 37.52 ms is where a peak should be: peaks of \(\sin(2\pi\cdot 60\,t)\) occur at \(t = (k + \tfrac14)T\). With \(k = 2\): \(2.25 \times 16.667\,\text{ms} = 37.5\,\text{ms}\). The input peak reads 4.9956 V rather than 5 V only because the cursor sits a few μs from the exact crest.

Example 3 — Where does conduction start? (LTspice)

Idea : put a cursor on V(vout) exactly where the blue output begins to rise above 0 V — that is the moment the diode starts conducting — and a second cursor on V(vin) at the same instant . The absolute value of \(v_{in}\) there is the input voltage needed to start conduction .

1. Read cursor 2 itself (not the Diff): \(v_{in} \approx 395\,\text{mV}\) when the output starts to move .

2. The instructor notes the cursor is "just a tad bit high" — V(vout) is already 25 mV rather than 0 V — so the true onset is slightly below 395 mV.

The simulated diode begins conducting at about \(v_{in} \approx 0.4\) V — "a bit sooner than the forward voltage" ; \(V_F\) is "not an absolute value" , but the output is flat until \(v_{in}\) gets into the ballpark of it.

Consistency check. The rising zero crossing before 33.54 ms is at \(2T = 33.333\) ms. 0.2099 ms later the input is \(5\sin(2\pi\cdot 60 \times 0.2099\,\text{ms}) = 5\sin(0.0791\,\text{rad}) = 0.395\) V — exactly cursor 2's reading. Note also that the Diff value (370 mV) is not the answer here: this measurement needs an absolute reading of one trace, while Example 2 needed a difference of two traces.

The point of the comparison: the second approximation (\(V_F = 0.7\) V constant) is "more than adequate", LTspice gets you closer, and the bench measurement in §7 confirms it .

3. Ripple peak-to-peak with cursors (smoothing capacitor, LTspice)

As an "easter egg" the instructor adds a capacitor C1 in parallel with R1 . (Why a capacitor smooths the output is rectifier theory, outlined in Other content; here we only need what the waveform looks like.) The output now charges up near each input peak and then sags slowly until the next peak — a sawtooth-like ripple riding on a DC level. The larger C, the smaller the ripple:

1. Peak-to-peak ripple = maximum − minimum: \(V_{r(pp)} = 4.1882051 - 4.1484136 = 0.0397915\,\text{V}\).

2. The Diff box shows −39.79 mV only because cursor 2 was placed on the lower point; the size of the ripple is the magnitude.

\(V_{r(pp)} \approx 40\) mV. The instructor calls this well within his accepted tolerance for almost any power supply he would build : for a load drawing about 4 mA, 2200 μF is more than adequate. Two 1000 μF capacitors in parallel would do essentially the same job .

Two more things you can read off this plot. (1) The ripple repeats once per input cycle — the peaks are about 16.7 ms apart — because a half-wave rectifier recharges the capacitor once per 60 Hz cycle, so the ripple frequency is 60 Hz. (2) LTspice's cursor box also printed "Freq: 718.03922 Hz". That is just \(1/|\Delta t| = 1/1.3926816\,\text{ms}\); it would be a frequency only if the two cursors were one period apart. Here they bracket the short charging interval, so 718 Hz means nothing physical — the same caution the instructor applies to automatic readouts in §6.

4. Lab: building the circuit and connecting the probes

The bench version is the same circuit as the simulation: a 1N4001 diode in series with a 1 kΩ resistor, driven by a function generator set to 60 Hz, 5 V peak, with the scope watching the input on one channel and the output on the other .

  1. Diode : a 1N4001, leads bent and pushed into the breadboard (the instructor points out where its cathode band is before inserting it).
  2. Resistor : a 1 kΩ (brown–black–red: 1, 0, ×10² = 1000 Ω). It shares a row of five connected holes with one diode lead, so the two are in series .
  3. AC source : the function generator's leads go across the series pair — red to the free diode lead, black to the bottom of the resistor .
  4. Channel 1 probe : clipped onto the input (so it doesn't move), and the oscilloscope ground clip goes to the bottom of the resistor — the same node as the generator's black lead, i.e. the circuit's 0 V reference.
  5. Channel 2 probe : on \(v_{out}\), the diode–resistor junction (its ground clip on the same bottom node).
  6. Summary : AC applied from the red and black leads; input on the yellow channel (CH1), output on the blue channel (CH2). Generator set to 60 Hz and 5 V peak ; the scope's screen is shown through its PC software (GW Instek / OpenWave) .

Settings visible later in the CH2 menu : coupling DC, input impedance 1 MΩ, invert off, bandwidth full, probe attenuation 10X. The trigger was on CH1, rising edge, level −200 mV, DC .

Why the ground clip placement matters. A scope probe measures the voltage of its tip relative to its ground clip, and on a bench scope every ground clip is tied to the scope chassis and mains earth. Most function generators' black output is earthed too. So all the ground clips must go to the same node as the generator's black lead (here the bottom of the resistor). Clipping a ground lead onto any other node would connect that node to earth and short out part of the circuit. With one common ground, CH1 shows \(v_{in}\) and CH2 shows \(v_{out}\), exactly as the voltages were defined on paper (both relative to the bottom node) .

DC coupling shows the true 0 V level of each trace, which matters here because the output is all-positive pulsating DC. 10X means the probe divides the signal by 10 and the scope multiplies its readings back by 10, so the probe setting on the scope must match the switch on the probe.

5. Choosing volts/division and time/division; counting divisions

When the scope software comes up, CH1 is at 500 mV/div and the time base is 5 μs/div, and the trace looks like a nearly flat line with a slight upward slope . Two settings have to change.

Horizontal (time/div)

At 5 μs/div the whole 10-division screen spans only 50 μs, about 0.3 % of one 16.7 ms period, so you only see a tiny, almost straight piece of the sine. The instructor changes the time base to 2 ms/div , so the screen spans 20 ms ≈ 1.2 cycles ("I have one cycle showing"). Then he goes to 5 ms/div, about three cycles (50 ms) .

Wording check. At the instructor says "we're zoomed out too much on the horizontal time base". The frame shows 5 μs/div, which is much too fast: the view is zoomed in on a 50 μs slice, and the fix is to increase the time per division, which is what he then does (to 2 ms/div). The action is right; read "zoomed out" as "set to the wrong scale".

Vertical (volts/div): the signal must fit in the screen's divisions

At 500 mV/div, "if we were going up 5 volts that means we're going up 10 divisions and I don't have 10 divisions of space" . This screen has 8 vertical divisions, 4 above and 4 below the centre line. A sine centred on the centre line can therefore have a peak of at most 4 divisions.

Example 5 — Amplitude by counting divisions

Given: CH1 at 2 V/div; the trace rises about 2½ divisions above the centre line and falls 2½ below it.

1. "If each division is 2 volts: two, four, and a half makes five volts" : \(V_p = 2.5\,\text{div} \times 2\,\tfrac{\text{V}}{\text{div}} = 5\,\text{V}\).

2. It goes down by the same amount, so the waveform is centred on 0 V: \(-5\) V at the trough.

3. (Follows from the reading:) \(V_{pp} = 5\,\text{div} \times 2\,\tfrac{\text{V}}{\text{div}} = 10\,\text{V}\).

\(v_{in}\) is 5 V peak (10 V peak-to-peak), matching the function-generator setting.

Choosing the best V/div. Bigger on screen means more precise readings: if you can judge the trace to about ±0.1 div, then at 2 V/div that is ±0.2 V, at 5 V/div it would be ±0.5 V. So pick the smallest V/div that still keeps the whole waveform on screen. For 5 V peak on a ±4-division screen that is 1.25 V/div. Scopes usually step 1–2–5, so 2 V/div is the right choice.

Leftover cursor readouts. The cursor box at the top of the screen still showed numbers from an earlier session (680 mV and −1.02 V at 500 mV/div became 2.72 V and −4.08 V at 2 V/div ). Horizontal cursors stay at fixed screen positions, so their voltage values scale with V/div (×4 here). They are not measurements of this signal until you move them onto it.

6. Measuring period and frequency: auto readout vs cursors vs Hz units

Method A: the scope's automatic frequency readout

The "F" box in the corner of the screen showed the frequency computed by the scope (e.g. 60.0122 Hz at ; it changes slightly from one update to the next: 59.98–60.03 Hz in the frames). The instructor accepts it: "the frequency absolutely is 60 hertz as being calculated by my oscilloscope" , and then explains why he checks it by hand anyway.

He tends not to fully trust the frequency the oscilloscope calculates, because "how do we know we're talking about the same frequency" . For a pure sine wave it is easy, but for more complicated waveforms that are a function of multiple frequencies he relies on that number less and less, and makes a manual measurement instead.

Method B: two time cursors and \(f = 1/T\)

He turns on the horizontal-position (time) cursors, which appear as vertical lines. One goes at a point where the cycle repeats and the other at the same point one cycle later . The scope prints each cursor's time and the difference Δ.

Example 6 — Frequency from the cursor period

Given (readout at ): cursor 1 at 200 μs, cursor 2 at 16.9 ms, Δ = 16.7 ms (both cursors at 80.0 mV on the trace, so ΔV = 0.00 V: they really are at equivalent points).

1. The time between two equivalent points one cycle apart is the period: \(T = 16.9\,\text{ms} - 0.2\,\text{ms} = 16.7\,\text{ms}\).

2. Frequency is the reciprocal : "one over the frequency equals the period, one over the period equals the frequency", so \(f = \dfrac{1}{T} = \dfrac{1}{16.7\,\text{ms}}\).

3. The instructor's unit trick : if you divide by milliseconds the answer is in kilohertz; by seconds, hertz; by microseconds, megahertz. So \(1 \div 16.7 = 0.05988\), and this is in kHz: \(f = 0.0599\,\text{kHz}\) ("call that .06 kilohertz") .

4. Shift the decimal point three places to convert kHz to Hz: \(0.0599\,\text{kHz} = 59.9\,\text{Hz} \approx 60\,\text{Hz}\) .

\(T = 16.7\) ms, so \(f = 1/T \approx 59.9\) Hz, essentially 60 Hz.

\[ f = \frac{1}{T}: \quad \frac{1}{\text{s}} = \text{Hz}, \qquad \frac{1}{\text{ms}} = \text{kHz}, \qquad \frac{1}{\mu\text{s}} = \text{MHz} \]
\(T\)
period measured between two equivalent points one cycle apart
\(f\)
frequency; the unit follows from the unit of \(T\) because \(1/10^{-3} = 10^{3}\) and \(1/10^{-6} = 10^{6}\)

Method C: let the cursors display in hertz

Finally he changes the cursors' horizontal unit (the "H Unit" choices are s, Hz, % and °) from seconds to Hz . The scope then does \(1/\Delta t\) itself: Δ = 59.88 Hz , which "might as well be 60 hertz".

MethodResultWhen
Scope's automatic frequency (F box)≈ 60.01 Hz

"Three different ways of all coming up with the same answer": it must be 60 Hz .

Reading the Hz-mode cursor box correctly. In Hz mode the box showed cursor 1 = 5.000 kHz, cursor 2 = 59.17 Hz and Δ = 59.88 Hz . The individual values are just \(1/t\) of each cursor's time from the trigger point (1/200 μs = 5 kHz, 1/16.9 ms = 59.17 Hz) and mean nothing on their own. Only the Δ line, \(1/(t_2 - t_1)\), is the signal's frequency. It is not 5000 − 59.17.

Why 59.88 and not 60.00? The cursors move in small steps and the period was read as 16.7 ms instead of 16.667 ms: a 0.2 % reading error gives a 0.2 % frequency error. Measuring across several cycles (e.g. 3 periods = 50.0 ms) spreads the same placement error over a longer time and improves the result.

7. Measuring the diode drop as a difference of peaks, and comparing with LTspice

Turning on the output channel. With CH2 turned on, the blue trace is the half-wave "pulsating DC" output, "exactly what LTspice told us" . Both channels are at 2 V/div and the time base is 5 ms/div .

Cleaning up the trace with averaging : in the Acquire menu, mode Average, 32 waveforms (choices 2 to 256) . The noise ("fuzz") on the traces shrinks because random noise averages out over 32 sweeps while the repeating signal does not.

Voltage cursors. After zooming in (time base 2 ms/div), he decides that the cursors that measure voltage, drawn as horizontal lines, are more useful here than time cursors . He puts one on the input peak and one on the output peak: "the difference between the peak to peak should be my forward voltage" .

Example 7 — Diode forward voltage on the bench

Given (cursor readout): cursor 1 = 5.00 V (input peak), cursor 2 = 4.44 V at first , adjusted to 4.40 V .

1. KVL at the peak instant: \(V_F = v_{in,pk} - v_{out,pk}\).

2. \(V_F = 5.00\,\text{V} - 4.40\,\text{V} = 0.60\,\text{V}\). The scope prints Δ600 mV. (With the first placement it read 5.00 − 4.44 = 0.56 V, which shows how much the reading depends on where you put the cursor.)

3. Compare: LTspice gave 0.65 V ; the second approximation assumes 0.7 V.

\(V_F \approx 600\) mV on the scope, versus 650 mV in LTspice: the simulation and the scope are "for the most part agreeing with each other" .

Note that the input peak itself reads exactly 5.00 V. That confirms the generator's 5 V-peak setting independently of the division count in §5. At 2 V/div the cursor moves in steps of about 40 mV (4.44 → 4.40 V), so the bench value is only good to a few tens of mV. Assumptions & limitations

  • \(T = 1/f\) is exact for any periodic signal; the hard part on a real scope is placing the cursors at two corresponding points one cycle apart (same level, same slope direction).
  • Cursor readings are only as good as the cursor placement. The instructor notes his LTspice cursor was placed "a tad bit high" ; the ~400 mV conduction-start value is therefore approximate.
  • The scope's automatic frequency is reliable for a clean single-frequency waveform such as this pure sine; for waveforms made of several frequencies (or noisy/poorly triggered signals) it may report something other than what you mean — hence the manual cursor check .
  • Counting divisions is limited by the grid resolution (about ±0.1–0.2 div by eye). Choose volts/div so the waveform is as large as possible while still fitting on the screen; then the same reading error is a smaller fraction of the signal.
  • Difference-of-peaks forward voltage (\(V_F \approx V_{in,pk} - V_{out,pk}\)) assumes both channels share the same ground and are measured at the same instant (the peak). It gives \(V_F\) at the peak current only — \(V_F\) varies with current (≈0.4 V when conduction starts, ≈0.6–0.65 V at the 4.3 mA peak).
  • Averaging (acquire mode average, 32) cleans random noise only on a stable, well-triggered repetitive signal; it distorts signals that change from one sweep to the next.
  • The scope ground clip is tied to earth ground through the scope; it must go to the circuit's reference node (here the bottom of the resistor / function-generator black lead), never to a point at a different potential.

Common mistakes

  • Waveform runs off the screen. At 500 mV/div a 5 V peak needs 10 divisions above centre but the screen only has 4 above centre (8 total) . Fix: increase V/div until the whole peak-to-peak fits (2 V/div here).
  • Forgetting to multiply divisions by the scale. 2.5 divisions is not 2.5 V; at 2 V/div it is 5 V.
  • Unit slips in \(f = 1/T\). 1/16.7 ms is 0.0599 kHz = 59.9 Hz, not 0.0599 Hz. Use the instructor's trick: 1/ms → kHz, 1/s → Hz, 1/μs → MHz .
  • Measuring "one period" between points that are not equivalent (e.g. a rising zero crossing to a falling zero crossing gives \(T/2\)). Put both cursors on the same feature (e.g. the point where the cycle repeats) one cycle apart.
  • Blindly trusting the auto-measure. Cross-check with cursors and with what the source is set to; the instructor got the same 60 Hz three ways .
  • Too few or too many cycles in the window. A time base so slow that the trace looks like a flat line (as at ) hides the waveform; aim for a few (≈2–3) cycles.
  • Simulation time for N cycles must be \(N\cdot T\) in seconds: \(5 \times (1/60) = 0.0833\) s, not \(5/60\) ms or \(5 \times 60\).

Recap & formula sheet

  • A 60 Hz signal has \(T = 1/60\) s ≈ 16.7 ms; five cycles take ≈ 0.0833 s (used as the LTspice stop time).
  • Pick V/div so the full waveform fits on the 8 vertical divisions: 5 V peak needs ≥ 1.25 V/div → the instructor used 2 V/div (2.5 div per peak).
  • Amplitude = divisions × V/div: 2.5 div × 2 V/div = 5 V peak.
  • Period by cursors: Δt = 16.7 ms → \(f = 1/16.7\,\text{ms} = 0.0599\,\text{kHz} \approx 60\) Hz; the scope's own readout said 60 Hz and the Hz-unit cursor said 59.88 Hz — three methods, same answer.
  • Voltage differences by cursors: diode drop at the peak ≈ 650 mV (LTspice) vs ≈ 600 mV (scope); conduction starts at \(v_{in}\) ≈ 400 mV in both; ripple with 2200 μF ≈ 40 mV peak-to-peak (LTspice).
