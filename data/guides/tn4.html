Overview

The first 24 minutes of this lecture cover the maximum power transfer theorem. The instructor first admits something earlier lectures skipped: a real battery or bench power supply is not an ideal voltage source. It behaves like an ideal source \(V\) in series with an internal resistance \(r_{int}\), which is the same shape as a Thévenin equivalent circuit. The theorem says the load receives the most power when \(R_L = r_{int}\). He shows this with one source (20 V, \(r_{int} = 10\,\Omega\)) and three loads: 10 Ω gives 10 W, 5 Ω gives 8.89 W and 30 Ω gives 7.5 W.

Learning objectives

After this lesson you should be able to:

  • Explain the difference between an ideal constant-voltage source and a real battery or bench power supply, and draw the real one as \(V\) in series with an internal resistance \(r_{int}\).
  • Say why a real source "looks like" a Thévenin equivalent circuit.
  • Identify the source and the load in a circuit, and give examples where maximum power transfer matters (speaker, antenna, microwave oven) and where it usually doesn't (digital logic, embedded systems).
  • State the maximum power transfer theorem: the load gets maximum power when \(R_L = r_{int}\).
  • For a source \(V\) with \(r_{int}\) and a load \(R_L\), find \(I_{RL}\), \(V_{RL}\) and \(P_{RL} = I_{RL}V_{RL}\) using series resistance, Ohm's law and KVL.
  • Reproduce the three examples (20 V, \(r_{int} = 10\,\Omega\); \(R_L\) = 10, 5, 30 Ω) and explain why more current (5 Ω) or more voltage (30 Ω) still gives less load power.
  • (Added) Use \(P_L = \dfrac{V_{TH}^2 R_L}{(R_{TH}+R_L)^2}\) and \(P_{max} = \dfrac{V_{TH}^2}{4R_{TH}}\), and explain why the efficiency at maximum power is only 50 %.

Prerequisites

Ohm's law
\(V = IR\). The instructor uses it to get each resistor's voltage from the loop current, e.g. \(1\,\text{A}\times 10\,\Omega = 10\,\text{V}\).
Series resistors and the single-loop current
In a single loop the same current flows through every element, and series resistances add. For a source \(V\) with \(r_{int}\) and \(R_L\): \(I = V/(r_{int}+R_L)\).
Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL)
The voltage drops around a closed loop sum to the source voltage (the algebraic sum is zero). The drop across the load is therefore \(V_{RL} = V - V_{r_{int}}\).
Electric power
The power absorbed by a resistor is \(P = IV\), which can also be written \(P = I^2R = V^2/R\). Unit: watt (W) = V·A.
Thévenin equivalent (tn1, tn2)
Any linear two-terminal network can be replaced by a voltage source \(V_{TH}\) in series with a resistance \(R_{TH}\). The real-source model in this lesson has exactly this form.
Norton equivalent and source transformation (tn3, st1)
A current source \(I_N\) in parallel with \(R_N = R_{TH}\) is equivalent to the Thévenin form, with \(V_{TH} = I_N R_N\). This is only needed for the added material at the end.

1. Ideal vs. real-world voltage sources: internal resistance

The instructor writes the title "Maximum Power Transfer Theorem" and describes the goal: transfer the most power possible from the source to the load . First, though, he points out something the course has not acknowledged yet.

Ideal sources are an idealization

So far every voltage source has been drawn as an ideal constant-voltage source. If it says 10 V, we expect 10 V from it all the time. That is not what a battery or the bench-top power supply used in lab really is.

Real sources have internal resistance

The thing that most resembles a constant voltage source in real life "actually has a bit of internal resistance." On the paper () he draws, next to the ideal battery, a dotted box labelled "Realworld Battery or Bench PS". It contains a battery \(V\) in series with a resistor, which he labels \(r_{int}\) for internal resistance .

He says this is how we model a battery or power supply. It applies to a bench supply, the power-supply unit in a PC, and "everything you've ever plugged into the wall" .

It looks like a Thévenin equivalent circuit . The instructor points out that the model looks like a Thévenin equivalent because it is a voltage source in series with a resistance. "So many things end up looking like this"; what changes is the context you use it in .

This is why the theorem is usually stated with Thévenin quantities. Any linear source network, however complicated, can be reduced to \(V_{TH}\) in series with \(R_{TH}\) (tn1). In this lecture \(V\) plays the role of \(V_{TH}\) and \(r_{int}\) plays the role of \(R_{TH}\). So "\(R_L = r_{int}\)" and "\(R_L = R_{TH}\)" are the same statement.

The two-9-volt-battery story

On a separate sheet () he sketches two 9 V batteries snapped into each other, one flipped around. He mentions it as something he did as a child. Connected like that, they get warm, even hot . The reason is internal resistance .

The internal resistance was not put there by the battery's designer. It is "an artifact of existence", a result of the battery's chemistry. It exists inherently in batteries and in bench-top supplies, so we have to live with it .

Why they heat up: snapping two 9 V batteries together joins each battery's + snap to the other's − snap. The two sources are then in series, aiding each other, around a closed loop. The only resistance in that loop is the two internal resistances, so the current is large and limited only by \(r_{int}\). The power \(I^2 r_{int}\) is dissipated inside the batteries as heat. An ideal source with \(r_{int} = 0\) would predict an infinite current, which is another sign that the ideal model is incomplete. (Please don't try this: shorted batteries can leak or rupture.)

2. Source and load: when do we care about power?

Sometimes we are not interested in the circuit's voltages and currents. All we care about is getting the most power from our source to our load . In red, below the first drawing, the instructor draws the model (). Dotted boxes separate the Source (\(V\) and \(r_{int}\)) from the Load (\(R_L\)), and the two meet at a pair of terminals.

When maximum power transfer matters

  • Speaker system . The speaker is the load. The source is the amplifier or receiver driving it . On the paper he sketches a block labelled "Amp, Audio Amplifier" connected to a speaker symbol labelled "Speaker" ().
  • Antennas transmitting radio frequencies: we want to transfer as much power as possible .
  • Microwave oven: the part emitting the microwaves should get the most power from the source .

When it usually doesn't matter

In embedded systems (the "262 type material"), FPGAs and digital logic, we typically don't care about transferring the most power. We care that the voltages and currents are correct . The cases where power matters are audio, microwave appliances, and antenna transmitters and receivers .

He adds that the internal resistance is "always" present, in every bench-top supply or battery. That makes this a good place in the course to bring it up .

3. The maximum power transfer theorem

"Very simply" : maximum power is transferred from the source to the load when \(r_{int} = R_L\) . That is, the load gets the most power when its resistance equals the source's internal resistance. He writes the statement in red at the top of a new page ().

\[ P_{RL} \text{ is maximum when } R_L = r_{int} \quad (\text{equivalently } R_L = R_{TH}) \]
\(R_L\)
load resistance, Ω
\(r_{int}\)
internal resistance of the source (its Thévenin resistance \(R_{TH}\)), Ω
\(P_{RL}\)
power dissipated by the load, W

This applies to a fixed source (fixed \(V\) and \(r_{int}\)) whose load you can choose. It tells you which load value takes the most power out of that source.

The written statement on the paper reads "Maximum is transferred from the source to the load when \(r_{int} = R_L\)". The word "power" is missing from the page but is spoken at .

How he demonstrates it

Instead of proving it, he uses "three very simple examples" . In all three the source stays the same: the same bench-top supply or battery, 20 V with \(r_{int} = 10\,\Omega\). Only the load changes, and each time we calculate the power dissipated by the load . The method is the same each time:

  1. Total resistance: \(R_T = r_{int} + R_L\) (series).
  2. Loop current: \(I_{RL} = V/R_T\) (Ohm's law).
  3. Load voltage: \(V_{RL} = I_{RL}R_L\) (Ohm's law), or \(V - I_{RL}r_{int}\) (KVL).
  4. Load power: \(P_{RL} = I_{RL}V_{RL}\).

A general formula and a short calculus proof are in section 9, "Beyond the video".

4. Example 1: matched load, \(R_L = r_{int} = 10\,\Omega\)

Example 1 — 20 V, \(r_{int} = 10\,\Omega\), \(R_L = 10\,\Omega\)

Given (paper at ): a 20 V battery, + on top. \(r_{int} = 10\,\Omega\) in series, then terminals, then the load \(R_L = 10\,\Omega\).

Goal: the power dissipated by the load, \(P_{RL} = I_{RL}V_{RL}\) .

1. Total resistance (series): \(R_T = 10\,\Omega + 10\,\Omega = 20\,\Omega\) .

2. Current (Ohm's law): \(I_{RL} = \dfrac{20\,\text{V}}{20\,\Omega} = 1\,\text{A}\) .

3. Voltages: \(V_{r_{int}} = 1\,\text{A}\times 10\,\Omega = 10\,\text{V}\), and the load also drops 10 V. By KVL, \(20 - 10 = 10\,\text{V}\); by Ohm's law, \(1\,\text{A}\times 10\,\Omega = 10\,\text{V}\) .

4. Power: \(P_{RL} = I_{RL}V_{RL} = 1\,\text{A}\times 10\,\text{V}\) .

\(P_{RL} = 10\,\text{W}\) . This is the maximum the theorem promises.

At the instructor says "10 volts times 10 ohms". He catches it himself at ("surprised nobody caught me"): power is volts × amps, \(10\,\text{V}\times 1\,\text{A}\). The final value on the paper, 10 W, is correct.

Where the rest of the power goes: \(r_{int}\) also dissipates \(1\,\text{A}\times 10\,\text{V} = 10\,\text{W}\). The ideal source therefore delivers \(20\,\text{V}\times 1\,\text{A} = 20\,\text{W}\), and exactly half of it (50 %) reaches the load.

5. Example 2: smaller load, \(R_L = 5\,\Omega\)

Example 2 — same source, \(R_L = 5\,\Omega\)

Given: the same 20 V source with \(r_{int} = 10\,\Omega\). The load is lowered to \(R_L = 5\,\Omega\) .

1. Total resistance: \(10 + 5 = 15\,\Omega\) .

2. Current: \(I_{RL} = \dfrac{20\,\text{V}}{15\,\Omega} = 1.33\,\text{A}\) . "Oh cool, current's gone up", but the theorem says the power should go down .

3. The voltage drops have shifted. Across the internal resistance: \(V_{r_{int}} = 1.33\,\text{A}\times 10\,\Omega = 13.33\,\text{V}\) . Because more voltage is lost inside the source, less is left for the load.

4. Load voltage (Ohm's law; 20 V − 13.33 V by KVL gives the same): \(V_{RL} = 1.33\,\text{A}\times 5\,\Omega = 6.66\ldots\,\text{V}\) ("6.66 repeating") .

5. Power: \(P_{RL} = 6.666\,\text{V}\times 1.3333\,\text{A}\) .

\(P_{RL} = 8.89\,\text{W}\), less than the 10 W of the matched case .

Exact check: \(I = \tfrac{4}{3}\,\text{A}\), \(V_{RL} = \tfrac{20}{3}\,\text{V}\), so \(P_{RL} = \tfrac{80}{9} = 8.889\,\text{W}\).

Even though the current went up, the load power went down. More power is now wasted by the source . In his words, he wants his power used by the load, not by the battery's internal resistance, which he doesn't care about "but I have to live with".

On the paper, \(V_{r_{int}}\) was first written "13.33 A". He notices at ("did I write amps") and corrects it to 13.33 V at .

Rounding. If you multiply the rounded numbers literally, \(1.33\times 6.66 = 8.86\,\text{W}\). The board's 8.89 W is the correct value, using the repeating decimals \(1.3\overline{3}\) and \(6.6\overline{6}\). Keep extra digits (or fractions) until the last step.

Legibility. On the page, "\(R_L =\)" is followed by a blacked-out character and then "5 Ω". The value used throughout, both spoken and in the arithmetic, is 5 Ω. The blot appears to be an overwritten character.

Power split for this load: \(r_{int}\) dissipates \(1.33\,\text{A}\times 13.33\,\text{V} = 17.78\,\text{W}\). The source delivers \(20\times 1.33 = 26.67\,\text{W}\) in total, and only \(8.89/26.67 = 33\,\%\) reaches the load.

6. Example 3: larger load, \(R_L = 30\,\Omega\)

Example 3 — same source, \(R_L = 30\,\Omega\)

Given: the same 20 V source with 10 Ω internal resistance. The load is now made larger, \(R_L = 30\,\Omega\). "Maybe" more load resistance gives more power? "I highly doubt it", because that's not what the theorem says .

1. Total resistance: \(10 + 30 = 40\,\Omega\) .

2. Current: \(I_{RL} = \dfrac{20\,\text{V}}{40\,\Omega} = 0.5\,\text{A}\) ("half an amp") .

3. Load voltage: \(V_{RL} = 0.5\,\text{A}\times 30\,\Omega = 15\,\text{V}\) . The load voltage is now higher than in Example 1.

4. Power: \(P_{RL} = 15\,\text{V}\times 0.5\,\text{A}\) .

\(P_{RL} = 7.5\,\text{W}\), again less than 10 W .

Power split: \(r_{int}\) drops \(0.5 \times 10 = 5\,\text{V}\) and dissipates \(2.5\,\text{W}\). The source delivers \(20\times 0.5 = 10\,\text{W}\), so 75 % reaches the load. The efficiency is higher than in Example 1, but the load power is lower, because the total power the source delivers has dropped.

7. Comparing the three loads

The conclusion : when the internal resistance of the source and the load resistance are equal, the load gets the maximum power. The full page at shows all three circuits side by side. Collected in one table:

Example\(R_L\)\(R_T = r_{int}+R_L\)\(I_{RL}\)\(V_{r_{int}}\)\(V_{RL}\)\(P_{RL}\) (board)
2 5 Ω15 Ω1.33 A13.33 V6.66 V8.89 W
1 10 Ω \(= r_{int}\)20 Ω1 A10 V10 V10 W (maximum)
3 30 Ω40 Ω0.5 A5 V*15 V7.5 W

*Not written on the board: \(0.5\,\text{A}\times 10\,\Omega\). (Rows are sorted by \(R_L\); the video order was 10, 5, 30 Ω.) All values were checked in Python.

  • Lowering \(R_L\) raises the current but lowers \(V_{RL}\), because more voltage is lost inside the source. Net effect: less load power.
  • Raising \(R_L\) raises \(V_{RL}\) but lowers the current. Net effect: less load power.
  • The best compromise is in between, at \(R_L = r_{int}\).

Reading the curve: it rises steeply for \(R_L < r_{int}\) and falls slowly for \(R_L > r_{int}\). Pairs of loads with \(R_{L1}R_{L2} = r_{int}^2\) give the same power. For example, 5 Ω and 20 Ω both give 8.89 W, since \(5\times 20 = 10^2\). So a mismatch "by a factor of 2" costs about 11 % of the maximum power in either direction.

8. Real sources, and when this matters

  • A real battery's internal resistance is much smaller than the 10 Ω used in the examples, but it is not negligible . On the whiteboard we usually treat sources as ideal, but there is always a small amount of resistance to contend with.
  • More complicated sources such as an audio amplifier have a lot going on internally: a sound wave is turned into a signal and applied to a load. Still, you can model one as a changing voltage with a series (internal) resistance, delivering power to a speaker that has its own resistance . The circuit is drawn in the figure in section 2.
  • It doesn't matter all the time . As computer engineers we usually care more that the bits are correct and the digital logic and its interface circuits work as expected than about wasted power on the source side.

"This matters sometimes, depending on your application" . It also shows a "sad fact of reality": internal resistance is something we have to live with. He still covers the topic because it is one "we should cover" .

He asks for questions . A student says it makes sense ("that's a good answer"), and he moves on to mesh analysis .

Maximum power vs. maximum efficiency. These are different goals. Matching (\(R_L = r_{int}\)) gives the most load power, but only 50 % efficiency: half the power is burned in the source.

  • Signal and RF systems (amplifier → speaker, transmitter → antenna, a weak sensor or antenna feeding a receiver) often match, because the available power is limited and you want as much of it as possible. In RF, matching also prevents reflections on transmission lines.
  • Power systems (the electric grid, battery-powered devices, a bench supply feeding a circuit) deliberately do not match. They keep \(R_L \gg r_{int}\) so the efficiency is high and the load voltage stays close to \(V\). Running a power plant "matched" would waste half of everything it generates.
  • Practical speaker amplifiers have an output resistance much smaller than the speaker's impedance (a high "damping factor"). The textbook matching picture is a simplification for that case.

9. Beyond the video: the general formula, the Thévenin view, and efficiency

The instructor demonstrates the theorem numerically. Here is the general result that the three examples illustrate.

Load power for any \(R_L\)

Source \(V_{TH}\) with \(R_{TH}\) (here \(V = 20\,\text{V}\), \(r_{int} = 10\,\Omega\)), load \(R_L\):

1. Series loop (Ohm's law): \(I = \dfrac{V_{TH}}{R_{TH}+R_L}\).

2. Load voltage (voltage divider): \(V_{RL} = V_{TH}\dfrac{R_L}{R_{TH}+R_L}\).

3. Load power: \(P_{RL} = I\,V_{RL} = I^2R_L\).

\[ P_{RL} = \frac{V_{TH}^2\,R_L}{(R_{TH}+R_L)^2} \]
\(V_{TH}\)
Thévenin (open-circuit) voltage of the source, V
\(R_{TH}\)
Thévenin (internal) resistance of the source, Ω
\(R_L\)
load resistance, Ω
\(P_{RL}\)
load power, W

Check: \(V_{TH} = 20\), \(R_{TH} = 10\): \(R_L = 5\) gives \(400\cdot 5/225 = 8.89\,\text{W}\); \(R_L = 10\) gives \(4000/400 = 10\,\text{W}\); \(R_L = 30\) gives \(12000/1600 = 7.5\,\text{W}\). These match the board.

Why the maximum is at \(R_L = R_{TH}\)

Differentiate with respect to \(R_L\) (quotient rule), holding \(V_{TH}\) and \(R_{TH}\) fixed:

\[ \frac{dP_{RL}}{dR_L} = V_{TH}^2\,\frac{(R_{TH}+R_L)^2 - 2R_L(R_{TH}+R_L)}{(R_{TH}+R_L)^4} = V_{TH}^2\,\frac{R_{TH}-R_L}{(R_{TH}+R_L)^3} \]

The derivative is positive for \(R_L < R_{TH}\), zero at \(R_L = R_{TH}\) and negative for \(R_L > R_{TH}\). So \(R_L = R_{TH}\) is the maximum. Substituting it in:

\[ P_{max} = \frac{V_{TH}^2}{4R_{TH}} \qquad \left(= \frac{I_N^2 R_N}{4} \text{ in Norton form}\right) \]
\(P_{max}\)
largest power the source can deliver to any resistive load, W
\(I_N,\ R_N\)
Norton current (A) and resistance (Ω), with \(R_N = R_{TH}\)

For the lecture's source: \(P_{max} = 20^2/(4\times 10) = 10\,\text{W}\), which is Example 1.

When the load is matched, \(V_{RL} = V_{TH}/2\) and \(I = V_{TH}/(2R_{TH})\). In Example 1 these are 10 V and 1 A.

Efficiency

\[ \eta = \frac{P_{RL}}{P_{total}} = \frac{I^2R_L}{I^2(R_{TH}+R_L)} = \frac{R_L}{R_{TH}+R_L} \]
\(\eta\)
efficiency, no unit (often written in %)
\(P_{total}\)
power delivered by the ideal source \(V_{TH}\), equal to \(V_{TH}I\), W

At maximum power the efficiency is exactly 50 %. Efficiency keeps rising as \(R_L\) grows, while load power falls.

Applying it to a whole network

To find the load that takes the most power from a complicated circuit: remove the load, find \(V_{TH}\) and \(R_{TH}\) at its terminals as in tn1/tn2, then set \(R_L = R_{TH}\) and compute \(P_{max} = V_{TH}^2/(4R_{TH})\). You can also start from a Norton equivalent (tn3) or use source transformation (st1) to switch between the forms.

Assumptions & limitations

  • Linear, resistive circuits (DC). The theorem as taught assumes the source can be modeled as \(V\) (or \(V_{TH}\)) in series with a resistance \(r_{int}\) (or \(R_{TH}\)), and that the load is a resistor.
  • The source is fixed; the load is chosen. The theorem answers "which \(R_L\) takes the most power from this source?" It does not say to increase \(r_{int}\) to match a given load. If you can change the source, a smaller \(r_{int}\) always gives more load power (added).
  • Maximum power ≠ maximum efficiency (added). At \(R_L = r_{int}\) only 50 % of the source's power reaches the load. Systems that care about efficiency keep \(R_L \gg r_{int}\).
  • Ideal source (\(r_{int} = 0\)) (added). There is no maximum: load power \(V^2/R_L\) grows without limit as \(R_L \to 0\). Real sources always have some \(r_{int}\), which the instructor stresses.
  • Real internal resistance is small and not constant (added). A battery's \(r_{int}\) is much smaller than 10 Ω (stated in the video) and changes with charge level, temperature and age. Bench supplies regulate their output so they behave nearly ideally within their current limit.
  • AC sources (added). With capacitors or inductors the condition becomes "load impedance = complex conjugate of the source impedance". That is beyond this course section.

Common mistakes

"More current means more load power." Example 2 has more current (1.33 A vs. 1 A), yet less load power (8.89 W vs. 10 W). Much more voltage is lost inside the source. Always compute \(P_{RL} = I_{RL}V_{RL}\) with both factors.

"More load voltage means more load power." Example 3 has more load voltage (15 V vs. 10 V), yet less power (7.5 W), because the current halved.

Multiplying volts by ohms. Power is \(V\times I\), not \(V\times R\). The instructor slips on this aloud at and fixes it at . A units check catches it: \(\text{V}\cdot\Omega\) is not a watt; \(\text{V}\cdot\text{A}\) is.

Wrong unit on a voltage drop. \(1.33\,\text{A}\times 10\,\Omega\) is 13.33 V, not "13.33 A". This happened on the board and was corrected at .

Rounding too early. \(1.33\times 6.66 = 8.86\), but the true answer is 8.89 W. Keep fractions (\(4/3\), \(20/3\)) or several digits until the end.

Using only \(R_L\) to find the current. The current is \(V/(r_{int}+R_L)\), not \(V/R_L\). Forgetting \(r_{int}\) is exactly the "ideal source" mistake this lecture warns about.

Matching to the wrong resistance (added). For a multi-resistor source network, \(R_L\) must equal the network's Thévenin resistance seen from the load terminals, not some single resistor in it.

Expecting high efficiency at maximum power (added). At the matched point half the power is wasted in the source (50 %).

Recap & formula sheet

  • Real batteries and power supplies are an ideal source \(V\) in series with an internal resistance \(r_{int}\). This has the same form as a Thévenin equivalent.
  • Maximum power transfer theorem: the load receives maximum power when \(R_L = r_{int}\) (\(= R_{TH}\)).
  • Lecture source, 20 V with \(r_{int} = 10\,\Omega\): \(R_L = 5\,\Omega\) → 8.89 W; \(R_L = 10\,\Omega\) → 10 W (max); \(R_L = 30\,\Omega\) → 7.5 W.
  • It matters for audio, antennas and microwave ovens. Digital and embedded designers usually care more about correct voltages and currents.
  • (Added) At maximum power the efficiency is only 50 %. Power systems avoid matching.

Loop current: \(I_{RL} = \dfrac{V}{r_{int}+R_L}\) (A)

Load voltage: \(V_{RL} = I_{RL}R_L = V - I_{RL}r_{int}\) (V)

Load power: \(P_{RL} = I_{RL}V_{RL} = I_{RL}^2R_L\) (W)

Theorem: \(P_{RL}\) is maximum when \(R_L = r_{int} = R_{TH}\)

(Added) General load power: \(P_{RL} = \dfrac{V_{TH}^2R_L}{(R_{TH}+R_L)^2}\) (W)

(Added) Maximum power: \(P_{max} = \dfrac{V_{TH}^2}{4R_{TH}} = \dfrac{I_N^2R_N}{4}\) (W)

(Added) Efficiency: \(\eta = \dfrac{R_L}{R_{TH}+R_L}\); \(\eta = 50\,\%\) at \(R_L = R_{TH}\)

Variables: \(V\) or \(V_{TH}\) source voltage (V); \(r_{int}\) or \(R_{TH}\) internal/Thévenin resistance (Ω); \(R_L\) load resistance (Ω); \(I_{RL}\) load current (A); \(V_{RL}\) load voltage (V); \(P_{RL}\) load power (W).
