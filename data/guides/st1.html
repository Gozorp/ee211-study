Learning objectives

After this lesson you should be able to…

  • Recognise a voltage source in series with a resistor and a current source in parallel with a resistor as two interchangeable forms.
  • Convert a series \(V\)–\(R\) pair into a parallel \(I\)–\(R\) pair with \(I = V/R\), keeping the same \(R\).
  • Convert a parallel \(I\)–\(R\) pair into a series \(V\)–\(R\) pair with \(V = IR\), keeping the same \(R\).
  • Draw the new source with the correct orientation: the current-source arrow points toward the terminal that the voltage source's + side faces.
  • Use a source transformation to check that a Thévenin result (\(V_{TH}, R_{TH}\)) and a Norton result (\(I_N, R_N\)) agree, as the class did with 5 V / 666.6… Ω and 7.5 mA.
  • Explain why a source transformation is a local swap of one source–resistor pair, not a Thévenin or Norton equivalent of the whole circuit.

Prerequisites

Ohm's law
\(V = IR\). The voltage across a resistor equals the current through it times its resistance. Every source transformation is just this law used once.
Ideal voltage and current sources
An ideal voltage source keeps a fixed voltage between its terminals no matter what current flows. An ideal current source pushes a fixed current in the direction of its arrow no matter what voltage appears across it.
Series and parallel
Elements in series carry the same current. Elements in parallel share the same two nodes, so they have the same voltage.
Thévenin and Norton equivalent circuits
Any linear two-terminal network can be replaced, as seen from its two terminals, by \(V_{TH}\) in series with \(R_{TH}\) (Thévenin) or \(I_N\) in parallel with \(R_N\) (Norton), with \(R_N = R_{TH}\). See Thévenin & Norton (also tn2, tn3); the class had just done the lab on this.

1. Source transformation: the idea and the rule

Two interchangeable pairs

The instructor calls this topic "really easy, really straightforward" but potentially "incredibly valuable", depending on the situation . Take a voltage source in series with a resistor: you can turn it into a current source in parallel with a resistor . You can also go back the other way . Seen from the two output terminals, the two circuits are identical.

It looks like Thévenin/Norton, but it is not the same thing

The left circuit is exactly what you get at the end of a Thévenin analysis, and the right circuit is exactly what you get at the end of a Norton analysis . The instructor warns against mixing the ideas up:

"Don't get confused and think these are Thévenin or Norton equivalent circuits" . A Thévenin or Norton equivalent is the result of a procedure: you derive it for a whole network. These two drawings only have the same output as that procedure would give . "Let's just take this for what it is," he says: a voltage source in series with a resistor that you can swap for the other form whenever you see it .

Why the difference matters. In a source transformation you replace one source–resistor pair that is already sitting in your circuit. Everything else in the circuit stays exactly where it was, and nothing is "looked into" from a pair of terminals. A Thévenin or Norton equivalent replaces an entire network, which may hold many sources and resistors, by one source and one resistor as seen from two chosen terminals. You have to compute \(V_{TH}\) (or \(I_N\)) and \(R_{TH}\) from that whole network. A source transformation needs no analysis at all, just Ohm's law on the pair. That is why it can be repeated branch by branch inside a bigger circuit, which is exactly how it is used for Millman's theorem.

The conversion rule: Ohm's law, same R

"All we have to do is perform Ohm's law on the voltage source … with its given resistor" . Written on the page :

\[ I = \frac{V}{R} \qquad\text{(series } V\text{–}R \;\to\; \text{parallel } I\text{–}R\text{)} \]
\[ V = I\,R \qquad\text{(parallel } I\text{–}R \;\to\; \text{series } V\text{–}R\text{)} \]
\(V\)
voltage of the source in the series form, V
\(I\)
current of the source in the parallel form, A
\(R\)
the resistor of the pair, Ω. It stays the same: in series with the voltage source, in parallel with the current source.

These apply whenever a voltage source has a resistor in series with it (for the first rule), or a current source has a resistor in parallel with it (for the second). The resistance stays the same, which matches how the class found \(R_{TH}\) and \(R_N\) "the same exact way" in the lab .

Orientation: which way does the arrow point?

The instructor does not say the orientation rule out loud, but every drawing in this lecture follows it. In each case the battery's long plate (+) faces the top terminal, and the current-source arrow points up toward that same top terminal (general form at ; examples at and ; lab at ).

\[ \text{The arrow of } I \text{ points toward the terminal connected to the } + \text{ side of } V. \]

Why the arrow must point that way. Look at the open-circuit voltage \(v_{ab}\) in both forms. In the series form no current flows, so \(v_{ab} = +V\): terminal a is positive. In the parallel form all of \(I\) has to flow through \(R\). If the arrow pushes current into node a, that current goes down through \(R\) from a to b, so \(v_{ab} = +IR = +V\). That matches only if the arrow points toward a, the + side. Check the short-circuit current too: the series form drives \(V/R\) out of the + terminal through the short. The parallel form drives all of \(I\) out of the terminal the arrow points to. Same direction, same size.

If the voltage source is flipped (+ at the bottom), the arrow must point down.

2. Worked conversions

Example 1 — 10 V in series with 2 Ω → current source

Given: a 10 V source in series with a 2 Ω resistor . Goal: the equivalent current source in parallel with a resistor.

1. Keep the resistor: \(R = 2\ \Omega\). "R stays the same" .

2. Ohm's law for the source value: \(I = \dfrac{V}{R} = \dfrac{10\ \text{V}}{2\ \Omega} = 5\ \text{A}\) .

3. Draw the 5 A source in parallel with 2 Ω, arrow pointing toward the terminal that the + plate faced (up).

10 V in series with 2 Ω ⇔ 5 A in parallel with 2 Ω. "This is identical to this from these two points" .

Example 2 — 2 A in parallel with 3 Ω → voltage source

Given: a 2 A current source in parallel with a 3 Ω resistor . Goal: the equivalent voltage source in series with a resistor. Suppose that in some circuit this form "didn't suit us" and a voltage source with a series resistor would be more useful. The instructor says next class will show such a case .

1. Keep the resistor: the parallel 3 Ω "is going to be 3 Ω" , now in series.

2. Ohm's law for the source value: \(V = IR = (2\ \text{A})(3\ \Omega) = 6\ \text{V}\) .

3. Draw the 6 V source with its + side toward the terminal the arrow pointed at (top).

2 A in parallel with 3 Ω ⇔ 6 V in series with 3 Ω.

The conversion itself is easy. The skill is noticing when it helps, and that "you probably need a little more experience to see when doing this source transformation … is beneficial" .

Quick check that the two forms really match. Put any load \(R_L\) across the terminals. Series form: \(i_L = \dfrac{V}{R+R_L}\). Parallel form (current divider): \(i_L = I\,\dfrac{R}{R+R_L} = \dfrac{V}{R}\cdot\dfrac{R}{R+R_L} = \dfrac{V}{R+R_L}\). They are the same for every \(R_L\). For Example 1 with \(R_L = 3\ \Omega\): \(10/(2+3) = 2\ \text{A}\), and \(5 \cdot 2/(2+3) = 2\ \text{A}\).

3. Checking the Thévenin/Norton lab with a source transformation

The instructor asks the class (out loud or in chat) for the Thévenin and Norton equivalents they measured or calculated in the previous lab . A few fragments are read from chat ("3 volts and 3 milliamps, 7.5 volts") . Then one student gives a complete set :

Lab result (one student)Value
Thévenin voltage \(V_{TH}\)5 V
Thévenin resistance \(R_{TH}\)666.6̄ Ω (666.666… Ω)
Norton current \(I_N\)7.5 mA
Norton resistance \(R_N\)the same 666.6̄ Ω ("you should have gotten the same resistance")

The other chat values that were read aloud ("3 volts and 3 milliamps", "7.5 volts") come without context. It is not clear which quantity each one refers to, so they are not used here. The lab circuit itself (and its load resistor) is not shown in this video. See the Thévenin & Norton lessons.

Example 3 — Is the lab's Norton current consistent with its Thévenin values?

1. If the two equivalents describe the same circuit, a source transformation of the Thévenin form must give the Norton form. Same \(R\), and \(I = V/R\) .

2. \(R_N = R_{TH} = 666.6\bar{6}\ \Omega\). ✔ (the student had the same resistance)

3. \(I_N = \dfrac{V_{TH}}{R_{TH}} = \dfrac{5\ \text{V}}{666.66\ \Omega}\). On the calculator (): \(5 \div 666.66 = 0.0075000750\ldots\ \text{A}\).

4. Exactly: \(666.6\bar{6}\ \Omega = \tfrac{2000}{3}\ \Omega\), so \(I_N = 5 \cdot \tfrac{3}{2000} = 0.0075\ \text{A} = 7.5\ \text{mA} = 7500\ \mu\text{A}\) . (The tiny extra digits on the calculator come from typing 666.66 instead of the repeating decimal.)

\(5\ \text{V} / 666.6\bar{6}\ \Omega = 7.5\ \text{mA}\), the same as the measured Norton current. "Source transformation works" .

This gives "multiple verifications" for the lab . With the Thévenin equivalent and with the Norton equivalent you should have measured the same current through the load resistor and the same voltage across it . Now you also know the name of the conversion that links them: source transformation. It "will become more useful … next class when we go over Millman's theorem" (see Millman's theorem).

Reverse check. Going the other way, \(V = I_N R_N = 7.5\ \text{mA} \times 666.6\bar{6}\ \Omega = 5\ \text{V}\). The general relation \(V_{TH} = I_N R_{TH}\) (equivalently \(R_{TH} = V_{TH}/I_N\)) is just this source transformation applied to the two equivalents.

Wrap-up of the topic

The instructor sums up: this is "a pretty easy quick thing to cover". What you need is to know that you can do this, that you can "jump back and forth between these whenever it suits you best", and that the name is source transformation . Then the class moves on to the next topic .

  • Variable resistors: potentiometers ("pots") and multi-turn trimmers. Demo with a 5 kΩ pot and two ohmmeters: wiper to each end reads about 4.7 Ω / 4.7 kΩ at one extreme and about 2.3 kΩ / 2.3 kΩ near the centre. The outer pins read a fixed 4.87 kΩ .

  • Voltage-divider review: \(V_{out} = V_{in} R_2/(R_1+R_2)\). Example: 10 V with 100 Ω and 100 Ω gives 5 V.
  • The bridge as two dividers: \(V_D = V_S R_2/(R_1+R_2)\) and \(V_C = V_S R_S/(R_x+R_S)\).

  • Demo: \(R_1 = R_2 = 12\ \text{k}\Omega\), 5 V supply, substitution box as \(R_S\). The meter starts at 2.28 V; the box is adjusted until it reads 0 V at 20k + 2k + 300 Ω = 22.3 kΩ .
  • Unknown resistor color code red-red-orange = 22 kΩ ±5 % (±1100 Ω), so the 300 Ω difference is within tolerance.
  • History: the bridge dates from the 1800s, when only needle (current) meters existed.
  • Modern use: sensor circuits. A sensor (thermistor, CdS light cell) replaces \(R_x\) and a fixed known resistor replaces \(R_S\). The bridge is run unbalanced and the voltage difference is measured.
  • Thermistor demo (about 0.2 V, changing as the sensor is warmed by fingers). Light-sensor demo (0.38 V → −1.58 V when covered). A potentiometer is used to "null" the baseline .
  • Summary: bridges are a good starting point for sensor circuits and help cancel temperature effects, because only ratios matter.

Assumptions & limitations

  • The pair must really be a pair. The resistor must be in series with the voltage source (same current, nothing branching off between them), or in parallel with the current source (across the same two nodes). Otherwise \(I = V/R\) does not apply.
  • Same R both ways. Only the source value changes. \(R\) is never inverted or rescaled ().
  • Not a Thévenin/Norton analysis. It swaps one local pair. It does not reduce a whole network unless you combine it with series/parallel steps or a Thévenin/Norton procedure ().
  • Applies to linear resistive DC circuits as used in this course.

Equivalent only at the terminals. The two forms give the same voltage and current to whatever is connected outside. Inside, they differ. Example 1 with nothing connected: in the 10 V/2 Ω form no current flows and the resistor dissipates 0 W. In the 5 A/2 Ω form, 5 A flows through the resistor, which dissipates \(5^2 \cdot 2 = 50\ \text{W}\). Do not use the transformed circuit to find the current or power in that resistor.

Ideal sources need a resistor. A voltage source with no series resistance (\(R = 0\)) or a current source with no parallel resistance (\(R \to \infty\)) cannot be transformed. The formulas would give infinite current or infinite voltage.

Common mistakes

Changing R. Writing \(R_{new} = 1/R\) or computing a new resistance. Fix: the resistor value is identical in both forms. Only its connection changes: series ↔ parallel.

Arrow pointing the wrong way. The current-source arrow must point toward the terminal that the voltage source's + side faces (and the reverse when converting back). Check: find the open-circuit voltage of both forms. It must have the same sign.

Multiplying when you should divide. V→I uses \(I = V/R\) (10 V/2 Ω = 5 A), and I→V uses \(V = IR\) (2 A·3 Ω = 6 V). Check the units: V/Ω = A and A·Ω = V.

Keeping the resistor in the wrong place. After V→I the resistor goes in parallel with the current source, not in series. A current source with a resistor in series still delivers the same current, so that resistor would do nothing.

Calling it a "Thévenin equivalent". It has the same shape, but it is a different operation. The instructor explicitly warns about this ().

Unit slips in the lab check. 5 V / 666.67 Ω = 0.0075 A = 7.5 mA = 7500 μA. Writing "7.5 A" or "0.75 mA" is a factor-of-1000 or factor-of-10 error. Typing 666.66 instead of 666.666… gives 0.00750008 A, which is fine to round.

Recap & formula sheet

  • A voltage source in series with \(R\) ⇔ a current source in parallel with the same \(R\).
  • Convert with Ohm's law: \(I = V/R\) one way, \(V = IR\) the other.
  • Arrow of the current source → toward the + side of the voltage source.
  • Examples: 10 V + 2 Ω ⇔ 5 A ∥ 2 Ω; 2 A ∥ 3 Ω ⇔ 6 V + 3 Ω; lab: 5 V + 666.6̄ Ω ⇔ 7.5 mA ∥ 666.6̄ Ω.
  • Not a Thévenin/Norton equivalent of a whole circuit, only a local swap. It is the first step of Millman's theorem.

Series V–R → parallel I–R: \(I = \dfrac{V}{R}\), \(R\) unchanged.

Parallel I–R → series V–R: \(V = I R\), \(R\) unchanged.

Thévenin ↔ Norton link: \(I_N = \dfrac{V_{TH}}{R_{TH}}\), \(R_N = R_{TH}\).

Orientation: the arrow of \(I\) points toward the terminal on the + side of \(V\).

Variables: \(V\) in volts (V), \(I\) in amperes (A; 1 mA = \(10^{-3}\) A), \(R\) in ohms (Ω).
