Overview

This lecture (LectLab 28, 3 Dec 2020) is mainly about phasors and how inductors behave in AC. For this topic, the important part is the stretch where the instructor connects the rotating phasor to the everyday quantities you read off an oscilloscope: frequency \(f\) (Hz), period \(T\) (s) and angular frequency \(\omega\) (rad/s), with \(f = 1/T\) and \(\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi/T\). He then works the wall-outlet example — 340 V peak-to-peak = 170 V peak = 120 V RMS at 60 Hz — finds \(T \approx 0.0167\) s and writes \(v(t) = 170\sin(2\pi\cdot 60\cdot t)\). Later, the 90° voltage–current shift of an inductor is explained as a quarter of a period.

Learning objectives

After this lesson you should be able to…

  • state what one hertz means (one cycle per second) and convert between frequency and period with \(f = 1/T\);
  • compute angular frequency with \(\omega = 2\pi f\) or \(\omega = 2\pi/T\) and explain where the \(2\pi\) comes from (one cycle = one rotation = \(2\pi\) rad);
  • write the instantaneous-value equation \(v(t) = V_p\sin(2\pi f t)\) for a sinusoid of given peak and frequency, and evaluate it at a given time;
  • convert the outlet voltage between 340 Vpp, 170 Vpk and 120 Vrms and say which one appears in \(v(t)\);
  • find the period of 60 Hz (≈16.7 ms) and the number of cycles in a given time;
  • convert a time shift between two waveforms into a phase angle (a quarter period = 90°).

Prerequisites

AC waveform, peak / peak-to-peak / RMS
A sine wave swings symmetrically about 0 V. Peak \(V_p\) is centre-to-crest, peak-to-peak \(V_{pp}\) is trough-to-crest \(= 2V_p\), and RMS \(= V_p/\sqrt{2}\) for a sine (the "equivalent DC heating" value). Covered in AC vs DC, Vp/Vpp/Vrms.
Radians
An angle measure where a full circle is \(2\pi\) rad (= 360°); a quarter circle is \(\pi/2\) rad (= 90°). The instructor reviews this at .
Sine function
\(\sin\theta\) is 0 at \(\theta = 0\), 1 at \(\pi/2\), 0 at \(\pi\), −1 at \(3\pi/2\), and repeats every \(2\pi\).
Seconds and SI prefixes
1 ms = 10−3 s, 1 kHz = 103 Hz. Hz is 1/s, so \(1/\text{Hz} = \text{s}\).

1. Writing a sinusoid: \(v(t) = V_{pk}\sin(\omega t)\)

Where this comes from (context). In the first ten minutes the instructor draws a circle marked in degrees and radians and then one cycle of a sine wave with the same marks: start at 0, peak at \(\pi/2\), zero crossing at \(\pi\), minimum at \(3\pi/2\), and back to the start at \(2\pi\) . A vector (the phasor) rotating once around the circle traces exactly one cycle, and the projection of the vector on the y-axis is the instantaneous value of the sine . The key fact for measuring waveforms: one cycle = one full rotation = \(2\pi\) radians = 360°.

The equation : the angle of the rotating phasor at time \(t\) is \(\omega t\), where \(\omega\) is the radian frequency ("ω, omega, is our radian frequency" is written on the page). Taking the y-projection with the sine function gives the instantaneous value :

\[ v(t) = V_{pk}\,\sin(\omega t) \]
\(v(t)\)
instantaneous voltage at time \(t\) (V)
\(V_{pk}\)
peak voltage: the length of the phasor, the largest value the wave reaches (V)
\(\omega\)
radian (angular) frequency (rad/s)
\(t\)
time (s)

This form applies to a sinusoid centred on 0 V that is at zero and rising at \(t = 0\). The instructor points out that this way "we can keep frequency out of it until we want to include it" : \(\omega\) stands in for the frequency until a number is needed. The same equation works for current or any other sinusoidal quantity. The instructor prefers the generic name "instantaneous value" for that reason .

2. Frequency, period and angular frequency

Frequency in hertz is cycles per second: 1 Hz is one cycle per second, 2 Hz two cycles per second, 1 kHz a thousand cycles per second . This is the number used so far in LTspice and on the function generator.

Radian frequency : \(\omega\) is two pi, "the amount to do one complete rotation", times the frequency . Since frequency is one over the period, \(\omega\) can also be written with the period . The page at reads: "Frequency (Hz) Cycles per Second", "ω (omega) – dimensionless" (spelled "dimentionless" on the page), \(\omega = 2\pi f\), \(f = 1/T\), \(\omega = 2\pi/T\).

\[ f = \frac{1}{T} \qquad\qquad \omega = 2\pi f = \frac{2\pi}{T} \]
\(f\)
frequency, cycles per second (Hz)
\(T\)
period, seconds per cycle (s)
\(\omega\)
radian (angular) frequency: radians per second (rad/s)
\(2\pi\)
radians in one complete cycle (one rotation of the phasor)

Derivation, step by step.

  1. By definition, one cycle takes \(T\) seconds, so the number of cycles per second is \(f = 1/T\).
  2. Each cycle is one rotation of the phasor, which is \(2\pi\) radians.
  3. Radians per second = (radians per cycle) × (cycles per second): \(\omega = 2\pi \cdot f\).
  4. Substitute \(f = 1/T\): \(\omega = 2\pi/T\). In words, the phasor sweeps \(2\pi\) radians every period.

Combining with §1: \(v(t) = V_{pk}\sin(\omega t) = V_{pk}\sin(2\pi f t) = V_{pk}\sin\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi t}{T}\right)\). Check: at \(t = T\) the argument is \(2\pi\), exactly one cycle.

About "dimensionless". Radians are a ratio (arc length / radius), so strictly \(\omega\) has units of 1/s, the same as Hz. To keep the two apart, engineers always write \(\omega\) in rad/s and \(f\) in Hz. Mixing them up is a factor-of-\(2\pi\) error (≈6.28×).

\(f\)\(T = 1/f\)\(\omega = 2\pi f\)
1 Hz1 s6.283 rad/s
60 Hz16.67 ms377.0 rad/s
1 kHz1 ms6283 rad/s

On a scope you measure \(T\) (the horizontal distance of one cycle × time/div), then compute \(f\) and, if needed, \(\omega\). Measuring a period on a real scope is shown in the diode-lab scope demo.

3. Wall-outlet example: 340 Vpp, 170 Vpk, 120 Vrms at 60 Hz

To make it concrete, the instructor uses "a voltage that I think we're all really familiar with": what comes out of the outlet .

  • Amplitude, three ways : the outlet is 170 V peak, which can equally be given as 340 V peak-to-peak, 170 V peak, or 120 V RMS. Pick whichever is most useful for what you are solving . (Only "Vac = 170 Vpk" is written on the page.)
  • Frequency : 60 Hz, i.e. 60 cycles per second. If one second has elapsed, 60 cycles have occurred.

Example 1 — Period and instantaneous-value equation for the outlet

Given (page at ): Vac = 170 Vpk, fac = 60 Hz; sine drawn between +170 and −170.

1. Check the amplitude forms. Peak-to-peak is crest to trough, \(V_{pp} = 2V_{pk} = 2(170) = 340\) V. For a sine, \(V_{rms} = V_{pk}/\sqrt{2} = 170/1.4142 = 120.2 \approx 120\) V. (Going the other way, \(120\sqrt{2} = 169.7 \approx 170\) V.)

2. Period : \(T = \dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{60\,\text{Hz}} \approx 0.0167\,\text{s}\). The calculator shows 0.016666…7, written on the page as "T = 1/60Hz ≈ 0.0167 s" with an arrow spanning one cycle .

3. Instantaneous value : put the peak and the frequency into \(v(t) = V_{pk}\sin(2\pi f t)\): "our peak voltage which is 170 volts times sine of two pi times our frequency times t" .

4. (Not done in the video, for completeness:) \(\omega = 2\pi(60) = 377\) rad/s, so the same equation is \(v(t) = 170\sin(377\,t)\) V.

\(T \approx 0.0167\,\text{s} = 16.7\,\text{ms}\), and \(v(t) = 170\,\text{V}\,\sin(2\pi\cdot 60\cdot t)\). This gives "the instantaneous voltage at any point along our sine" for any moment in time .

The instructor admits that when he first saw this it was "a little hard to wrap my mind around" a rotating vector. In practice, though, what we usually care about is the relationship between two of these vectors, which is where phasors become useful .

Reading these numbers on a scope (do not try this on a wall outlet: use a transformer or the function generator). To display 340 Vpp within 8 vertical divisions you need at least 340/8 = 42.5 V/div, so 50 V/div. For about three cycles of 60 Hz on 10 divisions, 3 × 16.7 ms / 10 ≈ 5 ms/div. The scope would show ±3.4 div, and one period would span 16.7/5 = 3.33 div. Note that a scope shows peak and peak-to-peak directly. RMS you either compute (\(V_{pk}/\sqrt{2}\), for sines only) or read from the scope's RMS measurement. See Vp / Vpp / Vrms on the scope.

4. Reading instantaneous values off the sinusoid (Q&A)

A student asks to see the previous page again and whether we know "the values between the points" on the sine , i.e. the points between 0, \(\pi/2\), \(\pi\), … .

  • The instructor puts the outlet equation next to the sine-and-phasor page . If the sine is 170 V peak, then \(v(t) = 170\sin(2\pi\cdot 60\, t)\) "gives you all of these points provided you enter in what time you want to know" . The frame at shows the sine labelled 170 V at the peak, with marks at \(\pi/2\), \(\pi\), \(3\pi/2\), \(2\pi\).
  • That is why the projection on the y-axis is the instantaneous value. He draws the phasor at a few increasing angles . At 0 the projection is zero. As the phasor rotates, the projection grows, matching points further up the sine , until the phasor points straight up and the projection is the maximum, 170 V .
  • "Our sine function gives us the value along the y-axis that we care about", which is why the equation is the peak voltage times sine . The page ends with \(V(t) = V_{pk}\sin(\omega t)\) boxed .

Worked values (not computed in the video). For the outlet, \(T = 16.67\) ms and \(v(t) = 170\sin(2\pi\cdot 60\,t)\). Evaluate with the calculator in radian mode:

5. Phase shift as a fraction of the period (90° = a quarter cycle)

In the inductor part of the lecture (outlined below), the voltage and current sines are drawn with the voltage reaching its peak first, and the gap between the two peaks is labelled 90° . Later the instructor explains how to read that number off the time plot: the shift is "a quarter of a cycle". One quarter, two quarters, three quarters, four quarters make one cycle, and "a quarter of 360 degrees is 90 degrees" .

\[ \theta = 360^\circ \times \frac{\Delta t}{T} \qquad \left(\text{or } \theta = 2\pi\,\frac{\Delta t}{T}\ \text{rad} = \omega\,\Delta t\right) \]
\(\theta\)
phase difference between two waves of the same frequency (° or rad)
\(\Delta t\)
time between corresponding points (e.g. the two peaks) (s)
\(T\)
period of either wave (s)

Which one is "ahead"? Time increases to the right, so the wave whose peak occurs earlier (further left) leads. "The voltage occurs at a time earlier than the current", so voltage leads and current lags . On the phasor diagram the same 90° appears as the angle between the V and I arrows, and it stays the same whatever instant you draw it at .

Measuring it on a scope. Put two time cursors on the two peaks (or two rising zero crossings) to get \(\Delta t\). Measure \(T\) on either trace, then \(\theta = 360^\circ\cdot\Delta t/T\). For 60 Hz, a 90° shift is \(T/4 = 16.67/4 = 4.17\) ms. The GW Instek scope in the diode-lab demo can even display cursor positions in degrees (its "H Unit" menu offers s, Hz, % and °).

Assumptions & limitations

  • \(f = 1/T\) and \(\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi/T\) apply to any periodic waveform for \(f\) and \(T\); the angular frequency \(\omega\) is mainly used for sinusoids, where one cycle corresponds to \(2\pi\) radians of the rotating phasor.
  • \(v(t) = V_p \sin(\omega t)\) assumes the sine starts at zero and rising at \(t = 0\) (zero phase) and has no DC offset. A shifted wave needs a phase term, e.g. \(V_p\sin(\omega t + \phi)\) — the video only uses the zero-phase form.
  • \(V_{pp} = 2V_p\) holds for any waveform symmetric about 0 V (no DC offset). \(V_{rms} = V_p/\sqrt{2}\) holds only for pure sinusoids; squares, triangles, rectified waves have different ratios.
  • The outlet numbers (120 Vrms, 170 Vpk, 340 Vpp, 60 Hz) are nominal North-American values; real outlet voltage varies by a few percent, and \(120\sqrt{2} \approx 169.7\) V is rounded to 170 V.
  • Calling \(\omega\) "dimensionless" (as in the video) means radians are treated as a pure number; in practice \(\omega\) is quoted in rad/s so that \(\omega t\) comes out in radians.

Common mistakes

  • Using \(f\) where \(\omega\) belongs (or vice-versa). \(\sin(60t)\) is wrong for a 60 Hz wave; the argument must be \(2\pi\cdot 60\cdot t = 377t\). Check: the argument of the sine must advance by \(2\pi\) every period \(T\).
  • Calculator in degree mode. \(\omega t\) is in radians. Evaluating \(170\sin(377t)\) with the calculator set to degrees gives wrong instantaneous values.
  • Mixing up the three amplitude descriptions. 120 V is the RMS value, 170 V is the peak (centre line to crest), 340 V is peak-to-peak (trough to crest). Writing \(v(t) = 120\sin(\omega t)\) for the outlet is a classic error — the coefficient of the sine is always the peak value.
  • Applying \(V_{rms} = V_p/\sqrt{2}\) to non-sinusoids. It is only valid for sine waves.
  • Unit slips in \(T = 1/f\). \(1/60\,\text{Hz} = 0.0167\) s = 16.7 ms, not 16.7 s. Hz is 1/s, so dividing 1 by Hz gives seconds.
  • Reading "leads/lags" backwards on a time plot. Time increases to the right, so the waveform whose peak occurs further left (earlier) is the one that leads .

Recap & formula sheet

A sinusoid is completely described by its amplitude (given as peak, peak-to-peak or RMS) and its rate of repetition (given as period \(T\), frequency \(f\) in Hz, or angular frequency \(\omega\) in rad/s). One cycle = one full rotation of the phasor = \(2\pi\) radians = 360° = one period \(T\). For the wall outlet: 170 Vpk = 340 Vpp ≈ 120 Vrms, 60 Hz, \(T \approx 16.7\) ms, \(\omega \approx 377\) rad/s, so \(v(t) = 170\sin(2\pi\cdot 60\, t)\) V.
