10 Ω is \(R_2 \parallel (R_1 + R_4)\). You stopped one step early: \(R_3\) is still in series between that combination and terminal A.

You used \(R_3 + R_2 \parallel R_1\) and left out \(R_4\). After the source is shorted, \(R_1\) and \(R_4\) are joined by the short into one series path (20 Ω), and that path is in parallel with \(R_2\).

Adding all four resistors treats them as one series chain. With the source shorted, \(R_1 + R_4\) and \(R_2\) are two paths between the same two nodes, so they combine in parallel first.

\(R_3 + R_2 = 40\ \Omega\) is what you get if you remove the source (open) instead of replacing it with a wire. Then \(R_1\) and \(R_4\) dangle and drop out. To kill a voltage source, short it.

The full source voltage would appear at A–B only if no current flowed anywhere. Current does flow around the loop \(V_1\), \(R_1\), \(R_2\), \(R_4\); only \(R_3\), which leads to the open terminal, carries none.

15 V is the voltage of A measured from the source's − terminal. B is not at that point: \(R_4\) sits between them, and its 5 V drop puts B at 5 V. \(V_{th} = V_A - V_B\).

5 V is the drop across \(R_4\) (or across \(R_1\)). A and B sit at the two ends of \(R_2\), so \(V_{th}\) is the voltage across \(R_2\).

That divider (\(20 \times 20/30\)) leaves out \(R_4\). \(R_4\) is in the same loop and carries the same current, so the loop resistance is \(10 + 20 + 10 = 40\ \Omega\).

You treated \(R_3\) as being in parallel with \(R_2\). That would be true only if A and B were shorted together. With A–B open, \(R_3\) carries no current and does not affect the loop.

Look at \(R_3\). Its right end goes only to the open terminal A. Can any current flow through it? What does that tell you about the voltage at A compared with the junction above \(R_2\)?

Without \(R_3\), what is left is one loop: \(V_1\), \(R_1\), \(R_2\), \(R_4\). A sits at the top of \(R_2\) and B at its bottom, so \(V_{th}\) is the voltage across \(R_2\).

For \(R_{th}\), redraw with \(V_1\) replaced by a wire. Which two resistors does that wire connect end to end? Combine them, then with \(R_2\), then add \(R_3\).

Alternative: \(V_1\), \(R_1\) and \(R_4\) form one series branch between the two junctions, a 20 V source with 20 Ω. Try source transformations from there.

  1. "Derive the Thévenin equivalent" means: replace the whole circuit, as seen from A and B, by one source \(V_{th}\) in series with one resistor \(R_{th}\) (the exam writes the subscripts in lowercase; they mean the same as \(V_{TH}\), \(R_{TH}\)). Two separate procedures:

    • \(V_{th}\) = the open-circuit voltage \(V_{AB}\) with nothing connected to A–B.
    • \(R_{th}\) = the resistance seen from A–B with \(V_1\) replaced by a short.

    Name the top junction (where \(R_1\), \(R_2\), \(R_3\) meet) X and the bottom junction (where \(R_4\), \(R_2\) and the wire to B meet) Y.

  2. With A–B open, \(R_3\) connects X to terminal A and nothing else. Current needs a closed loop, and there is none through \(R_3\), so

    \[ I_{R_3} = 0\ \text{A} \;\Rightarrow\; V_{R_3} = I_{R_3} R_3 = 0\ \text{V} \;\Rightarrow\; V_A = V_X . \]

    Likewise B is wired straight to Y, so \(V_B = V_Y\). Therefore \(V_{th} = V_X - V_Y\), the voltage across \(R_2\).

    The remaining circuit is a single loop: \(V_1 \to R_1 \to R_2 \to R_4 \to V_1\). One loop means one current through three series resistors, so a voltage divider gives \(V_{R_2}\) directly.

    • Series: \(R_T = R_1 + R_2 + R_4\) (Ω). Ohm's law: \(I = V_1/R_T\) (A), \(V_R = IR\) (V).
    • Voltage divider: \(V_{R_2} = V_1 \dfrac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2 + R_4}\).
    • Parallel: \(R_a \parallel R_b = \dfrac{R_a R_b}{R_a + R_b}\).
    • Source transformation (alternative route): a source \(V\) in series with \(R\) equals a current source \(I = V/R\) in parallel with the same \(R\), arrow pointing toward the + side. Back again: \(V = IR\).

  4. Loop resistance and current (clockwise: out of the + terminal, through \(R_1\) to the right, down \(R_2\), left through \(R_4\) back to the − terminal):

    \[ R_T = 10 + 20 + 10 = 40\ \Omega, \qquad I = \frac{20\ \text{V}}{40\ \Omega} = 0.5\ \text{A} \]

    Drops (Ohm's law):

    \[ V_{R_1} = 0.5 \times 10 = 5\ \text{V}, \quad V_{R_2} = 0.5 \times 20 = 10\ \text{V}, \quad V_{R_4} = 0.5 \times 10 = 5\ \text{V}, \quad V_{R_3} = 0 \times 20 = 0\ \text{V} \]

    KVL check around the loop: \(20 - 5 - 10 - 5 = 0\) ✓.

    Node voltages, taking the source's − terminal as 0 V: X is at \(20 - 5 = 15\ \text{V}\), so A is also at 15 V (no drop in \(R_3\)). Y is at \(0 + 5 = 5\ \text{V}\) (the current flows from Y back through \(R_4\) to the 0 V terminal, so Y is 5 V higher), and B is also at 5 V.

    Result and polarity:

    \[ V_{th} = V_A - V_B = 15\ \text{V} - 5\ \text{V} = 10\ \text{V} \qquad \left(= 20 \times \frac{20}{10 + 20 + 10}\right) \]

    Positive, so A is the + terminal of the equivalent.

  5. Start again from the original circuit and replace \(V_1\) by a wire.

    Follow a test current entering at A: it passes through \(R_3\) to X without splitting, so \(R_3\) is in series with everything behind it. At X it splits: one part goes down \(R_2\) to Y; the other goes left through \(R_1\), down the short and right through \(R_4\) to Y. It rejoins at Y and leaves at B.

    \[ R_1 + R_4 = 10 + 10 = 20\ \Omega \quad (\text{series: same current, joined by the short}) \]
    \[ R_2 \parallel (R_1 + R_4) = \frac{20 \times 20}{20 + 20} = 10\ \Omega \quad (\text{both run from X to Y}) \]
    \[ R_{th} = R_3 + 10\ \Omega = 20 + 10 = 30\ \Omega \]

  6. This route gives both numbers at once. It works because, after each swap, the next element (first \(R_2\), then \(R_3\)) is in parallel or in series with the source you just created, so the network keeps collapsing into one source–resistor pair.

    (1) Combine the source branch. \(V_1\), \(R_1\) and \(R_4\) carry the same current, so they are one series branch from Y to X. The order of elements in a series branch does not change what the branch does at its ends, so slide \(R_4\) around next to \(R_1\): a 20 V source (+ toward X) in series with \(R_1 + R_4 = 20\ \Omega\).

    (2) Transform to a current source. \(I = V/R = 20\ \text{V} / 20\ \Omega = 1\ \text{A}\), in parallel with the same 20 Ω. The arrow points toward X, the side the + plate faced.

    (3) Combine the parallel resistors. Now the 1 A source, the 20 Ω and \(R_2 = 20\ \Omega\) all sit between X and Y: \(20 \parallel 20 = 10\ \Omega\).

    (4) Transform back. \(V = IR = 1\ \text{A} \times 10\ \Omega = 10\ \text{V}\), + toward X, in series with 10 Ω.

    (5) Add \(R_3\). It is in series with that 10 Ω on the way to A: \(10 + 20 = 30\ \Omega\). The circuit is now one 10 V source in series with 30 Ω between A and B. That is the Thévenin form, so \(V_{th} = 10\ \text{V}\) and \(R_{th} = 30\ \Omega\), matching steps 4 and 5.

    Each transformation is a local swap of one source–resistor pair, not a Thévenin step by itself. The result is the Thévenin equivalent only because, at the end, everything between A and B has been absorbed into one source and one resistor.

  7. Put \(V_{th} = 10\ \text{V}\) in series with \(R_{th} = 30\ \Omega\) between A and B, with the source's + side toward A (because \(V_A - V_B = +10\ \text{V}\)). The drawing is in the answer below.

\(R_{th} = R_3 + R_2 \parallel (R_1 + R_4) = 20 + 10 = 30\ \Omega\).

\(V_{th} = 10\ \text{V}\) (A positive with respect to B).

  • Two methods agree. The divider/series–parallel route and the source-transformation route both give 10 V and 30 Ω.
  • Short-circuit check (\(R_{th} = V_{OC}/I_{SC}\)). Short A to B: now \(R_3\) is in parallel with \(R_2\), \(20 \parallel 20 = 10\ \Omega\), and the source sees \(10 + 10 + 10 = 30\ \Omega\), so it supplies \(20/30 = 0.6667\ \text{A}\). Half of that goes through \(R_3\) (equal 20 Ω branches): \(I_{SC} = 0.3333\ \text{A}\) from A to B. \(V_{OC}/I_{SC} = 10/0.3333 = 30\ \Omega\) ✓.
  • Range check. \(V_{th}\) is less than the 20 V source, as it must be for a passive divider, and \(R_{th}\) is more than \(R_3\) alone, since \(R_3\) is in series with a positive resistance.
  • Load check. With \(R_L = 30\ \Omega\) across A–B the equivalent gives \(10 \times 30/(30 + 30) = 5\ \text{V}\). Nodal analysis of the full circuit with that load also gives 5 V.
  • Including \(R_3\) in the loop when finding \(V_{th}\) (treating it as parallel with \(R_2\)). With A–B open, \(R_3\) carries no current and has no effect on \(V_{th}\). It does matter for \(R_{th}\).
  • Reporting 15 V: the voltage of A measured from the source's − terminal. B is not at 0 V; \(R_4\) lifts it to 5 V.
  • Forgetting \(R_4\) because it is drawn in the bottom wire. Resistors in the return wire are in series with the source just like those in the top wire.
  • Removing the source (open) instead of shorting it when finding \(R_{th}\): that gives 40 Ω.
  • Reusing the \(V_{th}\) drawing (with its currents) to find \(R_{th}\). Redraw with the source shorted.
  • In the source transformation, pointing the current arrow away from the + side, or changing the resistor value. The resistor stays the same; only the source changes.

Approach for a similar problem.

  • Different values, same layout: \(V_{th} = V_1 \dfrac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2 + R_4}\) and \(R_{th} = R_3 + R_2 \parallel (R_1 + R_4)\).
  • A–B moved across \(R_2\) directly (no \(R_3\)): \(V_{th}\) is unchanged and \(R_{th} = R_2 \parallel (R_1 + R_4)\).
  • More resistors in the source branch: add every resistor in series with the source (top or bottom wire) before transforming or dividing.
  • Several sources or branches: transform each source–series-resistor pair into a current source in parallel; add parallel current sources (with their directions) and combine the parallel resistors; transform back at the end. This is the idea behind Millman's theorem.
  • Check first: mark any resistor that leads only to an open terminal. It carries no current, so drop it for \(V_{th}\) and put it back for \(R_{th}\).

How the concepts combine: Thévenin's theorem defines the two numbers you need. The direct route finds them separately (a divider for \(V_{th}\), series–parallel reduction with the source shorted for \(R_{th}\)). Source transformation is a second route: repeated local swaps shrink the whole network into one source and one resistor, which gives \(V_{th}\) and \(R_{th}\) together and checks the first route.
