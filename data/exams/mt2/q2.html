Capacitor rules are the reverse of the resistor rules: capacitors in parallel add; capacitors in series use \(1/C_T = 1/C_1 + 1/C_2 + \dots\). \(C_3\) and \(C_4\) are in series, and that branch is in parallel with \(C_5\).

The right-hand block (\(C_3\), \(C_4\), \(C_5\)) comes out equal to \(C_2\) and \(C_6\), so you then have three equal capacitors in series (\(C/n\)). \(C_1\) sits directly across the source.

For b), recall how the course states the three “opposition” properties: resistance opposes the flow of current, inductance opposes a change in current, and capacitance opposes a change in …?

  1. Part a) asks for the equivalent (total) capacitance \(C_T\): the single capacitor that, connected to \(V_2\), would store the same total charge as the whole network. You find it by reducing the network one series or parallel group at a time, using the capacitor combination rules.

    The source value (12 V) does not affect \(C_T\). Capacitance depends only on the capacitors and how they are connected, just as an equivalent resistance does not depend on the source.

    Part b) is a concept question about what capacitance does in a circuit.

  2. Name the nodes. The exam marks four junctions: top-left (TL) and bottom-left (BL), which are the source terminals, and top-right (TR) and bottom-right (BR). The wire between \(C_3\) and \(C_4\) is a fifth node, but nothing else connects to it.

    • \(C_3\) and \(C_4\): series. The point between them connects to nothing else, so every bit of charge that leaves \(C_3\)'s lower plate lands on \(C_4\)'s upper plate. Same charge, one after the other.
    • (\(C_3\)–\(C_4\)) and \(C_5\): parallel. Both branches connect TR to BR, so they have the same voltage across them.
    • \(C_2\), the right-hand block and \(C_6\): series. They form one path TL → \(C_2\) → TR → block → BR → \(C_6\) → BL. Nothing else branches off at TR or BR once the block is one capacitor.
    • \(C_1\) and that path: parallel. Both connect TL to BL, directly across \(V_2\).

    Every group is clearly series or parallel (there is no bridge), so you can reduce the network from the far end toward the source.

  3. Capacitors combine the opposite way to resistors:

    \[ \text{Parallel: } C_T = C_1 + C_2 + \dots + C_n \]
    \[ \text{Series: } \frac{1}{C_T} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{C_n} \qquad \text{two in series: } C_T = \frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1 + C_2} \qquad n \text{ equal in series: } C_T = \frac{C}{n} \]

    \(C_T\) is the equivalent capacitance and \(C_1 \dots C_n\) are the individual capacitances, all in farads (F). Because every value here is in μF and the rules only add, multiply and divide capacitances, you can work in μF throughout and the answer comes out in μF.

    Two checks to keep in mind: a series result is smaller than the smallest capacitor in the group; a parallel result is larger than the largest.

  4. \[ C_{34} = \frac{C_3 C_4}{C_3 + C_4} = \frac{(8.2\ \mu\text{F})(8.2\ \mu\text{F})}{8.2\ \mu\text{F} + 8.2\ \mu\text{F}} = \frac{67.24\ \mu\text{F}^2}{16.4\ \mu\text{F}} = 4.100\ \mu\text{F} \]

    Shortcut: two equal capacitors in series give half of one, \(8.2/2 = 4.1\ \mu\text{F}\). Check: 4.1 μF is smaller than 8.2 μF, as a series result must be.

  5. \[ C_{345} = C_{34} + C_5 = 4.1\ \mu\text{F} + 1.5\ \mu\text{F} = 5.600\ \mu\text{F} \]

    Check: 5.6 μF is larger than both 4.1 μF and 1.5 μF, as a parallel result must be. Notice that \(C_{345}\) comes out exactly equal to \(C_2\) and \(C_6\); the exam was designed that way.

  6. The three capacitors carry the same charge one after another, so use the series (reciprocal) rule:

    \[ \frac{1}{C_{S}} = \frac{1}{C_2} + \frac{1}{C_{345}} + \frac{1}{C_6} = \frac{1}{5.6\ \mu\text{F}} + \frac{1}{5.6\ \mu\text{F}} + \frac{1}{5.6\ \mu\text{F}} \]
    \[ \frac{1}{C_{S}} = 0.17857\ \mu\text{F}^{-1} \times 3 = 0.53571\ \mu\text{F}^{-1} \quad\Rightarrow\quad C_{S} = \frac{1}{0.53571\ \mu\text{F}^{-1}} = 1.867\ \mu\text{F} \]

    Shortcut: three equal capacitors in series give \(C/3 = 5.6/3 = 1.8667\ \mu\text{F}\). Check: 1.867 μF is smaller than 5.6 μF, as a series result must be. Keep 1.8667 (more digits) for the next step and round only at the end.

  7. \[ C_T = C_1 + C_S = 1.5\ \mu\text{F} + 1.8667\ \mu\text{F} = 3.3667\ \mu\text{F} \approx 3.367\ \mu\text{F} = 3.367 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{F} \]

    Exactly, \(C_T = 1.5 + 5.6/3 = 101/30\ \mu\text{F}\). Check: 3.367 μF is larger than both 1.5 μF and 1.867 μF, as a parallel result must be.

  8. The course describes three passive properties by what each one opposes:

    PropertyOpposes…
    Resistance \(R\)the flow of current
    Inductance \(L\)a change in current
    Capacitance \(C\)a change in voltage

    Why: a capacitor's voltage is set by the charge on its plates, \(V = Q/C\). Charge has to flow onto the plates to change that voltage, and that takes time, so the capacitor's voltage cannot jump instantly. It builds up (or decays) gradually. So the answer is: capacitance is a property that opposes a change in voltage.

    The word change matters. A capacitor does not oppose a steady voltage; once charged it simply holds that voltage.

a) \(C_T = 3.367\ \mu\text{F}\) (≈ 3.37 μF, that is \(3.367 \times 10^{-6}\) F). Reduction: \(C_3\) series \(C_4\) = 4.1 μF; parallel \(C_5\) → 5.6 μF; series with \(C_2\) and \(C_6\) → 1.867 μF; parallel \(C_1\) → 3.367 μF.

b) Capacitance is a property that opposes a change in voltage.

  • Bounds. \(C_1\) is directly across the source, so \(C_T\) must be more than \(C_1 = 1.5\ \mu\text{F}\). The series path can add at most its smallest member, 5.6 μF, so \(C_T < 1.5 + 5.6 = 7.1\ \mu\text{F}\). 3.367 μF lies between.
  • Charge check at 12 V (uses the source value the question gave). Total charge \(Q = C_T V_2 = 3.3667\ \mu\text{F} \times 12\ \text{V} = 40.40\ \mu\text{C}\). \(C_1\) has the full 12 V: \(Q_1 = 1.5 \times 12 = 18.00\ \mu\text{C}\). The series path stores \(Q_S = 1.8667 \times 12 = 22.40\ \mu\text{C}\). \(18.00 + 22.40 = 40.40\ \mu\text{C}\).
  • Voltage check (KVL). The three equal 5.6 μF capacitors in series split 12 V equally (\(V_{Cx} = V_s C_S/C_x = 12 \times 1.8667/5.6 = 4.000\ \text{V}\) each): \(4 + 4 + 4 = 12\ \text{V}\). Inside the block, \(C_5\) has 4 V and the equal \(C_3\), \(C_4\) have 2 V each. At node TR the charge balances: \(Q_2 = 5.6 \times 4 = 22.40\ \mu\text{C}\) equals \(Q_3 + Q_5 = 8.2 \times 2 + 1.5 \times 4 = 16.40 + 6.00 = 22.40\ \mu\text{C}\).
  • Independent check. Solving the network directly by charge conservation at its nodes (no series/parallel rules), and simulating the network charging from 12 V through a resistor, both give \(C_T = 3.3667\ \mu\text{F}\).
  • Using the resistor rules. Adding series capacitors and taking reciprocals for parallel ones gives 1.340 μF. For capacitors it is the other way round: parallel adds, series uses reciprocals.
  • Adding \(C_3\) and \(C_4\) because they look like a “column” next to \(C_5\). They are in series with each other (nothing connects at the point between them); only their combination is in parallel with \(C_5\).
  • Forgetting \(C_1\) (1.867 μF) or treating it as in series with the rest (0.832 μF). It is connected straight across the source, so it is in parallel with everything to its right.
  • Leaving \(C_6\) out of the series path. A capacitor in the bottom (return) wire is just as much in series as one in the top wire.
  • Using the 12 V. \(C_T\) does not depend on the source voltage. You need \(V_2\) only if you are asked for charges or capacitor voltages.
  • Rounding early. Carry 1.8667 μF into the last step. Rounding it to 2 μF gives 3.5 μF, about 4 % high; even 1.9 μF gives 3.4 μF, right at the edge of a 1 % tolerance.
  • Part b): “opposes a change in current”. That is inductance. Capacitance opposes a change in voltage. Writing just “opposes voltage” also misses the key word change.

Approach for a similar problem.

  • Find the terminals first. \(C_T\) is always “as seen from” a pair of terminals, here the source terminals. If the question moves the source (or asks for the capacitance between two other points), the series/parallel groupings change, so redo the grouping for the new terminals.
  • Work from the far end toward the terminals. Mark every junction. Two capacitors are in parallel when they connect the same two nodes; they are in series when they are joined at a node that nothing else touches. Replace one group at a time and redraw.
  • Use the capacitor rules (parallel adds, series reciprocal) and the shortcuts: two in series \(C_1C_2/(C_1+C_2)\), \(n\) equal in series \(C/n\).
  • Check every step with the bounds: series result below the smallest, parallel result above the largest.
  • If the same network were made of inductors or resistors, the grouping would be identical but the rules swap: series adds, parallel uses reciprocals.
  • If asked for voltages or charges, work string by string. For a pure series string, find that string’s own equivalent \(C_S\) and the voltage \(V_S\) across the whole string. Every capacitor in the string carries the same charge \(Q_S = C_S V_S\), so \(V_{Cx} = Q_S / C_x = V_S C_S / C_x\): the voltage splits in inverse proportion to capacitance. \(Q = C_T V\) gives only the total charge drawn from the source. When a capacitor sits directly across the source, as \(C_1\) does here, that total is split between the branches (here 18.0 μC on \(C_1\) and 22.4 μC on the series path), so do not use \(C_T\) in the divider formula. Here \(C_S = 1.867\ \mu\text{F}\) and \(V_S = 12\ \text{V}\) give \(V_{C2} = 12 \times 1.867/5.6 = 4\ \text{V}\); using \(C_T\) instead would give a wrong 7.2 V. Capacitors in parallel share the same voltage.
