\(R_N\) and \(I_N\) are two separate problems on two separate drawings. Start with \(R_N\): replace \(V_1\) with a wire and look for two resistors in series or in parallel. If you can't find any, what kind of network is it?

With the source shorted, \(R_4\), \(R_5\) and \(R_6\) form a triangle (Δ) between the left-middle node, the right-middle node (B) and the bottom rail. Convert that Δ to a Y and the rest becomes series and parallel.

For \(I_N\), draw a wire from A to B on the original circuit. Look at \(R_3\): both of its ends are now on the same node. What current does it carry? The rest is series–parallel, so no Δ–Y is needed.

\(I_N\) is not the source current. Find the total current, then use KCL at A: the short carries what arrives through \(R_1\) minus what leaves through \(R_2\).

  1. The question asks for the Norton equivalent seen from terminals A and B: a current source \(I_N\) in parallel with a shunt resistance \(R_N\). It behaves exactly like the original circuit for anything connected to A–B. You need two numbers, and each one is its own small problem:

    • \(R_N\): deactivate \(V_1\) (replace it with a wire) and find the resistance seen from A–B. This is the same as \(R_{TH}\).
    • \(I_N\): put a short (a wire) across A–B in the original circuit and find the current in that wire.

    The arrangement \(R_2\), \(R_3\), \(R_4\), \(R_5\), \(R_6\) is a bridge: two middle nodes joined by a cross resistor (\(R_4\)). That is the clue that a Δ–Y conversion will be needed for at least one of the two parts.

  2. For \(R_N\): with \(V_1\) replaced by a wire, \(R_1\) runs from A to the bottom rail. Now every node has three resistors on it, and no pair is in series (no node with only two resistors) or in parallel (no two resistors sharing both nodes). Series–parallel rules cannot start. The course tool for this is to replace a Δ with an equivalent Y. The bottom Δ \(R_4, R_5, R_6\) is the same position as the bottom π the instructor converts in the bridge example.

    For \(I_N\): the short joins A and B into one node. \(R_3\) has both ends on that node, so it has 0 V across it and carries no current: it drops out. Without \(R_3\) the bridge is gone, and the circuit reduces with series and parallel steps plus Ohm's law and KCL.

  3. Δ → Y. For a Δ of three resistors between three nodes, the equivalent Y has a new centre node N and one arm to each of the three nodes. Each arm is the product of the two Δ resistors that touch that node, divided by the sum of all three:

    \[ R_{y,\text{node}} = \frac{R_{\Delta,\text{touching 1}}\; R_{\Delta,\text{touching 2}}}{R_{\Delta 1} + R_{\Delta 2} + R_{\Delta 3}} \]

    This is the course formula \(R_1 = \dfrac{R_B R_C}{R_A+R_B+R_C}\) (and its two partners) written so that it doesn't depend on letter names. All resistances in Ω.

    Series and parallel: \(R_s = R_a + R_b\), \(\;R_a \parallel R_b = \dfrac{R_a R_b}{R_a + R_b}\) (Ω).

    Ohm's law: \(V = IR\) (V, A, Ω). KCL: the currents entering a node equal the currents leaving it.

    Norton: \(I_N = I_{A\to B}\big|_{\text{A–B shorted}}\) (A), \(R_N = R_{AB}\big|_{V_1 \to \text{wire}}\) (Ω), and the Norton source arrow points toward the terminal the short-circuit current comes out of.

    Node names used below: A = the top wire (terminal A), M = the left-middle node, B = the right-middle node (terminal B), G = the bottom rail. Values are shown to 4 significant figures; the calculator keeps full precision.

  4. Replace \(V_1\) with a wire. \(R_1\) now runs from A to G. The bottom three resistors form a Δ between M, B and G:

    • \(R_4 = 10\ \Omega\) between M and B,
    • \(R_5 = 25\ \Omega\) between M and G,
    • \(R_6 = 30\ \Omega\) between B and G.

    Sum of the Δ resistors (the common denominator):

    \[ R_4 + R_5 + R_6 = 10 + 25 + 30 = 65\ \Omega \]

    Each Y arm uses the two Δ resistors that touch its node:

    \[ R_{yM} = \frac{R_4 R_5}{65} = \frac{10 \times 25}{65} = \frac{250}{65} = 3.846\ \Omega \quad(\text{M touches } R_4, R_5) \]
    \[ R_{yB} = \frac{R_4 R_6}{65} = \frac{10 \times 30}{65} = \frac{300}{65} = 4.615\ \Omega \quad(\text{B touches } R_4, R_6) \]
    \[ R_{yG} = \frac{R_5 R_6}{65} = \frac{25 \times 30}{65} = \frac{750}{65} = 11.54\ \Omega \quad(\text{G touches } R_5, R_6) \]

    In the course's lettering (dw1), \(R_A = R_4\), \(R_B = R_6\), \(R_C = R_5\), and the arms are \(R_2 = 3.846\ \Omega\) (to M), \(R_3 = 4.615\ \Omega\) (to B) and \(R_1 = 11.54\ \Omega\) (to G). The letter names clash with this exam's \(R_1\)–\(R_6\), which is why this solution names the arms by their nodes.

  5. Remove the Δ and put the Y on the same three nodes M, B, G. Nothing outside the Δ changes.

    Trace a pretend current from A to B. At A it splits three ways:

    • A → \(R_2\) → M → \(R_{yM}\) → N: one series branch, \(R_2 + R_{yM} = 20 + 3.846 = 23.85\ \Omega\).
    • A → \(R_1\) → wire → G → \(R_{yG}\) → N: another series branch, \(R_1 + R_{yG} = 10 + 11.54 = 21.54\ \Omega\).
    • A → \(R_3\) → B directly.

    The first two branches both run from A to N, so they are in parallel. From N the current reaches B only through \(R_{yB}\), which is in series. \(R_3\) is in parallel with that whole A → N → B path.

    Parallel pair (A to N):

    \[ 23.85 \parallel 21.54 = \frac{23.85 \times 21.54}{23.85 + 21.54} = \frac{513.6}{45.38} = 11.32\ \Omega \]

    Add the series arm to B:

    \[ R_{A\to N\to B} = 11.32 + R_{yB} = 11.32 + 4.615 = 15.93\ \Omega \]

    In parallel with \(R_3\):

    \[ R_N = 15.93 \parallel 20 = \frac{15.93 \times 20}{15.93 + 20} = \frac{318.6}{35.93} = 8.868\ \Omega \]

  6. Go back to the original circuit with \(V_1\) in place and put a wire from A to B. The current in that wire, from A to B, is \(I_N\).

    What changes:

    • A and B are one node, call it A = B. \(R_3\) connects that node to itself: 0 V across it, so \(I_{R3} = 0\) and it drops out.
    • \(R_2\) (A to M) and \(R_4\) (B to M) now both run from the A = B node to M, so they are in parallel.
    • \(R_6\) runs from the A = B node to G.

    Reduce from the far side toward the source:

    \[ R_2 \parallel R_4 = \frac{20 \times 10}{20 + 10} = \frac{200}{30} = 6.667\ \Omega \]
    \[ (R_2 \parallel R_4) + R_5 = 6.667 + 25 = 31.67\ \Omega \]
    \[ 31.67 \parallel R_6 = \frac{31.67 \times 30}{31.67 + 30} = \frac{950.0}{61.67} = 15.41\ \Omega \]
    \[ R_T = R_1 + 15.41 = 10 + 15.41 = 25.41\ \Omega \]

    Total current out of the source (Ohm's law):

    \[ I_T = \frac{V_1}{R_T} = \frac{30\ \text{V}}{25.41\ \Omega} = 1.181\ \text{A} \]

  7. Voltage at the A = B node (G = 0 V). \(R_1\) drops \(V_{R1} = I_T R_1 = 1.181 \times 10 = 11.81\) V:

    \[ V_{A} = V_1 - V_{R1} = 30 - 11.81 = 18.19\ \text{V} \]

    This 18.19 V sits across both branches to G:

    \[ I_{R6} = \frac{18.19\ \text{V}}{30\ \Omega} = 0.6064\ \text{A}, \qquad I_{R5} = \frac{18.19\ \text{V}}{31.67\ \Omega} = 0.5745\ \text{A} \]

    The 0.5745 A going to M splits between \(R_2\) and \(R_4\). Their shared voltage is

    \[ V_{R2} = V_{R4} = I_{R5}\,(R_2 \parallel R_4) = 0.5745 \times 6.667 = 3.830\ \text{V} \]
    \[ I_{R2} = \frac{3.830}{20} = 0.1915\ \text{A}\ (\text{A} \to \text{M}), \qquad I_{R4} = \frac{3.830}{10} = 0.3830\ \text{A}\ (\text{B} \to \text{M}) \]

    (Check: \(0.1915 + 0.3830 = 0.5745\) A, and M sits at \(18.19 - 3.830 = 14.36\) V \(= 0.5745 \times 25\).)

    Now apply KCL to the two ends of the short. On the A end, \(I_T\) arrives through \(R_1\); \(I_{R2}\) leaves through \(R_2\); \(R_3\) carries nothing; the rest goes into the short:

    \[ I_N = I_T - I_{R2} = 1.181 - 0.1915 = 0.9894\ \text{A} \]

    On the B end, the short current leaves through \(R_6\) and \(R_4\):

    \[ I_N = I_{R6} + I_{R4} = 0.6064 + 0.3830 = 0.9894\ \text{A}\ ✓ \]

    The result is positive in the A → B direction: the short-circuit current flows from A to B.

  8. The Norton circuit is \(I_N = 0.9894\) A in parallel with \(R_N = 8.868\ \Omega\), connected to A and B. The arrow direction comes from the short-circuit test: current came out of A, through the short, into B. The Norton source must push current the same way through an external short, so its arrow points up, toward A. With nothing connected, all of \(I_N\) flows through \(R_N\), which makes A the + terminal: \(V_{AB} = I_N R_N = 0.9894 \times 8.868 = 8.774\) V.

\(R_N = 8.868\ \Omega \approx 8.87\ \Omega\) (exactly \(470/53\ \Omega\)).

\(I_N = 0.9894\ \text{A} \approx 0.989\ \text{A}\) (exactly \(93/94\) A), flowing from A to B through a short, so the source arrow points toward A.

Independent check by nodal analysis (open circuit). Leave A–B open and take G = 0 V. Unknowns: \(V_A\), \(V_M\), \(V_B\). KCL (currents leaving each node add to zero):

\[ \text{A: } \frac{V_A - 30}{10} + \frac{V_A - V_M}{20} + \frac{V_A - V_B}{20} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 4V_A - V_M - V_B = 60 \quad(\times 20) \]
\[ \text{M: } \frac{V_M - V_A}{20} + \frac{V_M - V_B}{10} + \frac{V_M}{25} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; -5V_A + 19V_M - 10V_B = 0 \quad(\times 100) \]
\[ \text{B: } \frac{V_B - V_A}{20} + \frac{V_B - V_M}{10} + \frac{V_B}{30} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; -3V_A - 6V_M + 11V_B = 0 \quad(\times 60) \]

Solving: \(V_A = 21.08\) V, \(V_M = 12.03\) V, \(V_B = 12.31\) V. Substituting back into node A: \(-0.8915 + 0.4528 + 0.4387 = 0\) ✓. So

\[ V_{OC} = V_A - V_B = 21.08 - 12.31 = 8.774\ \text{V}\ (\text{A positive}), \qquad \frac{V_{OC}}{R_N} = \frac{8.774}{8.868} = 0.9894\ \text{A} = I_N\ ✓ \]
  • Size: \(R_N = 8.868\ \Omega\) is smaller than \(R_3 = 20\ \Omega\), as it must be, since \(R_3\) sits directly across A–B in parallel with the rest.
  • Sign: A is fed from \(V_1\)+ through only \(R_1\), and B sits lower in the network, so A should be the higher terminal. It is (21.08 V vs 12.31 V), which agrees with the arrow pointing toward A.
  • Other Δ: converting the Δ \(R_1, R_2, R_5\) (between A, M and G with the source off) instead gives arms 3.636, 9.091 and 4.545 Ω and the same \(R_N = 8.868\ \Omega\).
  • Load test: a 10 Ω load on A–B draws \(0.9894 \times 8.868/(8.868 + 10) = 0.465\) A from the Norton model; nodal analysis of the original circuit with the same load also gives 0.465 A.

  • Opening \(V_1\) instead of shorting it (gives 11.75 Ω), or deleting \(R_1\) along with the source (gives 7.651 Ω). Only the source becomes a wire.
  • Stopping at 15.93 Ω and forgetting that \(R_3\) is directly across A–B.
  • Putting the Y arms on the wrong nodes. Each arm uses the two Δ resistors that touch its node, not the one opposite.
  • Using the total source current (1.181 A) as \(I_N\). Part of it (0.1915 A) leaves node A through \(R_2\), goes on through \(R_5\) to the bottom rail and never enters the short: \(I_N = I_T - I_{R2}\).

Approach for a similar problem. Keep the two parts separate and check the shape of each circuit before calculating.

  • Different numbers, same bridge: the method is unchanged. Kill the source, convert one Δ (any triangle of three resistors works; pick the one that leaves series–parallel groups behind), reduce, then short A–B for \(I_N\).
  • A resistor directly across A–B (like \(R_3\) here): it carries no current when A–B are shorted, so ignore it for \(I_N\). It still counts for \(R_N\), in parallel with everything else.
  • Terminals placed so the short removes the bridge (as here): \(I_N\) needs no Δ–Y. If the short does not remove the bridge, you may need a Δ–Y for \(I_N\) too. Convert on the shorted circuit and remember that the Y only gives correct voltages at its three outer nodes. Go back to the original circuit to get currents in the removed resistors.
  • Balanced bridge (additional note, not from the lessons): if the cross resistor happens to have the same voltage at both ends, it carries no current and can be removed. That does not happen here, so the Δ–Y is needed.
  • Asked for Thévenin instead: \(R_{TH} = R_N\) and \(V_{TH} = I_N R_N\), with + at the terminal the Norton arrow points to. Or find \(V_{TH}\) directly as the open-circuit voltage, as in the nodal check.
  • Not sure your \(I_N\) is right? Find \(V_{OC}\) a second way (nodal analysis, or a Δ–Y on the open circuit) and check \(I_N = V_{OC}/R_N\).
