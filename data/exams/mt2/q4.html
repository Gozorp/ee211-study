20 V would be right only if no current flowed in \(R_1\). Here A is not a dead end: whatever enters A through \(R_1\) leaves through \(R_3\) toward the middle node, so \(R_1\) does carry current and drops some voltage.

That is the voltage of the middle node (the top of \(R_4\)), not of terminal A. A is one resistor further along: add the drop across \(R_3\), \(V_A = V_M + I\,R_3\).

You used a divider of \(R_2\) and \(R_4\) only (\(20 \times 15/25\)). That ignores the second path from the source to the middle node, through \(R_1\), A and \(R_3\). That 25 Ω path is in parallel with \(R_2\).

It looks like you put \(R_1\) in parallel with \(R_2\) because both start at the source's + node. Their other ends are different nodes (A and the middle node), so they are not in parallel. \(R_1\) is in series with \(R_3\) instead.

That is the drop across \(R_1\). \(V_{TH}\) is the voltage of A itself (relative to B): the source voltage minus that drop.

11 Ω is \(R_3 + R_2 \parallel R_4\), the path through the middle node. You left out \(R_1\): after shorting the source, \(R_1\) connects A straight to B, in parallel with that 11 Ω path.

You treated \(R_2\) and \(R_4\) as one series path (\(20 \parallel 30\)). After the source is shorted, the left end of \(R_2\) is connected to B, so \(R_2\) and \(R_4\) both run from the middle node to B: they are in parallel.

You combined \(R_1 \parallel R_3\) and \(R_2 \parallel R_4\) and added them. \(R_1\) and \(R_3\) do not share both nodes (\(R_3\) ends at the middle node, \(R_1\) ends at B once the source is shorted). Redraw with the source replaced by a wire and follow a test current from A.

You seem to have dropped \(R_4\). It still runs from the middle node to B and is in parallel with \(R_2\) (\(10 \parallel 15 = 6\ \Omega\)).

That comes from removing the voltage source (an open) instead of replacing it with a wire. To kill an independent voltage source, set its voltage to 0 V: a short.

\(R_1 \parallel R_3 = 4\ \Omega\) ignores \(R_2\) and \(R_4\), and \(R_1\), \(R_3\) are not in parallel: \(R_3\) ends at the middle node, not at B.

This is two separate problems. For \(V_{TH}\), leave A–B open and keep the source. For \(R_{TH}\), redraw with the source replaced by a wire. Start with \(V_{TH}\): current enters A through \(R_1\). Where can it go, given that nothing is connected to A from outside?

KCL at A: the current in \(R_1\) must continue through \(R_3\) to the middle node. So \(R_1\) and \(R_3\) form one 25 Ω path from the source's + node to the middle node, side by side with \(R_2\).

Combine that path with \(R_2\) (parallel), then the result is in series with \(R_4\). A voltage divider gives the middle-node voltage. Then walk along the 25 Ω path to find the voltage at A.

For \(R_{TH}\): once \(V_1\) is a wire, the source's + node and the bottom wire are the same node, which is B. Where does that put \(R_1\)? And \(R_2\)?

  1. "Derive the Thévenin equivalent" means: replace everything as seen from terminals A and B by one source \(V_{TH}\) in series with one resistor \(R_{TH}\). You need two numbers, each with its own procedure:

    • \(V_{TH}\) = the open-circuit voltage \(V_{AB}\), with nothing connected between A and B.
    • \(R_{TH}\) = the resistance seen from A–B with the voltage source replaced by a short.

    For the solution, call the source's + node N and the node where \(R_2\), \(R_3\) and \(R_4\) meet M. Take B (the bottom wire, the source's − side) as the 0 V reference, so \(V_{TH} = V_A - V_B = V_A\).

  2. At first glance this circuit does not look like a simple series–parallel network, because \(R_1\) jumps from N over to A. The key is KCL at A with A open:

    \[ I_{R_1}\ (\text{into A}) = I_{R_3}\ (\text{out of A, toward M}) \quad\text{because nothing else is connected to A.} \]

    So \(R_1\) and \(R_3\) carry the same current: they are in series, one path from N to M that passes through A. That path and \(R_2\) both run from N to M, so they are in parallel. The parallel group then sits in series with \(R_4\) (M to B) across the 20 V source. Now the circuit is an ordinary voltage divider.

    Unlike Question 8, A is not a dead end here: \(R_1\) and \(R_3\) close a loop with \(R_2\), so current does flow through \(R_1\) and \(R_3\), and they do drop voltage.

    • Series: \(R_s = R_a + R_b\). Parallel: \(R_a \parallel R_b = \dfrac{R_a R_b}{R_a + R_b}\). All resistances in ohms (Ω).
    • Ohm's law: \(V = IR\) (V in volts, I in amperes, R in ohms). Total current: \(I_T = V_1 / R_T\).
    • Voltage divider (series resistors carrying the same current): \(V_x = V_1 \dfrac{R_x}{R_T}\).
    • Thévenin definitions: \(V_{TH} = V_A - V_B\big|_{\text{A–B open}}\), \(\;R_{TH} = R_{AB}\big|_{V_1\ \text{shorted}}\).

  4. Series path through A:

    \[ R_1 + R_3 = 20\ \Omega + 5\ \Omega = 25\ \Omega \]

    In parallel with \(R_2\):

    \[ (R_1 + R_3) \parallel R_2 = \frac{25 \times 10}{25 + 10} = \frac{250}{35} = 7.143\ \Omega \]

    In series with \(R_4\), this is the total resistance the source sees:

    \[ R_T = 7.143\ \Omega + 15\ \Omega = 22.14\ \Omega \]

    Total current out of the source's + terminal (it returns through \(R_4\) and the bottom wire):

    \[ I_T = \frac{V_1}{R_T} = \frac{20\ \text{V}}{22.14\ \Omega} = 0.9032\ \text{A} \]

    (Exact fractions: \(R_T = 155/7\ \Omega\), \(I_T = 28/31\ \text{A}\). Values are rounded to 4 significant figures only for display; the calculator keeps full precision.)

  5. Middle node M (voltage divider, or \(I_T R_4\)):

    \[ V_M = V_1 \frac{R_4}{R_T} = 20 \times \frac{15}{22.14} = 13.55\ \text{V} \qquad (\text{check: } 0.9032\ \text{A} \times 15\ \Omega = 13.55\ \text{V}) \]

    The parallel group (N to M) has the rest of the source voltage:

    \[ V_{NM} = 20 - 13.55 = 6.452\ \text{V} \]

    Current in the path through A (Ohm's law on the 25 Ω path), and in \(R_2\):

    \[ I_{R_1} = I_{R_3} = \frac{6.452\ \text{V}}{25\ \Omega} = 0.2581\ \text{A}, \qquad I_{R_2} = \frac{6.452\ \text{V}}{10\ \Omega} = 0.6452\ \text{A} \]

    KCL at M: \(0.2581 + 0.6452 = 0.9032\ \text{A} = I_T\) ✓.

    Voltage at A. Start at N (20 V) and walk through \(R_1\) in the direction of its current (N → A), so the voltage drops by \(I_{R_1} R_1\):

    \[ V_{R_1} = 0.2581\ \text{A} \times 20\ \Omega = 5.161\ \text{V}, \qquad V_A = 20 - 5.161 = 14.84\ \text{V} \]

    Same result from the other side: walk from M up to A against the current in \(R_3\) (which flows A → M), so the voltage rises by \(I_{R_3}R_3 = 0.2581 \times 5 = 1.290\ \text{V}\): \(V_A = 13.55 + 1.290 = 14.84\ \text{V}\) ✓.

    Result and polarity. B is the bottom wire, 0 V. So

    \[ V_{TH} = V_A - V_B = 14.84\ \text{V} - 0\ \text{V} = 14.84\ \text{V} \quad (\text{exactly } 460/31\ \text{V}), \]

    positive, so A is the + terminal of the equivalent.

  6. Start again from the original circuit (do not reuse the voltages and currents from \(V_{TH}\)). Replace \(V_1\) by a wire. That wire joins node N to the bottom wire, so N and B become one node.

    Now see where each resistor lands, following a test current that enters at A and leaves at B:

    • \(R_1\) runs from A to N, and N is now B. So \(R_1\) is connected directly across A–B.
    • \(R_2\) runs from M to N = B, and \(R_4\) runs from M to B. Same two nodes: \(R_2 \parallel R_4\).
    • \(R_3\) runs from A to M, so it is in series with \(R_2 \parallel R_4\). That series path also goes from A to B, in parallel with \(R_1\).

    Calculate from the far end toward the terminals:

    \[ R_2 \parallel R_4 = \frac{10 \times 15}{10 + 15} = \frac{150}{25} = 6\ \Omega \]
    \[ R_3 + R_2 \parallel R_4 = 5 + 6 = 11\ \Omega \]
    \[ R_{TH} = R_1 \parallel 11\ \Omega = \frac{20 \times 11}{20 + 11} = \frac{220}{31} = 7.097\ \Omega \]

    Shorting the source did not remove any resistor here: the short is not connected across any single resistor. It only moved the left ends of \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) onto B.

  7. Put \(V_{TH} = 14.84\ \text{V}\) in series with \(R_{TH} = 7.097\ \Omega\) between A and B. Because \(V_{TH} = V_A - V_B\) came out positive, the source's + side faces A. The drawing is in the answer below.

\(V_{TH} = 14.84\ \text{V}\) (A positive with respect to B; exactly \(460/31\ \text{V}\)).

\(R_{TH} = R_1 \parallel (R_3 + R_2 \parallel R_4) = 20 \parallel 11 = 7.097\ \Omega\) (exactly \(220/31\ \Omega\)).

  • Range check. A lies on the path from N (20 V) to M (13.55 V), so \(V_A\) must be between 13.55 V and 20 V. 14.84 V is. It is closer to M because \(R_3\) (5 Ω) is small compared with \(R_1\) (20 Ω).
  • Parallel check. A parallel combination is smaller than its smallest branch: \(7.097\ \Omega \lt 11\ \Omega \lt 20\ \Omega\) ✓.
  • Short-circuit check (\(R_{TH} = V_{OC}/I_{SC}\)). Put a wire from A to B. Then \(R_1\) has the full 20 V across it: \(20/20 = 1\ \text{A}\). At M, \(R_2\) (10 Ω) feeds \(R_3 \parallel R_4 = 5 \parallel 15 = 3.75\ \Omega\), so \(V_M = 20 \times 3.75/13.75 = 5.455\ \text{V}\) and \(I_{R_3} = 5.455/5 = 1.091\ \text{A}\). \(I_{SC} = 1 + 1.091 = 2.091\ \text{A}\) (from A to B through the wire). \(V_{OC}/I_{SC} = 14.84/2.091 = 7.097\ \Omega\) ✓.
  • Load check. Connect \(R_L = 15\ \Omega\) across A–B. The equivalent gives \(14.84 \times 15/(7.097 + 15) = 10.07\ \text{V}\). Nodal analysis of the full circuit with the same load also gives 10.07 V.
  • Assuming no current flows in \(R_1\) "because A is open" and answering 20 V. Current stops only at a dead end. Here A connects onward through \(R_3\), so \(R_1\) and \(R_3\) carry a loop current.
  • Calling \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) parallel because they start at the same node. Parallel resistors must share both nodes. Check both ends.
  • Reporting \(V_M\) (13.55 V) instead of \(V_A\). Mark A and B before you start and read the voltage at exactly those points.
  • Finding \(R_{TH}\) on the same drawing as \(V_{TH}\), or opening the voltage source instead of shorting it (gives 19.29 Ω).
  • After shorting the source, forgetting that N and B are now the same node, so \(R_1\) is left out (11 Ω) or \(R_2\) is put in series with \(R_4\) (12 Ω).
  • Drawing the equivalent with the + side toward B, or with \(R_{TH}\) in parallel with the source. Thévenin is always source in series with \(R_{TH}\), + toward the higher terminal.

Approach for a similar problem.

  • Different values, same layout. The formulas carry over: \(V_M = V_1 \dfrac{R_4}{(R_1+R_3)\parallel R_2 + R_4}\), \(V_A = V_M + (V_1 - V_M)\dfrac{R_3}{R_1 + R_3}\), and \(R_{TH} = R_1 \parallel (R_3 + R_2 \parallel R_4)\).
  • A terminal in the middle of a path. Use KCL at the open terminal: if exactly two elements meet there, they carry the same current and are in series. Group, reduce, find node voltages, then \(V_{TH} = V_A - V_B\).
  • A terminal at a dead end (only one element touches it, as in Questions 8 and 9): that element carries no current and drops 0 V.
  • No series or parallel pair at all (a true bridge): use nodal analysis for \(V_{TH}\), or a Δ–Y conversion to create series–parallel structure.
  • For \(R_{TH}\): after shorting the sources, give every node a letter and relabel nodes the short merges (here N became B). Then list each resistor by its two node letters; resistors with the same pair are in parallel. A test current from A to B shows what is in series.
