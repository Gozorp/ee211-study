3. Terminal voltage. The current \(0.25 \times 10 = 2.5\,\text{A}\) flows a→x through \(R_3\), so a is higher than x: \(V_a = v_x + (2.5)(2) = 10 + 5 = 15\,\text{V}\). So \(V_{TH} = 15\,\text{V}\) (a +).

Part 2: \(R_{TH}\) by the test-source method

4. Set up. Short the independent 10 V source and keep the dependent source. Connect \(V_{test} = 1\,\text{V}\) across a–b (+ at a). It supplies \(I_{test}\) into node a.

5. KCL at x. \(R_1\) now goes from x to 0 V through the short, beside \(R_2\). The current arriving from a through \(R_3\) leaves through them: \(\dfrac{1 - v_x}{2} = \dfrac{v_x}{2} + \dfrac{v_x}{4}\). That gives \(0.5 = 1.25\,v_x\), so \(v_x = 0.4\,\text{V}\).

6. KCL at a. In: \(I_{test}\) and the dependent \(0.25\,v_x = 0.1\,\text{A}\). Out: through \(R_3\), \((1 - 0.4)/2 = 0.3\,\text{A}\). So \(I_{test} = 0.3 - 0.1 = 0.2\,\text{A}\).

7. \(R_{TH} = \dfrac{V_{test}}{I_{test}} = \dfrac{1\,\text{V}}{0.2\,\text{A}} = 5\,\Omega\).
Cross-check with \(V_{OC}/I_{SC}\): short a–b (all sources on). KCL at x: \(\frac{10 - v_x}{2} = \frac{v_x}{4} + \frac{v_x}{2}\), so \(v_x = 4\,\text{V}\). The short carries \(R_3\)'s current \(4/2 = 2\,\text{A}\) plus the dependent \(0.25 \times 4 = 1\,\text{A}\): \(I_{SC} = 3\,\text{A}\). Then \(R_{TH} = 15/3 = 5\,\Omega\) ✓.
The trap: zeroing the dependent source would give \(R_3 + R_1 \parallel R_2 = 2 + 1.33 = 3.33\,\Omega\), which is wrong.

Part 3: the load

8. \(I_L = \dfrac{V_{TH}}{R_{TH} + R_L} = \dfrac{15}{5 + 20} = 0.6\,\text{A}\) (and \(V_L = 0.6 \times 20 = 12\,\text{V}\)).

\(V_{TH} = 15\,\text{V}\) (a +), \(R_{TH} = 5\,\Omega\), and \(I_L = 0.6\,\text{A}\) with the 20 Ω load.

Two independent routes agree. The test source and \(V_{OC}/I_{SC}\) both give 5 Ω. Solving the full original circuit with the 20 Ω load attached (nodal analysis) gives \(v_x = 8.8\,\text{V}\), \(V_a = 12\,\text{V}\) and \(I_L = 0.6\,\text{A}\), the same as the equivalent.

\(V_{TH}\) is larger than the only independent source (15 V vs 10 V). A network of resistors and one independent source could never do that: every node voltage would stay between 0 and 10 V. The dependent source is an active element. With a–b open it delivers 2.5 A at 15 V, i.e. 37.5 W. For the same reason, \(R_{TH}\) can differ from the "sources-zeroed" value in either direction, and with other gains it can even come out negative.

Units. The gain 0.25 A/V is a conductance (0.25 S), so \(0.25\,v_x\) really is in amperes.

Part A: Thévenin equivalent of the source network (remove \(R_L\))

1. \(R_{TH}\). Replace \(V_s\) by a wire (0 V). Looking in from a, a test current can go down \(R_2\) to b, or left through \(R_1\) and down the wire to b. 2. \(V_{TH}\). With a–b open the only loop is \(V_s\)–\(R_1\)–\(R_2\): \(I = \dfrac{60\,\text{V}}{20 + 30\,\Omega} = 1.2\,\text{A}\), so \(V_{TH} = V_{ab} = 1.2\,\text{A} \times 30\,\Omega = 36\,\text{V}\) (divider: \(60 \cdot \frac{30}{50} = 36\,\text{V}\)), with a positive.

Part B: the load

3. Part 1. Maximum power transfer: \(R_L = R_{TH} = 12\,\Omega\). (Not 20 Ω: \(R_1\) is only part of the source network.)

4. Part 2. \(P_{max} = \dfrac{V_{TH}^2}{4R_{TH}} = \dfrac{36^2}{4 \times 12} = \dfrac{1296}{48} = 27\,\text{W}\).

Part C: efficiency in the original circuit (part 4)

6. Back to the real network. Equivalence guarantees the same terminal values, so with \(R_L = 12\,\Omega\) the original circuit also has \(V_{ab} = 18\,\text{V}\) and \(I_L = 1.5\,\text{A}\). 6 Ω and 24 Ω give the same power because \(6 \times 24 = 144 = R_{TH}^2\) (the pair rule noted under the guide's power plot). Halving the matched load costs 11 %, exactly like the lecture's 5 Ω case (8.89 W instead of 10 W), and doubling it costs the same.

The bleeder can only cost power. Without \(R_2\), the best possible load would get \(V_s^2/(4R_1) = 45\,\text{W}\). With it, \(P_{max} = \dfrac{V_s^2}{4R_1} \cdot \dfrac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} = 45 \times 0.6 = 27\,\text{W}\), which is smaller, as it should be.

Why not 50 %? The equivalent predicts 27 W lost in \(R_{TH}\), but the real network loses 99 W (88.2 W in \(R_1\) and 10.8 W in \(R_2\)). A Thévenin equivalent is equivalent only at the terminals. It gives the right load voltage, current and power, but says nothing about power inside the original circuit. Even with nothing connected, the supply delivers \(60^2/50 = 72\,\text{W}\) into \(R_1\) and \(R_2\).
