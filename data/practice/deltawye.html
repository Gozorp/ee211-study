With all arms \(R_Y = 2.2\,\text{k}\Omega\), the numerator is \(3R_Y^2 = 14.52\ \text{k}\Omega^2\) and every divisor is \(R_Y\): \[ R_\Delta = \frac{3R_Y^2}{R_Y} = 3R_Y = 6.6\,\text{k}\Omega \] Because all three divisors are equal, dividing by the "wrong" arm gives the same answer. That is why the habit went unnoticed last week.

(a) \(R_A = 14\,\text{k}\Omega\). (b) \(R_C = 3.5\,\text{k}\Omega\), and \(R_B = 7\,\text{k}\Omega\). (c) Option d: the student divided by an adjacent arm instead of the opposite one. (d) \(R_\Delta = 6.6\,\text{k}\Omega\) each.

All three meter pairs on the corrected Δ (third terminal open) match the Y:

  • A–B: \(14\parallel 10.5 = 6.0\,\text{k}\Omega = R_2+R_3\) ✓
  • A–C: \(3.5\parallel 21 = 3.0\,\text{k}\Omega = R_2+R_1\) ✓
  • B–C: \(7\parallel 17.5 = 5.0\,\text{k}\Omega = R_3+R_1\) ✓

Round trip. Convert the corrected Δ back with Δ→Y. The sum is 24.5 kΩ. \(R_1 = (7)(3.5)/24.5 = 1\,\text{k}\Omega\), \(R_2 = (3.5)(14)/24.5 = 2\,\text{k}\Omega\) and \(R_3 = (14)(7)/24.5 = 4\,\text{k}\Omega\): the original Y ✓.

Quick size test. Each Δ resistor must exceed the series sum of the two Y arms it spans. The student's \(R_B = 3.5\,\text{k}\Omega\) fails this, since \(R_3+R_1 = 5\,\text{k}\Omega\), so the slip could have been caught on paper.
